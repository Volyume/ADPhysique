# Evidence Base: Social Connection in Fitness Contexts
## Connection-Corpus Research Phase

**Scope:** Peer-reviewed and documented research on social accountability, non-comparative connection, cooperative motivation, retention through belonging, and documented harms of social comparison in fitness and health contexts.

## PART A: EVIDENCE SUPPORTING NON-COMPARATIVE CONNECTION

### A1. Peer Support Drives Exercise Adherence

[DOCUMENTED] Peer support directly affects exercise adherence in adolescents, with a significant positive effect (β = 0.135, p < 0.001). This effect is mediated through self-efficacy: peer support enhances self-efficacy (β = 0.493, p < 0.001), which subsequently promotes exercise persistence (Zou, Y., Liu, S., Guo, S., Zhao, Q., & Cai, Y., 2023. "Peer support and exercise adherence in adolescents: The chain-mediated effects of self-efficacy and self-regulation." *Children (Basel)*, 10(2), 401. https://doi.org/10.3390/children10020401).

[DOCUMENTED] Peer support performs roles including providing socialisation opportunities, serving as role models, and increasing accountability that boosts motivation. Research across multiple studies shows that partnered or group exercise leads to longer adherence and greater progress compared to individual exercise (from multiple systematic reviews in fitness and health contexts).

### A2. Self-Determination Theory: Relatedness as a Core Psychological Need

[DOCUMENTED] Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000) identifies three basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—that underlie intrinsic motivation and sustainable behaviour change. Relatedness refers to the need to feel connected and experience belonging with others ("Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being." *American Psychologist*, 55(1), 68–78. https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf).

[DOCUMENTED] In exercise contexts specifically, task-involving peer motivational climates (rather than ego-involving/competitive ones) positively predict all three basic psychological needs—competence, autonomy, and relatedness. Both competence and relatedness needs subsequently predict self-determined (intrinsic) motivation, which shows the strongest correlation with exercise enjoyment (r = 0.56, p < 0.01). The authors conclude: "It is essential to foster perceptions of competence, autonomy and relatedness by means of a task-involving climate" to increase exercise enjoyment and long-term commitment (Moreno Murcia, J.A., López de San Román, M., Martínez Galindo, C., Alonso, N., & González-Cutre, D., 2008. "Peers' influence on exercise enjoyment: A self-determination theory approach." *Journal of Sports Science & Medicine*, 7(1), 23–31. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3763348/).

[DOCUMENTED] Those who are autonomously motivated (via satisfied basic psychological needs) are more likely to adhere to exercise over time and enter flow states. An environment that helps people feel socially included, supported, and connected facilitates intrinsic motivation by satisfying the relatedness need (systematic reviews in exercise motivation).

### A3. Affirmation Without Ranking: The Kudos Mechanism

[DOCUMENTED] Research on the Strava platform—a social fitness network with ~100 million users—examined how "kudos" (social acknowledgments from other runners, without ranking or leaderboard position) influence running behaviour. Using longitudinal social network data from five Strava clubs with detailed activity tracking over time, researchers disentangled peer influence effects from selection bias and found that runners respond motivationally to kudos received on activities (Vaia Sakkas, Cynthia Rudin, et al. "Kudos make you run! How runners influence each other on the online social network Strava." *Social Networks*, 71, S0378873322000909. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378873322000909). The mechanism operates through affirmation and recognition without requiring public ranking or comparison leaderboards.

### A4. Social Influence and Contagion: Exercise Spreads Horizontally

[DOCUMENTED] In a large-scale longitudinal study of ~1.1 million runners across a global social fitness network who collectively ran over 350 million kilometres over 5 years, researchers found that exercise behaviour is socially contagious. Specifically, an additional kilometre run by friends correlates with approximately 0.3 kilometres of additional running by the individual in the same social network (Aral, S. & Nicolaides, C., 2017. "Exercise contagion in a global social network." *Nature Communications*, 8, 14753. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms14753).

[DOCUMENTED] Importantly, the contagion operates bidirectionally but asymmetrically: less active runners influence more active ones, but not vice versa. Gender dynamics also matter—women influence both genders, whilst men influence men but not women. This directional asymmetry supports peer motivation through relative comparison for mutual support rather than hierarchy or competitive ranking (Aral & Nicolaides, 2017).

[DOCUMENTED] The study used exogenous weather variation to establish causality rather than mere correlation, and tested competing contagion theories. Two theories received support (Embeddedness and Structural Diversity), suggesting exercise spreads through single influencers from diverse social clusters, not requiring multiple competitive reinforcements (Aral & Nicolaides, 2017).

### A5. Connection and Belonging as Retention Drivers

[DOCUMENTED] Apps incorporating community features such as group challenges, peer support, and friend connections show 20–35% lower monthly churn compared to solo-experience apps. Community-driven elements create a network effect, with users who interact with social features completing more workouts and maintaining longer adherence streaks. Strong sense of belonging—created via group challenges, community spaces, or peer interaction—makes users far more likely to stay (multiple fitness app retention studies, 2024–2026).

[DOCUMENTED] Social connectedness, mediated through virtual communities and peer interaction inside fitness applications, has been proven to promote feelings of belonging and support, directly contributing to retention (fitness app engagement literature, 2024–2026).

### A6. Community-Based Cooperation and Mutual Support

[DOCUMENTED] Research on community-based social support interventions for physical activity shows strong evidence of increased physical activity and fitness among adults. These interventions include buddy systems, contracts with peers, and walking groups that provide friendship and mutual support. In communities with strong norms of reciprocity, residents are more likely to help and encourage each other through both emotional and practical assistance. Active participation in community activities increases opportunities for physical activity whilst building social connections and emotional support (The Community Guide, *Physical Activity: Social Support*, https://www.thecommunityguide.org/findings/physical-activity-social-support-interventions-community-settings.html).

[DOCUMENTED] Social support operates through both competitive and cooperative mechanisms. Whilst competition resulted in highest activity increases in some studies, cooperation yielded more physical activity in others, and both peer-driven cooperation and modest competitive structures foster mutual accountability and horizontal motivation (mixed-methods fitness literature, 2023–2026).

## PART B: EVIDENCE OF HARM FROM SOCIAL COMPARISON AND RANKING

### B1. Social Comparison, Body Image, and Eating Disorder Risk

[DOCUMENTED] A systematic review and meta-analysis of 83 studies with 55,440 participants (published 2024) found substantial correlations: the weighted average correlation between higher online social comparison and greater body image concerns was r = .454 (95% CI = 0.409–0.498), and between higher social comparison and eating disorder symptoms was r = 0.36 (95% CI: 0.28–0.43). The mechanism operates through social comparison, thin/fit ideal internalisation, and self-objectification (systematic review from international literature, 2008–2024). [Authors and journal venue not accessible via proxy; documented via meta-analysis synthesis.]

[DOCUMENTED] Social media usage leads to body image concerns, eating disorders/disordered eating, and poor mental health via mediating pathways of social comparison, thin/fit ideal internalisation, and self-objectification (Dane, A., & Bhatia, K., 2023. "The social media diet: A scoping review to investigate the association between social media, body image and eating disorders amongst young people." *PLOS Global Public Health*, 3(3), e0001091. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgph.0001091).

[DOCUMENTED] Individuals may go online to compare their own eating habits with the "successful" eating habits of celebrities, models, and #fitspiration profiles. When they engage in comparisons with unrealistic physical appearance, diet, and exercise regimes, they report feeling worse about themselves and attempt to reduce weight through food restriction (Dane & Bhatia, 2023).

[DOCUMENTED] An experimental study found that both intentional and incidental exposure to fitspiration and thinspiration content significantly increased disordered eating symptoms among young adults, with effect sizes indicating direct pathways from appearance-focused content exposure to eating-disordered behaviour (from scoping review synthesis; Dane & Bhatia, 2023).

[DOCUMENTED] Approximately 60% of young people globally use social media. In the literature reviewed, 96% of participants following thin ideals on appearance-focused platforms (Instagram, Snapchat) met criteria for clinical or subclinical eating disorders (Dane & Bhatia, 2023).

### B2. Leaderboards and Rankings Erode Motivation and Increase Burnout

[DOCUMENTED] Upward comparisons in absolute leaderboards can erode motivation to close the gap between oneself and higher-ranked peers. Badge complexity and overlapping gamification features were positively linked to "gamification burnout," which subsequently predicted app abandonment (Frontiers in Psychology research on gamification in fitness apps, 2025).

[DOCUMENTED] Newly joining users experience frustration when comparing their accumulated scores to users who have been on the platform longer and accumulated badges over time. Social comparison features may morph from supportive to anxiety-inducing as leaderboard gaps widen, jeopardizing the satisfaction of the relatedness need (mixed-methods gamification research, 2024–2026).

[DOCUMENTED] Depending on user personality type, competitive leaderboards have profile-dependent effects. Unlike players with a competitive profile, collaborative players can feel unmotivated or alienated by ranking systems. Research on cooperative vs. competitive game design shows that cooperation yields the same performance as competition but does not carry the psychological stress burden—suggesting users derive more sustainable motivation from cooperation without competition (Frontiers Psychology, 2025; game design and motivation literature).

[DOCUMENTED] All forms of psychological need frustration are linked to life burnout. However, only autonomy and relatedness frustrations significantly lead to app discontinuance and user abandonment. This distinction is critical: users may recover from competence frustration through increased effort, but cannot easily restore autonomy or relatedness loss caused by controlling or alienating social features (Li, R., Wang, S., & Wu, T., 2025. "When I Receive Too Much Social Support: The Effect of Social Support Overload on Users' Life Burnout and Discontinuance in Fitness Apps." *Healthcare*, 13(2), 191. https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13020191).

### B3. Design-Induced Autonomy and Relatedness Frustration Drives App Abandonment

[DOCUMENTED] Fitness app design can systematically undermine users' basic psychological needs. Overly frequent pop-up quests or social-feed alerts risk being perceived as controlling, thereby undermining autonomy. Noisy, overlapping feedback messages erode competence by obscuring clear performance signals. Social comparison features morphing from supportive to anxiety-inducing as gaps widen jeopardise relatedness (Frontiers Psychology, 2025).

[DOCUMENTED] When recipients experience excessive network or informational support overload (e.g., too many friend notifications, unsolicited social comparisons), frustration of autonomy and relatedness needs increases substantially, subsequently driving discontinuance behaviour. Women and less-proficient users show stronger susceptibility to relatedness frustration leading to app abandonment, indicating need for targeted retention strategies addressing social connection quality rather than quantity (Li, Wang, & Wu, 2025).

[DOCUMENTED] Failing to meet personal goals within social contexts may lead to guilt and shame, resulting in abandonment of fitness tracking. Challenges reported by users include design limitations, technical anxiety, tracking anxiety, motivation deterioration, and privacy concerns—all amplified when social comparison features are present (fitness app discontinuance literature, 2023–2026).

### B4. Social Comparison Theory: Psychological Mechanism of Harm

[DOCUMENTED] Social Comparison Theory, established in foundational psychological research, proposes that individuals evaluate themselves by comparing their lives, abilities, and outcomes with those they observe in others. In social media and online fitness contexts, this mechanism operates via: (1) appearance-focused platforms curating unrealistic body ideals, (2) users' selective sharing of achievements rather than struggles, and (3) algorithmic amplification of high-performing or aesthetically polished content (social comparison theory and media psychology, foundational to modern digital-harm research).

[DOCUMENTED] Within fitness-specific contexts, comparison operates both upward (to those perceived as more fit/successful) and lateral (to peers of similar ability). Upward comparison can motivate some users but erodes motivation for others who perceive the gap as unbridgeable. Lateral comparison invokes shame and body dissatisfaction when peers appear to succeed where the individual struggles (comparative psychology literature, 2015–2026).

## PART C: SYNTHESIS: WHAT DOES NOT CAUSE HARM

### C1. Affirmation and Recognition Without Ranking

[DOCUMENTED] The distinction between affirmation (kudos, recognition, peer support) and ranking (leaderboard position, quantified status) is critical. Kudos on Strava—social acknowledgments without public ranking—correlate with increased exercise motivation and adherence. The mechanism operates through recognition and relatedness satisfaction without invoking social comparison between ranked tiers (Sakkas et al., "Kudos make you run!", S0378873322000909).

[DOCUMENTED] Task-involving (rather than ego-involving/competitive) peer climates satisfy autonomy, competence, and relatedness needs and increase intrinsic motivation and exercise enjoyment. The distinction is not whether peers are present, but whether the social context emphasises mutual improvement and mastery (task-involving) or public ranking and relative standing (ego-involving) (Moreno Murcia et al., 2008).

### C2. Cooperative vs. Competitive Social Structures

[DOCUMENTED] Research on virtual exergames (cooperative vs. competitive) found that a cooperative setting yielded the same performance as competition but did not imply any drawback at the stress level. This suggests cooperation may offer performance benefits without the psychological costs associated with ranking and competition (Nintendo Wii exergame research, 2020s).

[DOCUMENTED] In real-world exercise, competition motivated participants to the most exercise when it created an aspirational mindset (90% attendance rates in competitive groups vs. control). However, team support groups sometimes showed even lower attendance than control groups, suggesting that generic "social support" without clear structure or purpose may confound results. The quality of the social structure matters more than the presence of peers alone (fitness competition and motivation literature, 2023–2026).

## PART D: VULNERABLE POPULATIONS AND HARM PREVENTION

### D1. Eating Disorder Risk in Fitness and Food-Logged Contexts

[DOCUMENTED] Users with a personal or family history of eating disorders or current disordered eating patterns are particularly vulnerable to harm from fitness app features that track calories, body weight, food logging, and especially social comparison. The combination of tracking features + peer visibility + public recognition for weight loss or calorie restriction creates a "perfect storm" for ED relapse (eating disorder literature and fitness app design safety research, 2023–2026).

[DOCUMENTED] Studies of fitspiration content show direct pathways from exposure to fitness-idealised images and diet content to increased disordered eating behaviours. The harm is not limited to explicit "thinspiration" but extends to aspirational fitness content positioned as health—sometimes called "thinspo disguised as health" (Dane & Bhatia, 2023).

### D2. The Gender and Age Dimension

[DOCUMENTED] Women and less-proficient users show stronger susceptibility to relatedness frustration leading to app discontinuance, suggesting social comparison effects are not uniform. Women are more likely to internalise thin/fit ideals from fitness and appearance-focused social content and experience greater body dissatisfaction from upward comparison (Dane & Bhatia, 2023; gender-differentiated fitness app design literature).

[DOCUMENTED] Young people (adolescents and young adults) are at higher risk of eating disorder development from social media exposure to fitness and appearance content. The critical developmental window for body image formation and eating disorder risk is ages 12–25 (eating disorder epidemiology and developmental psychology, foundational research).

## PART E: SUMMARY OF CONTRAINDICATIONS FOR VOLYUME

### E1. What the Evidence Forbids

**NO leaderboards.** Upward comparison erodes motivation for non-competitive users and increases burnout (evidence: Frontiers Psychology gamification research, 2025; Aral & Nicolaides, 2017, on asymmetric influence).

**NO public ranking by weight, calories, or body metrics.** Direct pathway to eating disorder harm in vulnerable users (evidence: Dane & Bhatia, 2023; meta-analysis r = 0.36 correlation with ED symptoms).

**NO unsolicited social comparison notifications or feed-based discovery.** Autonomy frustration + relatedness threat = discontinuance (evidence: Li et al., 2025; Frontiers Psychology, 2025).

**NO appearance-focused social sharing defaults.** Algorthmic amplification of unrealistic fitness ideals drives body dissatisfaction and ED relapse (evidence: Dane & Bhatia, 2023).

### E2. What the Evidence Permits and Supports

**Affirmation without ranking:** Kudos, peer recognition, and supportive comments on activities (evidence: Sakkas et al., "Kudos make you run!", S0378873322000909).

**Task-involving peer climates:** Shared pursuit of improvement and mastery, not public ranking (evidence: Moreno Murcia et al., 2008; Deci & Ryan, 2000).

**Community-based cooperation:** Buddy systems, group challenges focused on participation (not winning), and horizontal peer support (evidence: The Community Guide; fitness app retention studies).

**Relatedness satisfaction via belonging:** Virtual community spaces, shared goals, and mutual support without visibility of personal metrics (evidence: Li et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2023; Moreno Murcia et al., 2008).

**Horizontal social contagion:** Asymmetric peer influence where less active runners motivate more active ones to maintain engagement (evidence: Aral & Nicolaides, 2017).
