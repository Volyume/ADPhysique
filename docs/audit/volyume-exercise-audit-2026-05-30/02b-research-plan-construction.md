# Research: what a well-constructed resistance-training plan requires

Part of the Volyume exercise audit, 2026-05-30. Live web research into the
current evidence on hypertrophy plan construction, how exercise selection
shifts by goal, anatomical subregion balance, session sequencing, how the
leading coaching apps build plans, and the case for a machine-only
hypertrophy programme.

All claims below carry inline source URLs. Where a finding rests on a
primary study or meta-analysis the link points at that paper; where it
rests on a coach or platform's stated method the link points there and is
labelled as such.

## 1. Hypertrophy plan fundamentals from current evidence

### Weekly volume per muscle group

The dose-response between weekly set volume and muscle growth is the most
replicated finding in the field. Schoenfeld and colleagues' volume
meta-analysis found a graded response: lifters doing 10 or more sets per
muscle per week grew significantly more than those doing fewer than 5,
and the jump from 5-9 sets to 10+ added growth again
([askvora summary of the Schoenfeld volume meta-analysis](https://askvora.com/blog/resistance-training-meta-analysis-muscle-growth)).
The more recent Pelland/Schoenfeld dose-response meta-regression put a
number on the slope: each extra weekly set was associated with roughly
0.37% more growth, an effect that keeps climbing but with diminishing
returns at the top end
([The Resistance Training Dose-Response, SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460);
[full PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/The-Resistance-Training-Dose-Response.pdf)).

The practical landmark that falls out of this is the often-quoted **10-20
sets per muscle per week** range, with roughly 12-20 being the "sweet
spot" for most trained lifters
([Cora App, sets per muscle group](https://www.corahealth.app/blog/sets-per-muscle-group)).
Beyond about 20 weekly sets the benefit flattens and, for some, excess
volume eats into recovery
([Dabbs Fitness, training volume](https://www.dabbsfitness.com/the-science-of-training-volume-for-muscle-growth/)).

### MEV / MAV / MRV framing

The volume-landmark vocabulary comes from Mike Israetel and Renaissance
Periodization. Four landmarks describe how much work a muscle needs
([Dr Mike Israetel, MV/MEV/MAV/MRV explained](https://drmikeisraetel.com/dr-mike-israetel-mv-mev-mav-mrv-explained/);
[RP Strength, training volume landmarks](https://rpstrength.com/blogs/articles/training-volume-landmarks-muscle-growth)):

- **MV (Maintenance Volume)** — the minimum to hold muscle you already have.
- **MEV (Minimum Effective Volume)** — the least that actually grows muscle.
  Below this you maintain, you don't grow.
- **MAV (Maximum Adaptive Volume)** — the band where the best growth happens.
- **MRV (Maximum Recoverable Volume)** — the ceiling you can recover from
  before fatigue outruns adaptation.

The RP programming model is to start a training block near MEV and add
volume week by week toward MRV, then deload and reset. RP's own position is
that this **MEV → MAV → MRV ramp produces better long-run growth than
sitting at high volume permanently**
([FitnessRec, programming volume landmarks](https://fitnessrec.com/articles/how-to-program-volume-landmarks-mrv-mav-and-mev-explained-for-optimal-muscle-growth);
[Arvo, RP training guide](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training)).
Landmarks are individual: every lifter's MEV, MAV and MRV differ, which is
why autoregulation (section 5) sits on top of them.

### Training frequency per muscle (1x vs 2x+ per week)

The frequency picture has matured. Schoenfeld's 2016 frequency
meta-analysis found that, **when weekly volume is equated, training a
muscle 2x/week beat 1x/week** for hypertrophy
([Schoenfeld & Grgic frequency meta-analysis, Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/How-many-times-per-week-should-a-muscle-be-trained-Schoenfeld-Grgic/5f3b9845fdfd15200bdac0f7b9b36da457c48025)).
The later and larger 2018/2019 analysis softened that: with volume
equated, **frequency itself doesn't meaningfully change hypertrophy** —
once the weekly sets are matched, splitting them across more days is mostly
a matter of preference and recovery
([Stronger By Science, training frequency](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/frequency-muscle/);
[equal-volume frequency RCT, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2021.789403/full)).

The operational read: **volume is the driver, frequency is the delivery
mechanism**. Hitting a muscle 2x+/week is the practical way to fit 12-20
quality sets in without 20-set marathon sessions, and it spreads fatigue so
each set is harder and better
([Powertec, training all three deltoid heads](https://powertec.com/blogs/power-up-blog/how-to-target-all-three-shoulder-heads-for-maximum-results)).

### Rep ranges and proximity to failure

Load and rep range are far more flexible than the old "8-12 for size"
rule. The evidence is that **roughly 5 to 30+ reps all build similar muscle
when sets are taken close to failure and volume is matched** — there is no
magic "hypertrophy rep range"
([Stronger By Science, the hypertrophy range fact or fiction](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/hypertrophy-range-fact-fiction/);
[FitnessRec, hypertrophy rep range](https://fitnessrec.com/articles/hypertrophy-rep-range-for-muscle-growth-science-based-training-guide)).
The practical middle (about 6-15 reps) is favoured because the load is
heavy enough to create tension but light enough to keep form and stack hard
sets without the systemic drain of heavy fives
([Stronger By Science, hypertrophy range](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/hypertrophy-range-fact-fiction/)).

What does matter is **how close to failure each set gets**. The last
several reps before failure drive most of the growth, so sets need to land
within roughly 0-4 reps in reserve (RIR). Sets stopped more than ~5 reps
short consistently underperform
([Nutrient Metrics, proximity to failure](https://nutrient-metrics.com/en/hypertrophy/proximity-to-failure/)).
Going to true failure on every set isn't required: training 1-2 reps shy of
failure grows comparable muscle with less fatigue, which is why most
programmes prescribe RIR 0-3 with the harder end reserved for isolation and
machine work where failure is safe
([Outwork Nutrition, training to failure](https://outworknutrition.com/blogs/learn/does-training-to-failure-build-more-muscle)).

### Progressive overload models

Growth needs the stimulus to climb over time. The common models
([Stronger By Science, progressive overload strategies](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/progressive-overload-strategies/);
[Jefit, progressive overload 2026](https://www.jefit.com/wp/guide/progressive-overload-in-2026-the-science-backed-guide-to-continuous-strength-gains/)):

- **Load progression** — add 2.5-5 lb when all prescribed reps are hit.
  Works well on compounds for newer lifters, stalls fast on isolation work.
- **Rep progression** — keep the load, add reps each session.
- **Double progression** — the workhorse for hypertrophy. Climb the reps
  within a target band (say 8-12), and when you hit the top of the band at
  the target RIR, add weight and drop back to the bottom. This lets a lifter
  just chase a fixed effort (e.g. 1 RIR) and let overload happen
  automatically
  ([Modern Fit Mastery, double progression](https://modernfitmastery.com/the-double-progression-method/)).

Importantly, **the route to overload doesn't change the outcome much**:
Chaves et al. found adding load and adding reps produced similar quad
growth and strength when effort was matched
([NIH PMC, load vs repetition progression](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9528903/)).
So a plan can pick whichever progression is easiest to track for a given
movement.

## 2. How exercise selection should differ by goal

The same muscle can be trained many ways. The goal decides which way.

### Hypertrophy

For pure muscle growth the selection logic optimises for **stimulus to the
target muscle per unit of fatigue** rather than for moving the most weight.
That pushes selection toward:

- A **machine and cable bias**, because guided paths and external stability
  let the lifter take the target muscle close to failure without the
  whole-body fatigue and stabiliser limit of a free-weight version
  ([Robor Fitness, selecting hypertrophy exercises](https://www.roborfitness.com/blogs/news/how-to-select-the-best-hypertrophy-exercises);
  [The Gym Revolution, cables vs free weights for hypertrophy](https://www.thegymrevolution.co.uk/blog/2026/03/01/are-cable-machines-better-than-free-weights-for-hypertrophy/)).
- **Wide rep ranges** (roughly 6-20+), often with the lighter, higher-rep
  end on isolation and the moderate end on compounds, since all of it grows
  muscle when taken near failure
  ([Hevy, strength vs hypertrophy](https://www.hevyapp.com/strength-vs-hypertrophy/)).
- **More exercises and more isolation**, to cover subregions (section 3)
  and to spread fatigue across movements.

The organising idea is the **stimulus-to-fatigue ratio (SFR)**, a term
Israetel coined: the muscle-building stimulus an exercise delivers relative
to the systemic and joint fatigue it costs. A high SFR means lots of
tension on the target with little whole-body cost, so more productive
volume fits inside recovery
([Iron Alliances, SFR](https://ironalliances.com/blog/stimulus-to-fatigue-ratio/);
[Outlift, optimising SFR](https://outlift.com/stimulus-to-fatigue-ratio-sfr/)).
Leg press and hack squat score high; heavy conventional deadlifts and rack
pulls score low because the fatigue cost is enormous relative to the
hypertrophy stimulus
([Modern Fit Mastery, exercise selection for hypertrophy](https://modernfitmastery.com/exercise-selection-for-hypertrophy/)).

### Strength

For maximal strength the logic flips to **specificity**: you get strong at
what you practise, and strength gains are largely specific to the trained
movement and modality
([Maik Wiedenbach, strength vs hypertrophy](https://www.maikwiedenbach.com/strength-training-vs-hypertrophy-differences/)).
That favours:

- A **compound barbell bias** — squat, bench, deadlift and their close
  variants — practised as skills.
- **Lower reps and heavier loads** (often 1-6), longer rest, lower total
  volume, and tighter fatigue management
  ([Evogen, hypertrophy vs strength](https://www.evogennutrition.com/blogs/news/hypertrophy-vs-strength-training);
  [Hevy, strength vs hypertrophy](https://www.hevyapp.com/strength-vs-hypertrophy/)).
- **Less machine/isolation work** — useful as accessories to bring up weak
  points, but not the core. The free-weight-vs-machine meta-analysis is
  explicit that strength carryover is **specific to the modality trained**,
  so a barbell-strength goal needs barbell practice
  ([free-weight vs machine meta-analysis, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37582807/)).

### General fitness / body recomposition

For someone training for health, capacity and a leaner, more muscular body
rather than a stage or a meet, the plan simplifies:

- **Compound-led, full-body or upper/lower splits**, 3-4 sessions a week,
  for the best return on limited training time
  ([BetterMe, body recomp plan](https://betterme.world/articles/body-recomp-workout-plan/);
  [BodySpec, body recomposition plan](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/body_recomposition_workout_plan_an_8week_guide)).
- An **upper/lower split hitting each major muscle ~2x/week** is the common
  recommendation, since it covers the frequency point from section 1 with
  few sessions
  ([Transparent Labs, body recomposition](https://www.transparentlabs.com/blogs/all/body-recomposition-how-to-lose-fat-and-gain-muscle)).
- Beginners are highly responsive, so **fewer exercises trained
  consistently with steady progression** beats variety. The bulk/cut
  cycling that competitive bodybuilders use is not required for most general
  trainees
  ([Transparent Labs, body recomposition](https://www.transparentlabs.com/blogs/all/body-recomposition-how-to-lose-fat-and-gain-muscle)).

The thread across all three: hypertrophy optimises **stimulus per fatigue
and subregion coverage**, strength optimises **specificity to the
barbell**, general fitness optimises **return on limited time with
sustainable, repeatable movements**.

## 3. Anatomical subregion balance across a week

A muscle is not one homogeneous block, and a single "primary mover"
exercise does not train all of it equally. Regional hypertrophy research
shows that **different exercises grow different regions of the same
muscle**, largely because they load the muscle at different lengths and with
different leverage
([NSCA, building a balanced and symmetrical physique](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/ptq/building-a-balanced-and-symmetrical-physique/);
[Stronger By Science, is regional hypertrophy predictable](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/regional-hypertrophy/)).
A muscle-length / regional-hypertrophy meta-analysis confirms region-specific
growth driven by the length at which a muscle is loaded
([muscle length and regional hypertrophy, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40570881/);
[SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/464)).
That is why training only the primary mover leaves gaps — the regions that
exercise doesn't load hard simply don't grow as much.

### Chest

EMG and longitudinal data both show regional bias by bench angle. Incline
pressing drives the **clavicular (upper) head**, flat pressing the
**sternocostal (mid) portion**, and decline the **lower pec**. An
incline-only group grew upper-chest thickness more than flat-only or
combined groups
([chest EMG, NIH PMC five bench inclinations](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7579505/);
[12 bench-press variations EMG](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/download/39/124/)).
The takeaway for a week: cover **incline + flat + (decline or dip)** rather
than two flat presses.

### Back

The back splits into a "width" job and a "thickness" job. **Vertical pulls**
(pull-ups, lat pulldowns) maximise lat activation and build width;
**horizontal rows** (seated row, chest-supported row) hit the rhomboids and
mid-traps for thickness and posture, which vertical pulls underwork
([Jefit, vertical and horizontal pulling](https://www.jefit.com/wp/exercise-tips/building-a-strong-back-importance-of-vertical-horizontal-pulling/);
[Legion, horizontal pull exercises](https://legionathletics.com/horizontal-pull-exercises/)).
A complete back week wants **at least one vertical pull, one horizontal row,
plus a hip hinge and a rear-delt/upper-back movement** — each covers a
different region
([Jefit, vertical and horizontal pulling](https://www.jefit.com/wp/exercise-tips/building-a-strong-back-importance-of-vertical-horizontal-pulling/)).

### Shoulders (three deltoid heads)

A systematic review of deltoid activation shows the three heads are best
hit by different movements: **anterior** by presses and incline flyes,
**lateral** by abduction-pattern lateral raises, **posterior** by
horizontal abduction / rows / rear-delt flyes and pull-ups
([deltoid activation systematic review, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1360859222001607);
[Chris Beardsley, how should we train the deltoids](https://sandcresearch.medium.com/how-should-we-train-the-deltoids-f00d9c5388e2)).
Pressing alone develops the front delt and neglects side and rear, so the
10-20 weekly delt sets need **deliberate splitting across all three heads,
not piled onto presses**
([Powertec, all three shoulder heads](https://powertec.com/blogs/power-up-blog/how-to-target-all-three-shoulder-heads-for-maximum-results)).

### Triceps (long-head bias)

The Maeo overhead-vs-neutral study is the cleanest demonstration of
subregion-driven selection. Training elbow extension with the shoulder
**flexed overhead** (which lengthens the long head) produced **~1.5x the
triceps growth** of the neutral-arm version: 19.9% vs 13.5%, and the
overhead arm grew more not only in the long head but in the lateral and
medial heads too
([Maeo et al., overhead vs neutral triceps, EJSS / Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279);
[Stronger By Science, overhead extensions vs pushdowns](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-triceps/)).
A triceps week built only on pushdowns under-trains the long head. It needs
**overhead or behind-the-body extension work as well**.

### Hamstrings (hip hinge AND knee flexion)

The hamstrings span two joints and the heads grow differently depending on
which joint moves. **Hip-hinge / stiff-leg deadlift work** preferentially
grows the **semimembranosus and long head of biceps femoris**, while
**knee-flexion / Nordic / leg-curl work** preferentially grows the
**semitendinosus**, with the short head of biceps femoris best reached by
knee flexion
([Stronger By Science, exercise selection for the hamstrings](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/exercise-selection-hamstrings/);
[hip- vs knee-dominant hamstring trial, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-023-00680-w)).
So a complete hamstring week pairs **one hip-hinge movement with one
knee-flexion movement** — neither alone covers all four heads.

### Why the primary mover leaves gaps

The mechanism tying these together: an exercise loads its target muscle
hardest at a specific length and leverage, and **growth concentrates in the
region under the most strain**. The preacher curl, for example, loads the
elbow flexors in their lengthened position and preferentially grows the
distal arm
([NSCA, balanced physique](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/ptq/building-a-balanced-and-symmetrical-physique/)).
Pick one exercise per muscle and you train the regions it favours and miss
the rest. The fix is variety chosen for **coverage**, not novelty:
different angles, different joint positions, hitting both lengthened and
shortened biases across the week.

## 4. Exercise sequencing within a session

### Compound before isolation

The exercise-order meta-analysis is clear that **whatever you put first
improves most**, because you're freshest. For strength, that means putting
the compounds you care about first. But for **hypertrophy specifically, the
meta-analysis found order doesn't meaningfully change total growth** —
compound-to-isolation and isolation-to-compound produced similar
hypertrophy
([exercise order meta-analysis, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/);
[hypertrophy variables umbrella review, NIH PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9302196/)).

So the default of compound-before-isolation is sound but **flexible**: it
exists mainly so heavy multi-joint work happens fresh (safety and load),
and so the lifter can pre-fatigue or prioritise a lagging muscle by
deliberately moving its isolation work earlier when growth in that muscle
is the goal.

### Antagonist pairing / supersets

Pairing opposing muscles (e.g. a row with a press, a curl with a triceps
movement) is an efficiency tool that holds up well. Agonist-antagonist
supersets **cut session time by roughly a third to a half while producing
the same hypertrophy, strength and endurance** as traditional straight sets
([superset vs traditional, SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/419);
[superset systematic review and meta-analysis, NIH PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/)).
The resting antagonist can even slightly boost reps on the working muscle
([Reshape, supersetting opposing muscles](https://www.reshapeapp.ai/blog/supersetting-opposing-muscles-save-time-without-sacrificing-strength)).

The trade-off is acute fatigue: supersets raise blood lactate, perceived
exertion and internal load, so they're best for time-limited sessions and
for muscle pairs that don't share stabilisers, not for back-to-back
high-fatigue compounds
([superset review, NIH PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/)).

### Fatigue management and placing high-fatigue / high-SFR work

The practical sequencing logic that falls out:

- Put **high-skill, high-load, high-fatigue compounds early**, while the
  lifter is fresh and technique is safe.
- Put **high-SFR, low-skill machine and isolation work later**, where it can
  be taken to or near failure safely without the lifter's stability or
  systemic fatigue being the limiter
  ([Iron Alliances, SFR](https://ironalliances.com/blog/stimulus-to-fatigue-ratio/);
  [Outlift, SFR](https://outlift.com/stimulus-to-fatigue-ratio-sfr/)).
- Use **antagonist supersets to compress time** when the pairing doesn't
  share fatigue, and keep them away from the heaviest compounds.
- For a **lagging muscle**, move its work earlier in the session to spend
  the freshest, highest-quality sets on it — order doesn't change total
  hypertrophy much, so this prioritisation is low-cost.

## 5. How leading coaching apps construct plans

A consistent pattern across the best platforms: a **structured volume model
(usually MEV→MRV landmarks), autoregulation from user feedback, fixed core
exercises with rotation between blocks, and progression by double
progression or RPE/RIR**.

### RP Hypertrophy (Renaissance Periodization)

The clearest expression of the landmark model in software. It ships ~45+
templates built on Israetel's principles, **starts each mesocycle near MEV
and ramps weekly volume toward MRV**, then deloads. It **autoregulates
volume from per-session feedback on pump, soreness and workload**, adjusting
the next session's sets up or down. When a lifter stalls, it prompts
swapping low-SFR movements for higher-SFR alternatives rather than just
piling on volume
([Alibaba Wellness, how RP Hypertrophy works](https://wellness.alibaba.com/fitlife/rp-hypertrophy-app-review-guide);
[Dr Muscle, RP Hypertrophy critique](https://dr-muscle.com/rp-hypertrophy-app-critique/)).

### Dr. Muscle

An AI-driven app built explicitly on Schoenfeld and Israetel's research. It
**encodes MEV/MAV/MRV landmarks** and uses machine learning over logged
sets — including "plus sets" (AMRAP) to read true capacity — to
**auto-adjust load and volume each session**: complete reps easily and it
adds weight or volume, struggle and it scales back, matching the individual's
real recovery rate
([Dr Muscle, the science behind the app](https://dr-muscle.com/building-muscle/);
[Dr Muscle, what makes it different](https://dr-muscle.com/what-makes-dr-muscle-different/)).

### Caliber

A coach-plus-software hybrid that favours **"mastery over variety"**: it
keeps the same core lifts for weeks and drives the **double-progression
method (reps first, then load)**. It selects from a database of 500+
validated exercises based on goals, equipment and limitations, organises
training into **3-6 week mesocycles rotating strength / hypertrophy /
conditioning**, and layers a human coach who reviews logged sessions and
form videos and adjusts the plan
([BarBend, Caliber review](https://barbend.com/caliber-fitness-app-review/);
[Caliber, how it works](https://caliberstrong.com/membership/)).

### Boostcamp

A library-and-tracking model rather than a generative one. It hosts 11,000+
programmes including **130+ coach-designed routines from respected
evidence-based coaches (Eric Helms, Alberto Nunez, Geoff Schofield)**,
filterable by level, goal and days/week, with progressive-overload tracking
and RPE/RIR logging built in. Its strength is curated, coach-authored
structure plus solid logging, free
([Boostcamp, free workout app](https://www.boostcamp.app/free-workout-app);
[Boostcamp, programs](https://www.boostcamp.app/programs)).

### JuggernautAI

The most sophisticated autoregulation of the group, aimed at strength /
powerlifting. It **prescribes a target RPE per set and recalibrates the next
session's loads from logged RPE vs performance** — come in strong and it
moves faster, come in beaten down and loads back off. It builds **periodised
blocks with wave loading and competition peaking**, and a low readiness
check-in can trigger a recommended extra rest day
([PowerliftingTechnique, JuggernautAI review](https://powerliftingtechnique.com/juggernaut-ai-review/);
[Fitness Volt, RPE calculator vs JuggernautAI](https://fitnessvolt.com/rpe-training/comparisons/rpe-calculator-vs-juggernautai/)).

### What makes the good ones good

- **An explicit volume model** (MEV→MRV landmarks) rather than a fixed set
  count, so volume ramps and deloads.
- **Autoregulation** from real feedback — soreness/pump/workload (RP, Dr.
  Muscle) or RPE/RIR and readiness (JuggernautAI) — so the plan tracks the
  individual's recovery instead of a generic curve.
- **Stable core exercises with deliberate rotation between blocks**
  (Caliber's mastery-over-variety, RP's swap-low-SFR-when-stalled) — enough
  consistency to progress, enough rotation to manage staleness and SFR.
- **A clear progression rule**, almost always double progression or RPE-led
  load adjustment.

## 6. The case for a machine-only hypertrophy programme

A machine-only plan is **not a compromise for growth** — for hypertrophy
specifically the evidence puts machines on equal footing with free weights,
and the practical advantages tilt in machines' favour for many lifters.

### Machines match free weights for muscle growth

The headline meta-analysis comparing free-weight vs machine training found
**no significant difference in hypertrophy** between modalities when volume
was equated; strength gains were specific to the modality trained, but
muscle size was not
([free-weight vs machine meta-analysis, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37582807/);
[full text, NIH PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10426227/)).
A within-subject 2024 trial trained one leg with free-weight lunges and the
other with machine incline leg press; **both legs grew significantly with no
real difference**, measured regionally at the rectus femoris and vastus
lateralis
([T3, machines vs free weights new study](https://www.t3.com/active/machines-vs-free-weights-is-one-better-for-muscle-growth-a-new-study-confirms)).
The consensus position is that, for size, the choice comes down to
preference and goals, not effectiveness
([Jefit, free weights vs machines](https://www.jefit.com/wp/jefit-news-product-updates/free-weights-vs-machines-what-does-science-really-say/);
[GrabGains, machines vs free weights](https://grabgains.com/blog/machines-vs-free-weights)).

### Why machines can be better for a hypertrophy goal

- **Higher stimulus-to-fatigue ratio.** Guided paths and external support
  remove the stabiliser and balance demand, so the target muscle, not a
  weak link, is the limiter. More productive volume fits inside recovery
  ([Iron Alliances, SFR](https://ironalliances.com/blog/stimulus-to-fatigue-ratio/);
  [The Gym Revolution, cables vs free weights](https://www.thegymrevolution.co.uk/blog/2026/03/01/are-cable-machines-better-than-free-weights-for-hypertrophy/)).
- **Safer to train near failure.** You can push to 0-1 RIR — the most
  productive zone — without a spotter and without the failure point being
  dangerous, which is exactly where free weights get risky
  ([Robor Fitness, hypertrophy exercise selection](https://www.roborfitness.com/blogs/news/how-to-select-the-best-hypertrophy-exercises)).
- **Stability and isolation.** Machines and cables isolate the target and
  hold a stable path, so effort lands on the muscle you're trying to grow
  ([The Gym Revolution, cables vs free weights](https://www.thegymrevolution.co.uk/blog/2026/03/01/are-cable-machines-better-than-free-weights-for-hypertrophy/)).
- **Accessibility.** Lower skill and bracing demand makes machines
  approachable for beginners, returning lifters, and anyone training around
  an injury or joint limitation, without sacrificing the growth stimulus.

### The honest caveat

The one thing machines don't transfer is **modality-specific maximal
strength** — strength carries over to the trained pattern, so a barbell-1RM
goal still needs barbell practice
([free-weight vs machine meta-analysis, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37582807/)).
For **hypertrophy, body recomposition and general fitness**, that caveat
doesn't bite, and a machine-only plan stands as a legitimate, evidence-backed
way to build muscle — with real upside in safety, accessibility and
stimulus-per-fatigue.

A machine-only programme should still apply every principle above: hit
**10-20 sets per muscle per week**, train each muscle **~2x/week**, work
**near failure (0-3 RIR)**, **progress by double progression**, and pick
machine and cable variations that cover each muscle's **subregions** (incline
+ flat + decline-pattern chest; vertical pulldown + horizontal row;
all three delt heads; overhead and pushdown triceps; seated/lying leg curl
plus a machine hip-hinge or hyperextension for hamstrings). The modality is
machines; the construction rules don't change.
