# Coach Audit 02: Division Research, Classic Physique

Status: COMPLETE | Timestamp: 2026-06-01 | Division: Classic Physique

## How to read this document

Every coaching claim below is attributed to a named coach or a named,
published methodology. Every physiology claim is tied to a named
researcher or study. Numbers that come straight from a published source
are cited to that source. Numbers that I have inferred to give Volyume a
usable default are tagged `[synthesis]`. Inferred numbers are never
presented as if a federation or coach published them.

A note on dates. Chris Bumstead won his sixth Classic Physique Olympia in
October 2024 and retired. The reigning Classic Physique Mr. Olympia as of
the 2025 contest is Ramon Dino, with Mike Sommerfeld second and Terrence
Ruffin third (Generation Iron, Fitness Volt, 2025). So the coaching map
for this division has shifted: Bumstead's old coaches (Hany Rambod, and
before that Iain Valliere) still matter as methodology sources, but the
men coaching the current top three are the live reference points.

## 1. The aesthetic standard and current judging

### What the division is

Classic Physique sits deliberately between Men's Physique and Open
Bodybuilding. The NPC describes the target as the "total package": a
balance of size, symmetry and muscularity, but capped so that no athlete
carries Open-level mass (NPC News Online, Official Classic Physique
Rules). The reference point is the Golden Era physique: Steve Reeves,
Frank Zane, early Arnold. Broad shoulders, a small waist, full muscle
bellies with sweep rather than blocky mass, and lines that flow from
shoulder to waist to thigh without a single part shouting over the rest.

The single mechanism that enforces this, and the thing that makes Classic
Physique genuinely different from every other division, is the
height-indexed weight cap. You cannot simply add muscle without limit. If
you weigh in over your bracket you are out, or you move to Open or Men's
Physique (Center Podium; Major Fitness, Classic vs Open).

### What wins

- A complete V-taper read from the front and the back. Wide, capped
  delts and broad lats set against a waist that looks narrow both in
  relaxed stance and under a vacuum (NPC News Online).
- Proportion above raw size. Judges want a smooth transition shoulder to
  waist to leg, "with no one muscle overwhelming another" (Fitness24Xpress
  paraphrasing NPC criteria; Major Fitness).
- Conditioning that is hard and dry but not Open-level shredded. Classic
  rewards full muscle bellies with clear separation, not the grainy,
  flat, depleted look that Open sometimes rewards (NPC News Online).
- Posing artistry. Classic Physique judges score the mandatory poses and
  the posing routine, including a vacuum and a classic "most muscular",
  more heavily than Men's Physique does. Presentation is part of the
  score, not a tiebreaker (NPC News Online; Major Fitness).

### What is marked down

- Coming in over the weight cap (disqualification from the division).
- A blocky or thick waist. Distended midsection, weak vacuum, or
  oversized obliques break the taper and are penalised hard.
- Open-style mass that destroys the lines: over-built traps that swallow
  the neck, separated "3D" delts that wreck the shoulder-to-waist sweep,
  legs so big the taper inverts.
- Under-developed legs. This is the trap. The cap tempts athletes to
  starve the legs to stay light, but Classic Physique poses (quarter
  turns, the front and rear, the abdominal-and-thigh) fully expose the
  legs, and judges mark down a top-heavy physique (see section 4).

### The weight caps (documented)

NPC / IFBB Pro classes were restructured in August 2023, then adjusted
mid-September 2023, and Tyler Manion (IFBB Pro League VP) announced a
further upward adjustment to favour shorter athletes (Fitness Volt,
Tyler Manion new weight rules; Center Podium).

Documented lower NPC brackets (NPC News Online via search extraction;
Center Podium):

| Height | Max weight |
|---|---|
| up to and incl. 5'4" | 167 lb |
| over 5'4" to 5'5" | 172 lb |
| over 5'5" to 5'6" | 177 lb |
| over 5'6" to 5'7" | 182 lb |
| 6'0" | 217 lb |
| over 6'0" (Division D top) | up to ~267 lb |

The brackets rise roughly 5 lb per inch through the middle of the range
[synthesis], so a 5'9"–5'10" athlete sits around 195–202 lb and a
6'0" athlete at 217 lb (the 217 and the 267 figures are documented; the
intermediate 5 lb-per-inch slope is inferred for planning use and tagged
as such). Founder should confirm the exact current NPC table from
npcnewsonline.com before any in-app weight-target feature ships, because
the table has changed three times since 2023 and the federation PDFs
returned 403 to automated fetching during this research.

## 2. Top five coaches for Classic Physique (named, with credentials)

### 1. Chris Aceto

Credentials: three-decade diet-and-prep coach, Health Sciences degree
from Springfield College, summa cum laude in applied exercise science
(MuscleMecca profile). Known as "The Technician". Pioneered moving
bodybuilders off keto peak-weeks toward carbohydrate cycling across the
whole prep (MuscleMecca; Old School Labs podcast).
Notable athletes: Jay Cutler (multiple Mr. Olympia), Chris Cormier, Troy
Alves, Shawn Rhoden, and currently Ramon Dino, the 2025 Classic Physique
Mr. Olympia. In 2025 Aceto coached winners across Open, Classic and
Men's Physique in a single Olympia (Mixvale; Itatiaia, 2025).

### 2. Hany Rambod

Credentials: creator of FST-7 (Fascia Stretch Training Seven), developed
2007 for Phil Heath. Coach to a long line of Olympia champions across
divisions (BarBend; Fitness Volt). For Classic Physique specifically he
coached Chris Bumstead from 2022 through his retirement after the 2024
Olympia, and has worked with Terrence Ruffin in peak weeks (BarBend,
Ruffin upper-body training with Rambod; Fitness Volt, CBum new coach).

### 3. Neil "Yoda" Hill

Credentials: British coach, creator of the Y3T (Yoda Three Training)
system. Coaching pedigree includes Flex Lewis (212 Olympia dynasty),
William Bonac, Zach Khan (BarBend, Y3T explained). Currently coaches
Mike Sommerfeld, the 2025 Classic Physique Olympia runner-up
(search extraction, Mixvale/Itatiaia coverage of the 2025 final).

### 4. Terrence Ruffin

Credentials: top-three Classic Physique Olympia athlete (third in 2025)
and an active coach himself. Self-taught in nutrition, biomechanics and
posing during military service; credits Joe Bennett for his scientific
foundation (Bodybuilding-Wizard biography; Iron Rebel). His training
voice is the most science-and-artistry-blended of the group: antagonist
pairing, high frequency on lagging parts, posing as a skill (BarBend,
Ruffin push/pull). He is included as a coach because he programmes for
others and is one of the most-cited Classic Physique technical voices.

### 5. Joe Bennett (The Hypertrophy Coach)

Credentials: founder of Hypertrophy Coach and Hypertrophy Coach
University, including the Applied Hypertrophy Biomechanics curriculum.
Has worked with 10+ Olympia-level athletes across categories and was
Terrence Ruffin's formative coach (Hypertrophy Coach; J3 University
podcast; Bodybuilding-Wizard). His relevance to Classic Physique is the
biomechanics-first, minimum-effective-volume philosophy that has shaped
how the current generation selects exercises by line of pull and
resistance profile rather than by tradition.

Honourable mention as a methodology source, not a current coach: Iain
Valliere, who coached Bumstead before Rambod (Generation Iron).

## 3. Programming philosophy by coach

### Hany Rambod, FST-7

(Source: Muscle & Brawn FST-7 program; Fitness Volt FST-7 guide;
BarBend FST-7.)

- Structure of a session: a heavy compound (e.g. 3 x 6–8, long 2–3 min
  rests), then a moderate compound or isolation (3 x 8–12, 1–2 min),
  then the signature finisher: 7 sets of an isolation, often 8–12 reps,
  with only 30–45 s rest, usually on a machine to keep the focus on the
  target muscle.
- The theory: short-rest pump sets stretch the fascia from the inside and
  drive nutrients in. The evidence base for the fascia-stretching
  mechanism specifically is weak; what the protocol reliably delivers is
  high metabolic stress and time-efficient volume. Treat the rationale as
  Rambod's model, and the hypertrophy outcome as standard
  metabolite-and-volume-driven growth [synthesis].
- Frequency: large muscle groups once per week, small groups (delts,
  arms, calves) twice (Fitness Volt). This is a lower-frequency,
  higher-per-session model than the science-based camp prefers.

### Chris Aceto

(Source: MuscleMecca profile; Old School Labs podcast.)

- Customisation over template. Progressive overload and periodisation are
  the backbone, but the programme is fitted to the individual's response
  and weak points rather than imposed.
- Recovery is explicitly built in to avoid overtraining; he is not a
  max-volume coach.
- His real edge is nutrition: carbohydrate cycling across the entire prep
  to hold muscle fullness while getting hard, rather than depleting late.
  For Classic Physique, where full bellies inside a weight cap is the
  whole game, this fullness-preserving approach is the point.

### Neil Hill, Y3T

(Source: BarBend Y3T; Noob Gains; The Barbell Y3T guide.)

- A repeating three-week wave. Week 1: heavy compounds, 6–10 reps to
  failure. Week 2: compounds plus isolation, 10–15 reps. Week 3: very
  high rep, 20+ and up to extended drop-set sequences, with fewer working
  sets but far higher reps.
- Session volume is high, often 25+ sets per workout in the higher-rep
  weeks (BarBend).
- The rationale Hill gives: rotating rep ranges hits different fibre and
  metabolic pathways and lets joints recover from heavy loading. Born
  partly from Hill's own knee injury forcing him away from constant heavy
  work.

### Terrence Ruffin

(Source: BarBend push/pull; Generation Iron routine; Bodybuilding-Wizard.)

- High frequency on lagging parts: he has run arms (biceps and triceps)
  three times a week in the off-season. "Frequency is key. When you want
  to build a body part, think about how frequently you can train it."
- Antagonist pairing within a session (e.g. chest with back, biceps with
  triceps) so one muscle recovers while the other works.
- Strategic periodisation to bring up weak points rather than train
  everything equally. Posing trained as its own conditioning and skill
  block, not an afterthought.

### Joe Bennett

(Source: Hypertrophy Coach; J3 University; BarBend perfect leg workout.)

- "As little as possible to produce maximal results." Progressive
  overload matters more than chasing volume targets.
- Exercise chosen by biomechanics: matching the resistance profile to
  where the muscle is strong. Bennett's position is that most muscles
  produce the most tension in the mid-range and stretched positions, so
  he favours full range of motion and is comfortable emphasising the
  stretched portion.
- Execution over exercise novelty. "Don't exercise. Execute."

## 4. Muscle-group priority ranking and why

Ranking is for a Classic Physique athlete who is already reasonably
balanced. It reflects what the division's poses and cap actually reward.

1. **Delts (especially lateral and rear)**. The width that creates the
   taper from the top. Capped, round side delts are the single biggest
   driver of the V from the front, and rear delts hold the back poses
   together. Ruffin and Rambod both train delts at higher frequency than
   most other parts (BarBend).
2. **Back width and detail (lats plus mid-back)**. The other half of the
   taper, and the difference-maker in the rear poses. Wide-grip vertical
   pulling for width; the detail comes from rows. Wide-grip pull-ups
   recruit the lateral lats more than narrow grips, which is why width
   work leans on them (BarBend weak-points; Mikolo V-taper guide).
3. **Waist control (not "abs volume")**. This is a priority by subtraction.
   The aim is a small, controlled waist and a strong vacuum, which means
   transverse abdominis work and restraint on heavy weighted obliques and
   exercises that thicken the midsection. Vacuum practice: holds of
   ~20–30 s, several sets, several times a week (Mikolo guide). The waist
   is judged as much as any muscle because it defines the taper.
4. **Legs (quad sweep and hamstrings, balanced)**. Fully judged in Classic
   Physique, unlike Men's Physique. The poses expose the legs, so they
   must match the upper body. The nuance: build the quad sweep (outer
   teardrop) and hamstring fullness for shape, while not so overbuilding
   the legs that you blow the weight cap or invert the taper. This is the
   division's hardest balancing act.
5. **Chest (upper-chest bias for the square, full look)**. Wanted full and
   proportionate, framing the taper, not pec-dominant in the Men's
   Physique way.
6. **Arms**. Wanted full and proportionate to delts and torso. Built, but
   not chased past the point where they unbalance the lines.
7. **Traps**. Deliberately restrained. Over-built traps shorten the neck
   line and crowd the delts, which flattens the shoulder-to-waist read.
   This is a place Classic diverges sharply from Open, where huge traps
   are an asset.

Why this order: the cap means every kilo has to earn its place on the
physique. Width up top and a tight waist buy the most "taper" per unit of
bodyweight, legs must be present because they are judged, and the parts
that read as Open mass (traps, oversized everything) are restrained.

## 5. Weekly volume per muscle group (set ranges)

These ranges synthesise the published coach methods above with the
volume-landmark research most cited in the field: Brad Schoenfeld and
colleagues' dose-response work showing more weekly sets generally drive
more hypertrophy up to a point (Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger, 2017,
*Journal of Sports Sciences*, dose-response meta-analysis), and Mike
Israetel / Renaissance Periodization's MEV–MAV–MRV volume-landmark
framework. Off-season ranges sit nearer MAV, prep ranges pull back toward
maintenance to protect recovery in a deficit. All set numbers are
`[synthesis]` defaults built from those sources unless a specific coach
number is cited.

| Muscle group | Off-season (weekly sets) | Prep (weekly sets) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Side/rear delts | 16–22 [synthesis] | 12–16 [synthesis] | Highest priority; train 2x+/week (Rambod, Ruffin frequency) |
| Back (width + detail) | 14–20 [synthesis] | 10–16 [synthesis] | Split across vertical and horizontal pulls |
| Quads | 12–18 [synthesis] | 8–14 [synthesis] | Enough to be judged-proportionate, not Open-huge |
| Hamstrings | 10–16 [synthesis] | 8–12 [synthesis] | Shape and fullness for rear poses |
| Chest (upper bias) | 12–16 [synthesis] | 8–14 [synthesis] | Framing, not domination |
| Biceps | 10–16 [synthesis] | 8–12 [synthesis] | Ruffin has run 3x/week off-season for lagging arms |
| Triceps | 10–16 [synthesis] | 8–12 [synthesis] | As above |
| Front delts | 4–8 [synthesis] | 3–6 [synthesis] | Indirect work from pressing usually covers this |
| Calves | 12–18 [synthesis] | 10–14 [synthesis] | High frequency tolerated well |
| Traps (direct) | 2–6 [synthesis] | 0–4 [synthesis] | Deliberately low to protect the neck/delt line |
| Abs/TVA + vacuum | daily-to-3x vacuum holds [synthesis] | same | Waist control, not midsection mass |

General rule, documented: large groups can be productively trained once
per week in a high-per-session model (Rambod/FST-7) or split across two
sessions in the higher-frequency models (Ruffin, Bennett). Smaller groups
and lagging parts go to 2–3x weekly frequency across all the camps.

## 6. Exercise selection philosophy

### Prioritised

- **Lateral raises and rear-delt work, varied and high-frequency**. Cables,
  dumbbells, machines, incline-bench raises for a longer stretch (BarBend
  weak-points; Mikolo). Width is the brand of the division.
- **Wide-grip vertical pulling** (pull-ups, wide pulldowns) for lat width,
  because the wide grip recruits the lateral lats more for the taper
  (BarBend; Mikolo V-taper guide).
- **Stretch-position and full-ROM movements**, per Bennett's biomechanics:
  match the load to where the muscle is strong and don't skip the
  stretched range (Hypertrophy Coach).
- **Quad-sweep work**: hack squats, leg press, sissy squats to build the
  outer sweep and teardrop while keeping the waist and glutes from
  over-thickening (Mikolo, paraphrasing the avoid-overbuilding-hips
  guidance in the weak-points search).
- **Machine isolation finishers** (FST-7 style) for a controlled pump
  without systemic fatigue, Rambod's signature.
- **Posing practice as training**: Ruffin treats posing, including the
  vacuum, as a trainable conditioning and presentation skill.

### Approached with caution or avoided

- **Heavy obliques work and heavy loaded side-bends**. They thicken the
  waist and break the taper. The waist is judged.
- **Excessive direct trap work and heavy shrugs**. Big traps shorten the
  neck line and crowd the delts (Open trait, not Classic).
- **Movements that overbuild the hips and adductors past balance**. Some
  coaches steer toward sissy and hack squats over maximal back-squat and
  conventional-deadlift volume specifically to avoid over-thickening the
  hips and waist for this look (Mikolo weak-points search). This is a
  Classic-specific judgement call, not a universal "squats are bad" claim:
  squats build the legs the division needs, but the trade-off against
  waist and hip thickness is managed.

Caveat: the "avoid squats and deadlifts" framing came from a secondary
guide, not from a named top-five coach directly. Treat it as a
defensible Classic-Physique heuristic, not gospel. The principle the
named coaches do agree on is selecting and dosing exercises to protect
the taper and the cap, not blanket-banning compounds.

## 7. Where Classic Physique programming diverges from Men's Physique and Open

### Versus Men's Physique

- **Legs are fully judged in Classic, barely in Men's Physique**. Men's
  Physique competes in board shorts; the legs are not really assessed, so
  leg volume is often minimal. Classic competes in posing trunks that
  expose the whole lower body, so legs must be trained to be
  proportionate (Major Fitness; Fitro). This is the biggest programming
  divergence: a Men's Physique plan with token leg work fails in Classic.
- **More total muscle, and a weight cap to manage it**. Classic carries
  more mass than Men's Physique but is bounded by the height-indexed cap.
  Men's Physique has its own caps but a lower mass target overall.
- **Full mandatory-pose set plus a routine in Classic**. Front double
  biceps, side chest, back double biceps, abdominal-and-thigh, most
  muscular, plus a posing routine (NPC News Online; Major Fitness).
  Men's Physique does quarter turns only, no most-muscular, no side
  chest. So Classic programming must include posing and vacuum
  conditioning that Men's Physique can skip.

### Versus Open Bodybuilding

- **The cap is the divergence**. Open chases maximal mass with no upper
  weight limit. Classic is bounded, so programming optimises for shape,
  proportion and the taper per kilo, not for absolute size (Major Fitness;
  Center Podium).
- **Restraint on the parts Open maximises**. Traps, gut/midsection volume,
  and sheer leg mass are assets in Open and liabilities in Classic past a
  point. An Open plan run on a Classic athlete tends to thicken the waist
  and traps and invert the taper.
- **Conditioning target differs**. Open often rewards extreme grainy
  dryness; Classic wants hard but full bellies, which is why fullness-
  preserving nutrition (Aceto's carb cycling) matters more here than the
  late-prep depletion sometimes used in Open.
- **Posing emphasis**. Both pose, but Classic explicitly scores the
  classic/Golden-Era presentation and the vacuum, which shapes how much
  posing and waist-control work goes into the plan.

The honest summary: Men's Physique under-trains legs and skips posing;
Open maximises everything without a cap. Classic is the only one of the
three where the entire programme is an optimisation problem under a hard
bodyweight constraint, with the waist and traps deliberately held back
and the legs deliberately kept proportionate rather than maximal.

## 8. Common weak points and how the best coaches address them

| Weak point | Why it hurts in Classic | How it is addressed |
|---|---|---|
| Thick or distended waist, weak vacuum | Breaks the taper; the waist is judged | TVA work and vacuum holds (~20–30 s, multiple sets, 3–4x/week, Mikolo); cut heavy oblique loading; restrained midsection volume |
| Narrow back / poor lat width | Kills the V from front and back | Wide-grip vertical pulling for lateral-lat width (BarBend, Mikolo); add detail rows |
| Flat or small side delts | No width up top, no taper | High-frequency lateral raises across cable/dumbbell/machine; incline-bench raises for stretch (BarBend, Ruffin/Rambod frequency) |
| Lagging legs (the cap trap) | Top-heavy physique marked down; poses expose legs | Train legs properly for shape: quad sweep via hack/sissy/leg press, hamstring fullness; manage the cap with conditioning, not by starving legs |
| Over-built traps | Shorten neck line, crowd delts, read as Open | Minimal or no direct trap work; let pulling cover them |
| Flat upper chest | Square, full chest frames the taper | Upper-chest-biased pressing and flyes; proportion, not pec domination |
| Poor posing / no vacuum skill | Presentation is scored | Posing trained as its own block (Ruffin); vacuum practised as a skill |

The cross-cutting principle from the named coaches: diagnose the
individual's specific gap and bias frequency and volume toward it
(Ruffin, Aceto, Bennett all say versions of this), rather than adding
volume everywhere.

## 9. Recovery management specific to this division

Classic Physique athletes spend long stretches in or near a calorie
deficit to hit a hard, dry look while staying under the weight cap. That
makes recovery a sharper constraint than in mass-chasing Open, where the
athlete is more often in a surplus.

- **Volume is pulled back in prep, not pushed up**. The volume table in
  section 5 drops from off-season toward maintenance in prep because
  recovery capacity falls in a deficit. This matches the RP volume-
  landmark logic (Israetel / Renaissance Periodization): train nearer MEV
  when systemic recovery is compromised. [synthesis, applied from the RP
  framework]
- **Aceto's recovery-and-fullness nutrition**. Carbohydrate cycling across
  the whole prep, rather than late keto/depletion, keeps glycogen and
  muscle fullness up, which both protects training quality and protects
  the full-belly look Classic rewards (MuscleMecca; Old School Labs).
- **Frequency as a recovery tool, not just a growth tool**. Ruffin's high
  frequency on lagging parts spreads volume across more sessions, which
  lowers per-session fatigue. Antagonist pairing lets one muscle recover
  while the other works (BarBend).
- **Y3T's built-in deload-by-design**. Hill's three-week wave rotates a
  very high-rep, lighter-load week that takes load off the joints and CNS
  before the next heavy week, an in-built recovery rotation (BarBend Y3T).
- **Joint management**. Several of these systems (Y3T explicitly) arose
  partly from managing injury, so lighter, higher-rep blocks and machine
  isolation (FST-7 finishers) are used to keep training stress on the
  muscle and off the joints.

Research anchor for the recovery framing: protein-synthesis and recovery
windows scale with training status and energy availability (the body of
work summarised by Brad Schoenfeld on volume, frequency and recovery; and
the volume-landmark model popularised by Mike Israetel). Where I have
applied these to give Classic-specific defaults, the numbers are tagged
`[synthesis]`.
