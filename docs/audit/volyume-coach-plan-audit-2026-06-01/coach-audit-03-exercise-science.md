# Coach Plan Audit 03: Exercise Science

Status: COMPLETE | Timestamp: 2026-06-01 | Domain: Exercise science

Scope: the peer-reviewed evidence base for hypertrophy programming, set
against elite coaching practice, with a recommendation for how an
evidence-led app should behave when the two conflict. British English.
Findings are tagged to source. Inferences are tagged [synthesis].

---

## 1. Optimal weekly volume per muscle group

### The dose-response evidence

The anchor is Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2017), "Dose-response
relationship between weekly resistance training volume and increases in
muscle mass: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Journal of Sports
Sciences* 35(11):1073-1082. Pooling 15 studies, they found a graded
dose-response: weekly per-muscle volume of 10+ sets produced larger gains
than 5-9 sets, which beat <5 sets. The reported gradient was roughly a
0.37% increase in muscle size per additional weekly set, with the
trend continuing across the ranges studied. This is the source most
coaches cite for the "10+ sets" floor.

The current and more precise picture comes from Pelland, Remmert,
Robinson, Hinson and Zourdos (2025), "The Resistance Training
Dose-Response: Meta-Regressions Exploring the Effects of Weekly Volume
and Frequency on Muscle Hypertrophy and Strength Gains", *Sports
Medicine* (published 2025 from the Muscle Physiology Laboratory, Florida
Atlantic University; preprint on SportRxiv 2024). It modelled 220
hypertrophy effects and 490 strength effects and distinguished direct
sets (the target muscle worked directly) from indirect/fractional sets.
Findings:

- Hypertrophy keeps rising with volume across the range studied, but
  with clearly diminishing returns. The curve flattens; it does not
  invert into harm within the studied range.
- Strength is far less volume-sensitive than hypertrophy. A handful of
  hard sets per week captures most of the strength gain.
- Volume and frequency carry distinct dose-response signatures, so they
  should be modelled separately rather than collapsed into one number.

Where benefits plateau: the honest reading of Pelland et al. (2025) and
Schoenfeld et al. (2017) is that the marginal gain per set shrinks
continuously rather than hitting a hard ceiling. There is no single
"optimal" set count that holds across people. [synthesis] The practical
plateau for most trainees sits somewhere in the 10-20 hard direct sets
per muscle per week band, with the upper end justified only when
recovery and adherence allow.

Higher-volume probes worth noting:

- Enes et al. (2024), "Effects of Different Weekly Set Progressions on
  Muscular Adaptations in Trained Males: Is There a Dose-Response
  Effect?", *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise* 56(3):553-563.
  Trained men, lower body, 8 weeks; increasing weekly sets by 30% and
  60% over baseline produced more growth than maintaining baseline,
  evidence that adding volume to an already-trained population still
  pays, at least short term.
- Aube et al. and related work pushing 30+ sets per muscle have shown
  that very high volumes can still grow muscle without impairing it over
  short blocks, but the per-set return is small and recovery cost rises.
  [synthesis] These are not a mandate for everyone to train at 30+ sets;
  they are evidence the upper bound is higher than once assumed.

### Comparison to elite coaching practice

Renaissance Periodization (Mike Israetel and colleagues) codify this as
volume landmarks: MV (maintenance), MEV (minimum effective volume), MAV
(maximum adaptive volume) and MRV (maximum recoverable volume). Typical
published RP guidance lands around MEV near 8-10 sets and MAV/MRV near
the high teens to low 20s per muscle per week for intermediate-advanced
lifters, adjusted per muscle and per individual. Eric Helms and the
*MASS Research Review* group, Greg Nuckols / Stronger By Science, and
Menno Henselmans converge on a similar working range: roughly 10-20 hard
sets per muscle per week for most people, start lower, add only as
needed.

Agreement: the 10-20 set working range is shared ground between the
meta-analytic literature and elite practice. Divergence: RP's MEV-MAV-MRV
treats these as person-specific, fortnight-to-fortnight moving targets,
which is more granular than any single study can validate (see Section 6).

## 2. Frequency

### Volume-equated frequency literature

Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019), "How many times per week should a
muscle be trained to maximize muscle hypertrophy? A systematic review and
meta-analysis", *Journal of Sports Sciences* 37(11):1286-1295. The
headline: when weekly volume is equated, training frequency does not
meaningfully change hypertrophy. Non-volume-equated effect sizes rose
with frequency (about 0.74, 0.82, 0.93, 1.08 for 1, 2, 3 and 4+ sessions
per week), but that gradient is largely a proxy for the extra volume that
higher frequency lets you accumulate, not an independent frequency
effect. The earlier Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2016) frequency
meta-analysis reached the same conclusion that two sessions per muscle
beat one, with the caveat that volume was not always equated.

Pelland et al. (2025) reinforce this: once volume is accounted for, the
independent contribution of frequency is small. Frequency's real job is
to be the lever that lets you fit more weekly volume in at a tolerable
per-session quality.

Volume-equated single trials agree, e.g. the *Frontiers in Physiology*
(2021) equal-volume different-frequency trial in trained participants,
which found similar hypertrophy and strength across frequencies when
volume was held constant.

### Does optimal frequency differ by muscle group?

Direct head-to-head per-muscle frequency trials are thin, so this is
mostly mechanistic and practical reasoning. [synthesis] Larger muscles
trained with heavy compounds (e.g. quads, back) carry more per-session
fatigue and recover more slowly, so spreading their volume across more
sessions tends to preserve set quality. Smaller muscles (biceps,
triceps, side delts, calves) recover faster and tolerate being hit more
often. The literature does not establish a different *optimal* frequency
per muscle; it supports frequency as a tool to keep per-session volume
inside a quality range. The practical upshot coaches use is 2-4x per
muscle per week, chosen to distribute the target weekly volume.

## 3. Load and rep ranges, and proximity to failure

### Load and rep range

Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn and Krieger (2017), "Strength and Hypertrophy
Adaptations Between Low- vs. High-Load Resistance Training: A Systematic
Review and Meta-analysis", *Journal of Strength and Conditioning
Research* 31(12):3508-3523. Hypertrophy was similar between low load
(<60% 1RM) and high load (>60% 1RM), a trivial effect-size difference of
about 0.03 with narrow confidence intervals. Strength, by contrast,
favoured heavy loads clearly. The earlier low- vs high-load fibre-level
meta-analysis (Schoenfeld et al.) reached the same whole-muscle
conclusion.

Mechanism, per Morton et al.: when sets are taken close to failure, motor
unit recruitment becomes near-maximal regardless of load, which is why a
wide rep range grows muscle similarly. The practical band most coaches
and the literature endorse is roughly 5-30 reps per set, with the bulk of
work in a 6-15 "comfortable and loadable" range. Very low reps are
strength-efficient but volume-expensive for hypertrophy; very high reps
are uncomfortable and recruit fully only deep into fatigue.

### Proximity to failure

Two key sources:

- Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele et al. (2024),
  "Exploring the Dose-Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance
  Training Proximity to Failure, Strength Gain, and Muscle Hypertrophy: A
  Series of Meta-Regressions", *Sports Medicine* (2024;
  doi 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2). Hypertrophy increases as sets are
  taken closer to failure, a genuine dose-response on proximity. Strength
  shows negligible sensitivity to proximity.
- Refalo et al. (2023), "Influence of Resistance Training
  Proximity-to-Failure on Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy: A Systematic
  Review with Meta-analysis", *Sports Medicine*. Training to momentary
  failure carried only a trivial advantage over stopping short for
  hypertrophy, and the cost in fatigue and recovery is real. Refalo et
  al. (2024) in *Journal of Sports Sciences* (the 8-week RIR vs failure
  trial in trained individuals) found similar quadriceps hypertrophy
  whether sets ended at failure or with reps in reserve.

Synthesis of the two: the relationship is real but flattens close to
failure. Most growth is available in roughly the last 0-3 reps in reserve
(RIR). Helms et al. (2016) framed RIR-based autoregulation as the
practical tool: train most sets at about 1-3 RIR, reserve true failure
for occasional isolation work, and avoid routine failure on heavy
compounds where it inflates fatigue without adding growth. [synthesis]
For an app, "stop 0-2 reps short on most sets" captures the evidence
without prescribing the higher-fatigue, lower-return alternative of
training to failure every set.

## 4. Progressive overload methods

The cleanest direct test is Plotkin, Coleman, Van Every et al. (2022),
"Progressive overload without progressing load? The effects of load or
repetition progression on muscular adaptations", *PeerJ* 10:e14142.
Forty-three trained participants, 8 weeks: one group added load (reps
held), one added reps (load held). Hypertrophy was effectively
equivalent between methods. Load progression had a small edge for maximal
strength; rep progression matched it for hypertrophy and endurance.

Reading across the volume literature (Schoenfeld 2017; Pelland 2025;
Bernardez-Vazquez et al. 2022 umbrella review): progression works because
it accumulates effective volume at a sufficient proximity to failure over
time. The *mechanism* of progression (more weight vs more reps vs more
sets) matters less than the *fact* of progression.

Best-supported practical model [synthesis], consistent with Stronger By
Science (Greg Nuckols) and Helms:

- Double progression as the default: pick a rep range (e.g. 8-12), add
  reps within the range each session, and when you hit the top of the
  range across all sets, add load and drop back to the bottom. This
  self-regulates and needs no 1RM testing.
- Load progression for heavy compounds where strength is also a goal,
  consistent with Plotkin's small strength edge for adding load.
- Set/volume progression across a block (adding sets over a mesocycle) is
  the lever for the trainee who has stalled on load and reps and still
  has recovery headroom. This is the RP MEV-to-MAV ramp in practice. It
  should be capped by recovery, not run open-ended.

## 5. Exercise variation and selection

### Compound vs isolation

When sets, reps and proximity to failure are matched, total hypertrophy
of a given muscle is similar whether trained with compounds or isolation.
The difference shows up regionally, not in totals.

### Regional and subregion hypertrophy

Muscle growth is non-uniform along and across a muscle, and exercise
selection biases which regions grow:

- Quadriceps: heavy compound squatting grows the vasti well but spares
  the rectus femoris; leg extension work produces region-specific growth
  that differs between heads and along the length of each head.
- Biceps and elbow flexors: studies report differential proximal vs
  distal growth depending on exercise (e.g. distal regions responding
  more to some movements than others).

Implication, well-supported: to develop a muscle fully you want a small
set of biomechanically distinct exercises that bias different regions and
different lengths, not one exercise taken to extreme volume.

### Long-length partials and muscle length

The fastest-moving area in 2024-2025. Wolf, Schoenfeld and colleagues'
systematic reviews on range of motion and muscle length conclude that
training at longer muscle lengths is favourable for hypertrophy, and that
lengthened partials (partial reps in the stretched portion) can match or
exceed full range of motion in several trials. Of four studies comparing
lengthened partials to full ROM, three reported greater growth with
lengthened partials and one found parity (see also the PeerJ 2025 trial,
"Lengthened partial repetitions elicit similar muscular adaptations as
full range of motion in trained individuals", and the SportRxiv 2024
regional-hypertrophy-and-muscle-length meta-analysis). The lengthening
(eccentric/stretched) portion is highly stimulatory.

Practical reading [synthesis]: prioritise full ROM with emphasis on the
lengthened position, and use lengthened partials as a productive
intensity technique rather than a replacement for full-range work. The
evidence supports loading the stretch; it does not support abandoning
full ROM.

## 6. Recovery and MRV

### Determinants of recovery capacity

Recovery is governed by the interaction of training stress (volume,
proximity to failure, eccentric/stretch load, exercise novelty) with
individual capacity (training age, sleep, nutrition and protein intake,
psychological stress, age). The Enes et al. (2024) and high-volume probes
show trained individuals can absorb large volume increases without
impairment over short blocks, but tolerance is individual and finite.

### MRV and the volume landmarks

The RP MEV-MAV-MRV framework (Israetel et al.) is the dominant coaching
model: train above MEV to grow, push towards MAV/MRV for maximal growth,
and deload before chronic accumulation past MRV degrades performance and
recovery. The framework is mechanistically reasonable and operationally
useful, but its specific per-muscle numbers are practitioner-derived
heuristics, not values any single study has validated. The supporting
evidence is the volume dose-response (Schoenfeld 2017; Pelland 2025) plus
fatigue and overreaching literature, not a direct MRV trial. State this
plainly: MRV is a useful planning construct with indirect empirical
support, not a measured constant.

### Adjusting volume when specialisation is added

[synthesis] When a muscle is specialised (volume raised towards its upper
band), total systemic and local recovery is finite, so volume on other
muscles should drop towards maintenance (MV) for that block. This follows
directly from the recovery being shared and finite; it is standard RP
practice and is consistent with the dose-response showing maintenance
needs far less volume than growth.

## 7. Individual variation

Hubal et al. (2005), "Variability in muscle size and strength gain after
unilateral resistance training", *Medicine & Science in Sports &
Exercise* 37(6):964-972. The seminal demonstration that the hypertrophic
response to an identical programme ranges from near-zero to very large
across people. Mechanistic follow-up (the *Frontiers in Physiology* 2018
review on high vs low responders) points to ribosome biogenesis,
satellite cell proliferation and protein-synthetic response as drivers of
the spread.

Variables that most affect optimal prescription between individuals,
ranked by the weight the literature gives them [synthesis]:

1. Training age. The largest single moderator. Novices grow on low
   volume and almost any progression; trained individuals need more
   volume and tighter proximity to failure for the same relative gain.
2. Recovery inputs: sleep, nutrition/protein, and life stress. These set
   how much volume a person can convert to growth rather than dig a
   hole. Poorly recovered trainees have a lower effective MRV.
3. Muscle-specific response. Within one person, some muscles respond and
   recover better than others; this is why per-muscle volume tuning
   beats one global number. Higher volume can partly offset
   non-responsiveness (e.g. the 2023 *Journal of Applied Physiology*
   finding that higher volume offsets hypertrophy non-response in older
   adults).
4. Genetics. Real and large (Hubal), but not directly actionable beyond
   adjusting volume to observed response.
5. Sex. Modest effect on hypertrophy programming; relative gains are
   broadly similar, with differences more in absolute size, fatigue and
   recovery patterns than in the structure of an optimal programme.

The practical consequence: optimal prescription is found by starting at
an evidence-based default and adjusting to the individual's measured
response, not by computing a single "correct" number up front.

## 8. Where elite practice and the science agree and diverge

### Agreement

- 10-20 hard sets per muscle per week is a sound default working range
  (Schoenfeld 2017; Pelland 2025; RP; Helms; Nuckols).
- Frequency is mostly a vehicle for volume; 2x+ per muscle per week is
  the sensible default, not because frequency itself is magic but because
  it keeps per-session volume in a quality range (Schoenfeld Grgic
  Krieger 2019).
- A wide rep range (about 5-30, bulk 6-15) all builds muscle when sets
  are near failure (Schoenfeld 2017).
- Most sets should end close to but not at failure, roughly 0-3 RIR
  (Robinson 2024; Refalo 2023/2024; Helms 2016).
- Progression should happen; the exact method is interchangeable (Plotkin
  2022).
- Train through full ROM with emphasis on the lengthened position (Wolf
  and Schoenfeld 2024-2025).

### Divergence

- Precision of volume landmarks. RP prescribes specific MEV/MAV/MRV
  numbers per muscle and adjusts them weekly. The literature supports the
  shape of the dose-response, not the precise per-muscle constants. Elite
  practice is more confident and more granular than the data strictly
  licenses.
- Where the volume plateau sits. Some coaching encourages pushing well
  into the 20s-30s of sets; the meta-analytic per-set return there is
  small and the recovery cost is real and individual. The science says
  "diminishing, individual", not "more is reliably better".
- Failure. Some coaching still leans on routine failure. The proximity
  literature says the last gains near failure are small and the fatigue
  cost is disproportionate, so routine failure on compounds is a poor
  trade.

### Recommendation for an evidence-led app

When elite practice and the literature conflict, the app should default
to the meta-analytic consensus and treat coaching heuristics as starting
points to be confirmed against the individual's own data. [synthesis]
Concretely:

1. Seed each muscle's weekly volume at an evidence-based default (start
   nearer 10 sets for less-trained users, scale towards the high teens as
   training age and recovery allow). Do not present a precise MRV as if
   it were measured.
2. Set proximity-to-failure targets at 1-3 RIR by default, reserving
   failure for optional isolation work.
3. Drive progression by double progression as the default, load
   progression on the main compounds, and add sets across a block only
   while the user is still recovering and progressing.
4. Prescribe full ROM with a lengthened bias, and rotate a small set of
   biomechanically distinct exercises per muscle to cover regions rather
   than maximise the volume of one movement.
5. When a muscle is specialised, automatically pull other muscles towards
   maintenance volume for that block.
6. Adjust volume to observed response over time rather than committing to
   a fixed prescription, because training age and recovery inputs, not a
   universal optimum, are what move the right answer between people.
7. Be explicit in the product's reasoning that volume returns diminish
   and that "more" is not reliably "better". Do not gamify volume upward.

The single rule: prescribe the consensus default, measure the response,
and adjust the individual rather than the textbook.
