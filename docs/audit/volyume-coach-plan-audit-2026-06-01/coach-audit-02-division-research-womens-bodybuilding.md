# Coach Plan Audit 02: Division Research, Women's Bodybuilding

Status: COMPLETE | Timestamp: 2026-06-01 | Division: Women's Bodybuilding

Scope note. This document treats Women's Bodybuilding as its own division:
maximum female muscular development plus contest conditioning, the direct
female analogue of Open Men's Bodybuilding. It is not Women's Physique,
Figure, Wellness, or Bikini. Where a source speaks to those adjacent
divisions it is flagged, because their coaching logic does not transfer
cleanly. Every coaching claim below is attributed to a named coach or
named athlete. Every science claim is attributed to a named study or
author. Numbers that are documented are cited; numbers that are reasoned
from documented material are tagged [synthesis].

---

## 1. The aesthetic standard and how it is judged today

Women's Bodybuilding is scored on the same three pillars the IFBB Pro
League applies to Open Men: muscular development, symmetry and
proportion, and conditioning. Reporting on the division describes the
emphasis as "extreme muscle development, muscle separation, and overall
conditioning", judged on "muscle mass, symmetry, proportion, and overall
conditioning, with less emphasis on femininity compared to other
divisions" (Bodybuilding Wizard division breakdown; CenterPodium
division guide). This is the defining contrast with Women's Physique,
which is explicitly capped on muscularity and asks for a softer, less
dense look.

What the judges reward:

- Muscularity: size, fullness and density across the whole physique, not
  one or two strong parts. This is the primary criterion (Bodybuilding
  Wizard; BarBend, "How Bodybuilding is Judged").
- Symmetry and proportion: balanced left-to-right development and a
  balanced top-to-bottom physique, with no part visibly outrunning or
  lagging the rest (Bodybuilding Wizard).
- Conditioning: low body fat revealing striations, muscle separation
  and vascularity. Conditioning is what separates closely matched
  physiques on the day (Bodybuilding Wizard; 12WeeksOut, NPC judging
  criteria).

Posing carries the score. The mandatory round drives placings: front
double biceps, side chest, rear lat spread, rear double biceps, and the
abdominal and thigh shots. The rear lat spread and rear double biceps in
particular expose back width and thickness, glute-hamstring tie-in,
hamstring separation and calf, so weakness there is hard to hide
(BarBend, "Bodybuilding Poses"; Natural Bodybuilding Coach, judging and
poses).

What gets marked down:

- Flat or "spilling" conditioning. Coming in soft (smooth, no
  separation) loses to a smaller athlete who is harder. Over-dieting
  that leaves the athlete stringy and flat also loses, because the
  muscularity pillar collapses. The win sits at the point where size is
  held and conditioning is full. [synthesis, from the three-pillar
  weighting in Bodybuilding Wizard and 12WeeksOut]
- Imbalance. A blocky waist, a weak rear, or a dominant upper body over
  thin legs reads against symmetry and proportion (Bodybuilding Wizard).
- Poor posing and transitions. Because the score is built in the
  mandatory round, a physique that cannot hit and hold the shots gives
  placings away (BarBend, poses).

The current bar for "what wins" is set by Andrea Shaw. She took her sixth
straight Ms. Olympia in 2025, described in prejudging reporting as
challenged by Ashley Lynette Jones and Angela Yeo but holding the title
on the combination of size, shape and conditioning (Generation Iron,
2025 Ms. Olympia results and prejudging analysis).

## 2. Top coaches in the world for Women's Bodybuilding

Naming caveat. Women's Bodybuilding is a small division and several of
its top athletes self-coach or use a partner plus a team rather than a
single celebrity coach. The list below names the people most directly
documented in current Women's Bodybuilding outcomes, then the
crossover coaches whose methods most shape the division.

1. John Simmons. Coach to reigning six-time Ms. Olympia Andrea Shaw, and
   her training partner. Shaw trains at Powerhouse Gym in Highland Park,
   Michigan, and her programme is built around heavy compound work
   (deadlifts, presses) plus isolation for separation, training that has
   moved from three days a week early on to near-daily (Generation Iron,
   Andrea Shaw profile; Steel Supplements, Shaw routine). Notable
   athlete: Andrea Shaw, the defining physique of the current era.

2. George Farah. Long-standing contest-prep coach known across the IFBB
   for a structured carbohydrate-down, protein-up taper over roughly 16
   weeks, with cardio rising through prep (Muscle Armor / Scribd, "Pre
   Contest Prep with IFBB Pro George Farah"). His prep template is one of
   the most widely circulated documented female-applicable prep
   frameworks. Notable: coached multiple IFBB pros across women's
   divisions and Open Men.

3. Kim Oddo (Body By O, Temecula CA). The most-cited female-physique prep
   coach of the modern era, founder of Body By O, a former competitor
   turned nutritionist with a decades-long client roster across women's
   divisions. His stated philosophy is lean, full musculature with high
   conditioning achieved through high-intensity training plus heavy
   cardio focus, and a hard line on building enough off-season size
   before prep starts (Body By O; Bodybuilding.com Oddo interview). Note:
   much of Oddo's volume is Figure and Women's Physique, so his
   "femininity" framing is division-specific and should not be read as
   the Women's Bodybuilding ceiling.

4. Charles Glass ("Godfather of Bodybuilding"). 35-plus years training
   IFBB champions including Flex Wheeler, Kai Greene, Shawn Rhoden,
   Dexter Jackson, and on the women's side Angelica Teixeira and Courtney
   King (Godfather of Bodybuilding site; Fitness Volt, Glass workout).
   His method is biomechanical: "make the muscle move the weight" rather
   than move weight A to B, full range over heavy load, and grip and
   angle tweaks to bias weak heads (his reverse-grip rear-delt cue, his
   wide-back work) (BarBend, Glass wider back; Fitness Volt, rear delt
   tip). His weak-point correction work is the most documented in the
   sport and transfers directly to female back, delt and detail work.

5. Hany Rambod (FST-7 / Evogen). Creator of Fascial Stretch Training
   (FST-7): seven high-rep sets on the final movement for a body part to
   drive pump and fascial stretch, across a six-day split hitting 2-3
   body parts per session, with two-a-days deployed selectively in prep.
   Rambod states FST-7 programmes apply to men and women alike
   (hanyrambod.com; Evogen, "Two A Days For Contest Prep"; Muscle and
   Brawn FST-7 breakdown). His system is the most influential volume-and-
   pump framework adopted by female competitors building detail.

Historically significant, still shaping the division:

- Iris Kyle, ten-time Ms. Olympia and the most decorated bodybuilder of
  either sex, now coaches upcoming athletes through her own company
  (Fitness Volt, Helle Trevino interview; Helle Trevino site). Her
  competitive standard still defines "maximum development".
- Helle Trevino, two-time Rising Phoenix world champion, has coached
  online since 2003 and currently works with coach David DeMesquita and
  daily trainer "Gina" on her own prep, a documented example of the
  team-not-guru model in this division (Helle Trevino, About; Fitness
  Volt interview).
- Lenda Murray and the Wings of Strength organisation (which runs the
  Rising Phoenix / Ms. Olympia ecosystem) anchor the division's
  institutional memory and athlete pipeline (Wings of Strength athlete
  profiles).

## 3. Programming philosophy: mass phase vs contest prep

The cross-cutting documented evidence on how competitive physique
athletes actually programme comes from da Silveira et al. (2025),
"Quantification of weekly strength-training volume per muscle group in
competitive physique athletes", Frontiers in Sports and Active Living,
an online cross-sectional survey of 154 competitive physique athletes
covering both off-season and pre-contest training across ten muscle
groups. Its headline finding: most categories reduce training volume in
pre-contest for most muscle groups, with the change in weekly sets and
weekly sessions differing by muscle group and by division (da Silveira
et al., 2025, Frontiers SPOR 10.3389/fspor.2025.1536360).

Mass / off-season phase (building maximum muscle):

- Volume. Highest of the year. The off-season is where the bulk of
  weekly sets sit; prep then trims (da Silveira et al., 2025).
- Frequency. Most documented top approaches run each major muscle group
  roughly twice a week, or a body-part split that touches everything
  across a six-day week. Rambod's FST-7 split hits 2-3 body parts per
  session over six days (Muscle and Brawn FST-7). Shaw has moved to
  near-daily training (Generation Iron, Shaw profile).
- Exercise selection. Compound-led. Shaw's programme is built on
  deadlifts and presses with isolation layered on for separation (Steel
  Supplements). Glass adds biomechanically chosen isolation to bias weak
  heads (BarBend / Fitness Volt, Glass).
- Load and progression. Progressive overload on the compounds, with
  Glass's caveat that controlled full-range tension beats chasing load
  (BarBend, Glass wider back). Rep work spans heavy compounds down to
  the 8-10 rep FST-7 finishers (Muscle and Brawn).

Contest prep phase (revealing the physique):

- Volume. Trimmed from off-season per the survey, the resistance work
  protecting muscle while the deficit and cardio strip fat (da Silveira
  et al., 2025).
- Energy and cardio. Farah's documented template steps carbohydrate down
  and protein up over roughly 16 weeks while cardio rises in duration and
  frequency (Muscle Armor / Scribd, Farah prep). Shaw eats five to six
  small meals, 30-50 g protein per meal, carbs around training
  (Generation Iron, Shaw diet).
- Goal shift. Off-season adds tissue, prep keeps tissue while cutting
  fat. Oddo's stated rule is to confirm enough size was added before prep
  begins, because prep does not build (Bodybuilding.com Oddo interview).

The principle every named coach shares: build in the off-season, reveal
in prep, do not try to do both at once.

## 4. Muscle-group priority ranking and why

Ranked for the Women's Bodybuilding score, driven by what the mandatory
poses expose and where the division is most often won or lost. Ordering
is [synthesis] from the judging pillars in section 1 and the posing
literature; the underlying facts are cited.

1. Back (width and thickness). The rear lat spread and rear double
   biceps are where size and conditioning are read most harshly, and
   back is the hardest area to fake. Glass's documented specialism is
   building a wide back through full-range work over heavy load (BarBend,
   Glass wider back). Top priority.
2. Glute-hamstring tie-in and hamstrings. Judges of female physique and
   bodybuilding shows specifically look for the glute-ham tie-in, and the
   rear shots expose hamstring separation and glute development (T
   Nation, female glute training; BarBend, poses). A weak rear sinks the
   rear-facing mandatories.
3. Quads (sweep and separation). Front and rear thigh shots demand sweep
   plus deep separation. Da Silveira et al. (2025) found quadriceps among
   the muscle groups with significant volume differences across divisions
   and phases, reflecting how much leg work the look needs.
4. Shoulders (the V-taper anchor). Capped, round delts widen the frame
   and improve the symmetry score from the front. Glass and Rambod both
   put dedicated detail work into delt heads (Fitness Volt, Glass rear
   delt; Muscle and Brawn FST-7).
5. Arms and chest (separation and detail). Front double biceps and side
   chest reward separation and density. Da Silveira et al. (2025) flag
   pectorals, deltoids and triceps surae as muscle groups with notable
   programmed-volume differences across divisions and phases.
6. Calves and abdominals. Detail and conditioning markers in the lower-
   body and ab-thigh shots, often weak points by genetics rather than
   effort (da Silveira et al., 2025, on triceps surae and abdominal
   volume differences).

Why this order: the division is judged on whole-body development plus
conditioning, and the rear-facing mandatories carry weight that the
front shots do not, so the posterior chain and back are prioritised
ahead of the more visible "mirror" muscles. [synthesis, anchored to
BarBend poses and the three-pillar judging in section 1]

## 5. Weekly volume per muscle group

Documented anchor. Da Silveira et al. (2025) is the only large
real-world quantification of per-muscle weekly sets in competitive
physique athletes. Its documented structural findings: off-season volume
is the high point, most muscle groups see a pre-contest reduction, and
weekly sets and weekly sessions both vary by muscle group and by
division, with significant between-category differences for pectorals,
deltoids, triceps surae, quadriceps and abdominals (da Silveira et al.,
2025, Frontiers SPOR). The full-text per-muscle mean set counts sit
behind a host that blocked automated retrieval on 2026-06-01, so the
specific integer means are not reproduced here to avoid presenting an
unverified number as documented.

General research context for women specifically. Reporting on women's
volume work (BarBend, "How Much Training Volume Do Women Need to Get
Stronger?") summarises a 24-week study in 40 resistance-trained women
where groups doing five and ten weekly sets per muscle gained more
strength and muscle than groups doing fifteen and twenty sets, the
20-set group improving least. This argues against assuming higher
female volume is automatically better, and supports a moderate
landing zone.

Practical landing ranges for Women's Bodybuilding, given the priority
ranking in section 4. These are [synthesis], reasoned from the
moderate-volume women's data above plus the off-season-high /
prep-reduced structure documented by da Silveira et al. (2025). They are
not lifted from a single study.

- Back: off-season ~14-20 sets/wk, prep ~12-16. [synthesis]
- Glutes + hamstrings (combined posterior): off-season ~12-18, prep
  ~10-14. [synthesis]
- Quads: off-season ~12-18, prep ~10-14. [synthesis]
- Shoulders (all heads): off-season ~12-18, prep ~10-14. [synthesis]
- Chest: off-season ~10-14, prep ~8-12. [synthesis]
- Biceps / triceps each: off-season ~8-12, prep ~6-10. [synthesis]
- Calves: off-season ~10-16, prep ~8-12. [synthesis]
- Abdominals: off-season ~6-12, prep ~6-12 (held or raised for
  conditioning detail). [synthesis]

The off-season-to-prep direction of travel (a trim, not a collapse) is
the documented part. The integers are inference and tagged as such.

## 6. Exercise selection philosophy

Prioritised:

- Compound multi-joint movements as the base. Shaw's programme is built
  on deadlifts and presses for whole-physique mass (Steel Supplements,
  Shaw routine). These drive the size pillar.
- Full range of motion over maximal load. Glass's documented rule:
  "make the muscle move the weight", full range matters more than moving
  massive loads, especially for back width (BarBend, Glass wider back).
- Angle and grip variation to bias weak heads. Glass's reverse-grip,
  elbows-high cue to recruit rear delt, rear trap and mid-trap is a
  documented example of selecting a variation specifically to fix a weak
  point (Fitness Volt, Glass rear delt tip).
- High-rep pump finishers for detail and fullness. Rambod's FST-7
  seven-set finisher on the last movement for a body part is the
  canonical detail tool, used on both sexes (Muscle and Brawn FST-7;
  hanyrambod.com).
- Posterior-chain and rear-delt isolation, because the rear-facing
  mandatories carry the score (section 1; BarBend poses). [synthesis
  link to selection]

Avoided or de-prioritised:

- Loading patterns that build the waist or blunt the V-taper. Heavy
  loaded oblique and direct waist-thickening work is generally limited
  because a thick waist costs the proportion score (proportion pillar,
  section 1). [synthesis]
- Ego-load training at the expense of range and target-muscle tension,
  per Glass's stated philosophy (BarBend, Glass).
- Trying to add mass in prep. Oddo's documented position is that size is
  an off-season job and prep preserves rather than builds
  (Bodybuilding.com Oddo interview).

## 7. Division-specific practices and female-physiology considerations

Practices that differ from general hypertrophy training:

- Posing as a trained, fatiguing skill, not an afterthought. Because the
  mandatory round carries the placing, peak-week and prep include
  significant posing practice to hold the shots under fatigue (BarBend,
  poses; Natural Bodybuilding Coach, judging).
- Conditioning is pushed to a competitive extreme the general lifter
  never approaches, which is exactly what creates the physiology problem
  below.

Female physiology at this level (cited):

- Menstrual dysfunction is common during prep and is a direct cost of
  the energy deficit. The Cureus 2025 study "Menstrual Health in Female
  Bodybuilders During Competition Preparation" (reviewed July 2025,
  published 7 August 2025) reports 85.7% of competitors had regular
  periods before prep but 57.1% experienced amenorrhoea for at least one
  cycle during prep. A separate 2024 cohort found cycle disorders in
  58.65% of women, with three-month-plus amenorrhoea in 43.27% before
  the study and 15.38% during it.
- The mechanism is low energy availability. Loucks' threshold of 30
  kcal/kg fat-free mass per day is the reference, and in the figure-
  competitor case study and the LEAF-Q physique-athlete work, energy
  availability falls below 30 kcal/kg FFM at the start of prep and
  stays there for the duration (PMC11161107, LEAF-Q menstrual cycle
  disorders in female physique athletes; PMC5133123, drug-free figure
  competitor case study). Menstrual cycle disorder shows up as an early
  warning sign of the energy deficit, ahead of other markers
  (PMC11161107).
- Body-composition measurement is robust to cycle phase. A case-control
  study found only minor intra-individual body-composition variation
  across the menstrual cycle, with no significant fat-mass or fat-free-
  mass change and no difference between women with and without hormonal
  contraception (PMC12009753). Practical read: cycle phase does not
  meaningfully corrupt a DEXA or scale reading, so day-to-day weight
  noise should not trigger panic cuts.
- Luteal-phase effects on intake and expenditure. During the luteal
  phase, energy intake and expenditure rise and carbohydrate/fat
  cravings increase relative to the follicular phase (Nature, IJO,
  "Impact of the menstrual cycle on determinants of energy balance"),
  which is why some coaches phase diet pressure to the cycle. A 6-month
  RCT in overweight premenopausal women found a menstrual-cycle-adapted
  weight-loss programme increased weight loss versus standard
  (ScienceDirect, S000291652204504X). This is non-athlete evidence and
  should be read as directional, not as a validated bodybuilding
  protocol. [synthesis on the transfer]

The honest operational point: at this division's conditioning,
amenorrhoea is the statistical norm in prep, not an anomaly. A good
coach plans for it, monitors energy availability against the 30 kcal/kg
FFM line, and builds a recovery runway, rather than treating a lost cycle
as a sign the diet is working.

## 8. Common weak points and how the best coaches address them

- Back width and detail. The single most exposed area in the rear
  mandatories. Glass's documented fix: full-range pulling over heavy
  load, plus grip and angle variation to recruit the target (BarBend,
  Glass wider back). Rambod adds FST-7 finishers for thickness and
  fullness (Muscle and Brawn).
- Rear delts. Glass's documented reverse-grip, elbows-high cue keeps
  tension on the rear delt, rear trap and mid-trap rather than letting
  bigger pressing muscles take over (Fitness Volt, Glass rear delt tip).
- Glute-hamstring tie-in. Judges read this directly in the rear shots
  (T Nation, female glute training). Addressed with posterior-chain
  priority and dedicated hamstring/glute work early in the session when
  fresh. [synthesis from section 4 priority logic]
- Conditioning vs fullness balance. The classic failure is arriving
  flat. Farah's gradual carb-down, protein-up taper with managed cardio
  is the documented method for stripping fat while protecting size
  (Muscle Armor / Scribd, Farah). Oddo's rule that prep preserves and
  does not build is the guard against over-dieting (Bodybuilding.com).
- Posing under fatigue. Fixed by treating posing as trained conditioning
  work through prep (BarBend, poses).
- Symmetry / waist control. Managed by selection (limiting waist-
  thickening loaded work) rather than by spot reduction (proportion
  pillar, section 1). [synthesis]

## 9. Recovery management for this division

- Energy availability is the primary recovery lever, not a secondary
  one. Because prep runs below 30 kcal/kg FFM for weeks (PMC11161107;
  PMC5133123), recovery capacity, sleep and connective-tissue repair are
  all compromised in late prep. Coaches manage this by trimming, not
  collapsing, resistance volume into prep (da Silveira et al., 2025) so
  the reduced recovery budget is spent on muscle-preserving work.
- Menstrual status as a recovery readout. Given amenorrhoea rates of
  ~57% in prep (Cureus 2025) and cycle disorders as the earliest
  deficit marker (PMC11161107), tracking cycle status doubles as an
  early-warning system for over-reaching, ahead of strength loss or mood
  change.
- Post-show refeed and cycle recovery. The figure-competitor case study
  documents body-composition and menstrual-cycle changes through both
  prep and recovery, underlining that menses often returns only after a
  deliberate post-show calorie and body-fat rebound (PMC5133123).
  Recovery is a planned phase, not an automatic snap-back.
- Volume periodisation as recovery insurance. The off-season-high,
  prep-reduced volume structure (da Silveira et al., 2025) is itself a
  recovery strategy: peak training stress is placed when the athlete is
  fed and can recover, and pulled back when the deficit removes recovery
  capacity.
- Body-composition tracking can ignore cycle-phase noise. Since cycle
  phase does not materially move fat-free or fat mass readings
  (PMC12009753), coaches can trust trend data and avoid reactive cuts on
  cycle-driven weight swings, which protects against unnecessary deficit
  deepening.
