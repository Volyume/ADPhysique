# Coach Audit 02: Division Research, Open Men's Bodybuilding

Status: COMPLETE | Timestamp: 2026-06-01 | Division: Open Men's Bodybuilding

## Scope and method

Live web research conducted June 2026. Coaching claims are tied to a
named coach or a published method. Science claims cite a named
researcher or paper. Documented figures carry a source. Inferred or
training-floor figures are tagged [synthesis]. British English
throughout. Source URLs are listed at the end.

A note on the field as it stands in 2026: Open is the heavyweight,
no-upper-limit class. It is judged on the most muscle a man can carry
in contest condition. It is not Classic Physique and it is not 212.
The aesthetic ceiling is higher and the margin for a soft midsection or
a flat back is smaller. This document treats that on its own terms.

---

## 1. Definitive aesthetic standard and current judging

The IFBB Pro Open Men's class is scored on the "total package": muscle
size, symmetry, proportion, conditioning and stage presence, assessed
across eight mandatory poses. Seven judges score each man, the highest
and lowest scores are dropped, and the rest are averaged. Judges look
for full, round muscle bellies (mass), deep striations, vascularity and
clear separation between muscle groups (conditioning), and balance
left-to-right and upper-to-lower (symmetry and proportion). Source:
Fitness Volt judging explainer, BarBend "How Bodybuilding Is Judged",
IFBB Pro rules.

What wins: the man who is the biggest while still holding hard
conditioning and a balanced shape. Size alone does not win. Condition
alone does not win. The title goes to whoever loses the fewest points
across all three axes at once.

What gets marked down:
- Loss of fullness when a competitor over-diets. The clearest 2026
  case study is Samson Dauda at the 2025 Olympia. Judges had pushed him
  for years to come in sharper. He did, pushed conditioning harder than
  ever, and dropped from champion to fourth because he came in flat and
  underestimated how long he needed to fill back out. Lee Priest stated
  publicly that the harder condition made his muscles stop popping.
  Source: Fitness Volt (Dauda statement and Lee Priest analysis).
- A blocky or distended waist, which ruins the V-taper and the
  symmetry score.
- Weak posterior chain. Roughly half the mandatory poses are rear
  shots (rear double biceps, rear lat spread), so a thin back, flat
  rear delts, stringy hamstrings or small calves are exposed directly.
  Source: BarBend weak-points piece, StrengthLog mandatory-poses guide.

The 2025 Olympia result is the reference point for 2026 prep thinking:
Derek Lunsford reclaimed the title (first to win the Open back after
losing it since Jay Cutler in 2009) by being "drier, crisper, sharper"
with striated delts and a tight midsection, while still holding his
mass. That is the standard the field is now chasing. Source: Generation
Iron, BoxLife.

## 2. Top coaches in the world (named, credentialled)

1. **Hany Rambod ("The Pro Creator").** Creator of FST-7. The most
   decorated Open coach of the modern era, with multiple Mr. Olympia
   titles across athletes including Phil Heath, Jay Cutler and most
   recently Derek Lunsford. Announced retirement at the end of the 2024
   season. Source: alphabodybuilders profile, Derek Lunsford Wikipedia.

2. **George Farah ("The Champion Maker").** Prep and nutrition
   specialist. Took Kai Greene to the 2011 New York Pro win and to
   three runner-up Olympia finishes (2012-2014). Also worked with
   Dexter Jackson and Big Ramy. Known for carb-manipulation and
   depletion/refeed prep. Source: Generation Iron interviews.

3. **Chad Nicholls ("The Diet Doc").** Nutrition architect behind
   Ronnie Coleman's first Olympia win in 1998 and later Big Ramy. Also
   guided Flex Wheeler and Nasser El Sonbaty. His stated hierarchy is
   diet first, training second, drugs third, supplements last. Source:
   Sportskeeda, Fitness Volt.

4. **Matt Jansen.** Modern Open and prep coach who built a large
   competitive stable, has helped 12 athletes earn IFBB pro cards
   across divisions, and coached Nick Walker (on and off) and Iain
   Valliere. Note: he has drawn criticism from former clients over
   alleged one-size-fits-all programming and aggressive protocols, which
   is fair to flag in an audit. Source: Fitness Volt, fitnesscoachratings.

5. **Milos Sarcev.** Creator of the giant-set / "Hyperemia Advantage"
   high-volume method. Coached Dennis Wolf and Ben Pakulski. Source:
   Grokipedia profile, JYM Supplement Science breakdowns.

Beyond the named five, current and active names that matter in 2026:
- **Chris Aceto** and **Fabricio Pacholok**, the team Derek Lunsford
  moved to after Rambod retired, behind the 2025 Olympia win. Aceto is
  a long-standing nutrition coach (Jay Cutler era, author of
  "Championship Bodybuilding"). Source: Endomondo, Fitness Volt.
- **Neil Hill ("Yoda")**, creator of Y3T, behind seven-time 212
  Olympia champion Flex Lewis and Open athlete William Bonac. Source:
  BarBend Y3T explainer, The Barbell.
- **Justin Harris** (Troponin, 25+ years IFBB Pro coaching) and **Joe
  Bennett** (Hypertrophy Coach), both evidence-leaning programmers with
  Open and Olympia-level clients. Source: Troponin, J3 University.

## 3. Programming philosophy

The defining feature of Open programming is that it separates the
maximum-mass pursuit (off-season) from the conditioning/prep phase, and
the two phases look genuinely different.

### Off-season: maximum mass

The dominant Open methods are high-volume and pump-driven. Hany
Rambod's **FST-7** caps a body part's session with 7 sets of 8-12 reps
on an isolation move, 30-45 seconds rest between those sets, after the
heavier compound work. The stated rationale is to engorge the muscle
with blood and stretch the fascia that, in Rambod's model, limits how
big the belly can grow. Source: BarBend FST-7, Julien Quaglierini
summary, Jefit routine.

Milos Sarcev's **giant sets** take volume further: four or more
exercises for one muscle back to back with minimal rest, building over a
12-week cycle from lower to higher complexity, with the aim of maximum
blood flow and nutrient delivery into the worked muscle ("hyperemia").
Source: JYM Supplement Science, Grokipedia.

Neil Hill's **Y3T** rotates rep ranges over a three-week block: week 1
compound work at 6-10 reps, week 2 compound plus isolation at 10-15,
week 3 high-rep flush work at 20+ with more isolation. The cycle is
pitched at hitting different fibre types and giving connective tissue a
break from constant heavy loading. Source: The Barbell, BarBend,
Bodybuilding Warehouse.

Across these methods the off-season pattern is: train near failure,
chase a strong pump, accumulate high weekly volume, eat in a surplus,
and progress load and reps over time.

### Prep / conditioning phase

Prep is led by nutrition coaches, and the training shift is toward
holding muscle while stripping fat. George Farah's prep model drops
carbs and raises protein as the show nears, lengthens and intensifies
cardio, then runs carb depletion and refeed in the final weeks. Source:
Generation Iron, Farah prep PDF. Chris Aceto runs carb cycling across
the whole cycle rather than late-stage keto, and is associated with
very high carb intakes for big athletes (he describes Jay Cutler
prepping on around 1,000g carbs and very high calories). Source:
Fitness Volt. Chad Nicholls' through-line is that diet is the first
lever, training the second. Source: Sportskeeda.

The 2025 Dauda lesson sits here: prep conditioning has to be timed so
the athlete refills and looks full on the day, not just dry. Pushing
condition without protecting fullness loses shows. Source: Fitness
Volt.

## 4. Muscle-group priority ranking and why

Open is a full-body mass sport, so no group is optional. Priority comes
from where shows are won and lost, which is the back and the
proportional details. Ranking is for emphasis, not for ignoring
anything.

1. **Back (width and thickness).** Multiple rear mandatory poses make
   the back the single biggest scoring surface. Thickness and detail
   here separate the top placings.
2. **Legs (quads and especially hamstrings).** Quad sweep drives the
   front, hamstrings drive the rear double biceps and side shots.
   Hamstrings are a named common lag. Source: RitFit, BarBend.
3. **Rear delts.** A named common weak point. They complete the
   3D shoulder look and show in every rear and side pose. Source:
   BarBend, dmoose.
4. **Calves.** Notoriously hard to build, fully exposed in the rear
   double biceps, and a classic place to lose proportion against a big
   upper body. Source: RitFit.
5. **Abs and serratus / tight midsection.** Not just size, control.
   A distended or blocky waist costs symmetry points and breaks the
   taper, as the Dauda fullness-vs-waist trade-off showed. Source:
   Fitness Volt.
6. **Chest, arms, front delts, traps.** Still trained hard, but these
   are rarely the deciding weak points at the top because they respond
   more readily and are less brutally exposed from the rear.

The reason the lagging groups (rear delts, hamstrings, calves, abs)
rank high despite being small is that judges score the weakest link in
a back-dominated posing round, not the biggest body part.

## 5. Weekly volume per muscle group

Documented science floor: Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2017) found
10+ weekly sets per muscle group beat fewer than 5, with each added
weekly set associated with roughly 0.37% extra growth, and the broad
evidence-based sweet spot landing at about 10-20 hard sets per muscle
per week split across 2+ sessions. Frequency of 2+ sessions per muscle
per week beat once per week at matched volume (Schoenfeld et al. 2016).
Above ~20 sets returns flatten and overreaching risk rises. Source:
AskVora meta-analysis summary, PMC umbrella review, Semantic Scholar
frequency meta-analysis.

Open practice runs at or above the top of that science range, because
these are advanced, enhanced athletes whose recovery ceiling is far
higher than the trained-natural subjects in those studies. The
following per-muscle ranges are a [synthesis] from the named methods
(FST-7's compound work plus 7-set finisher, Sarcev giant sets, Y3T
high-rep weeks) mapped onto the research, not figures any one coach
publishes as a weekly total:

| Muscle group | Off-season weekly sets | Prep weekly sets | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back | 18-25 [synthesis] | 14-18 [synthesis] | Highest priority surface |
| Quads | 16-22 [synthesis] | 12-16 [synthesis] | High but recovery-capped |
| Hamstrings | 12-18 [synthesis] | 10-14 [synthesis] | Raised vs typical to fix lag |
| Chest | 14-20 [synthesis] | 10-14 [synthesis] | |
| Shoulders (incl. rear delt) | 16-22 [synthesis] | 12-16 [synthesis] | Rear delt gets dedicated sets |
| Rear delts (subset) | 6-10 [synthesis] | 5-8 [synthesis] | Counted inside shoulders |
| Arms | 12-18 [synthesis] | 9-13 [synthesis] | |
| Calves | 12-16 [synthesis] | 10-14 [synthesis] | High frequency, hard to grow |
| Abs | 8-12 [synthesis] | 8-12 [synthesis] | Control and detail focus |

Direction of travel in prep: total volume usually falls modestly to
protect recovery in a deficit, intensity techniques are kept to hold
muscle, and the cut comes mostly from diet and cardio rather than from
piling on more sets. This matches the Farah / Aceto / Nicholls
"diet-led prep" view in section 3. [synthesis] on the exact taper.

## 6. Exercise selection philosophy

Prioritised:
- Heavy compound builders early in a session for raw mass: rows,
  pulldowns, presses, squats, leg press, hack squats. FST-7 and Y3T
  both open with these. Source: BarBend FST-7 and Y3T.
- Machines and cables for the pump/finisher work, because constant
  tension and a stable groove let the athlete chase blood flow and
  fascia stretch with less joint and stabiliser fatigue. This is the
  whole point of the FST-7 7-set finisher and Sarcev's giant sets.
  Source: BarBend, JYM Supplement Science.
- Stretch-position and peak-contraction isolation for lagging detail
  muscles (rear-delt cable work leading with the elbow, lying or seated
  leg curls, calf raises with a slow eccentric). Source: BarBend
  weak-points piece.

Avoided or de-prioritised:
- Movements that load the spine or stabilisers more than the target
  muscle when the goal is local pump rather than mass. Open prep work
  leans away from those once the heavy base is done.
- Anything that promotes a thicker waist for athletes already fighting
  midsection distension (very heavy loaded carries, heavy standing
  twists, aggressive ab-thickening work). [synthesis] applied from the
  Dauda waist/symmetry point.
- Maximal low-rep singles. Open is hypertrophy, not powerlifting, so
  load is a tool for tension in roughly the 6-15 range, with high-rep
  flush work on top, not a 1-3 rep strength chase. Source: Y3T ranges.

The selection logic in one line: compound to build the mass, isolation
and constant-tension machine work to finish it and to fix the specific
spots judges mark down.

## 7. Practices specific to Open that differ from general hypertrophy

- **Heavy blood-flow / pump work as a deliberate growth driver, not a
  finisher afterthought.** FST-7's 7 sets at 30-45s rest and Sarcev's
  4+ exercise giant sets exist specifically to flood the muscle. General
  hypertrophy guidance treats the pump as a by-product. Open coaches
  treat hyperemia and fascia stretch as the mechanism. Source: BarBend,
  JYM Supplement Science. (The fascia-stretch and hyperplasia rationale
  is coach theory, the evidence for it is weaker than for plain
  mechanical-tension volume, which is worth flagging in an audit.)
- **Intra-workout nutrition timed to the pump.** Sarcev's hyperemia
  model has athletes taking fast carbs and amino acids during the
  engorged window. That is an Open-specific habit, not standard
  hypertrophy advice. Source: JYM Supplement Science.
- **Intensity techniques as routine, not occasional.** Drop sets,
  rest-pause, partials and forced reps are baked into Open sessions to
  push volume and metabolic stress past what straight sets give.
- **Three-week fibre-type rotation (Y3T)** deliberately cycles in 20+
  rep weeks, which most general programmes treat as junk volume. Open
  uses it to vary the stimulus and rest connective tissue. Source:
  The Barbell, BarBend.
- **Prep that protects fullness over chasing dryness.** The 2025 Dauda
  result reframed this for the whole field: an Open prep is judged a
  failure if it strips condition at the cost of muscle fullness on the
  day. Source: Fitness Volt.

## 8. Common Open weak points and how the best coaches address each

- **Rear delts.** Train them earlier and more often, cut momentum, lead
  with the elbow, keep tension on the joint. Practically this means
  dedicated rear-delt cable and reverse-pec work given its own slot
  rather than a few token sets after pressing. Source: BarBend
  weak-points piece. Rambod's FST-7 finisher logic applies well here
  given the small muscle and high pump response. [synthesis] on the
  method pairing.
- **Hamstrings.** Earlier placement, more weekly exposure, clearer
  progression, and both knee-flexion (leg curls) and hip-hinge
  (stiff-leg / Romanian) work to hit the full muscle. Source: BarBend,
  Mirafit.
- **Calves.** Train them in a way the athlete is not used to: heavy,
  focused reps with a slow eccentric and an explosive concentric, and
  high frequency. They resist growth, so the answer is novelty plus
  frequency, not just more of the same. Source: RitFit, BarBend.
- **Abs / serratus and waist control.** Detail and control work plus
  strict waist management, avoiding thickening movements for those who
  distend. The judging cost of a blocky waist is the lever here. Source:
  Fitness Volt, BarBend.
- **Lagging back thickness.** More rowing volume and rear-chain work,
  given the back is where shows are won. [synthesis] from the judging
  weighting in section 1.

General principle the coaches share, stated plainly by the BarBend and
Mirafit weak-point guidance: a lagging muscle gets trained first, more
often, and with cleaner progression than the rest.

## 9. Recovery management at this level of volume and size

At Open volumes and bodyweights, recovery is the limiter, and the named
coaches treat nutrition and rest as the larger half of the result, not
training. Chad Nicholls' explicit hierarchy (diet first, training
second) captures the priority. Source: Sportskeeda.

Practical recovery levers in current Open practice:
- **Food first.** The high-carb, high-calorie approach associated with
  Aceto (Cutler-era carb intakes) and Farah's structured carb
  manipulation is what fuels recovery from the volume. Recovery is bought
  at the dinner table before it is bought with rest days. Source:
  Fitness Volt, Generation Iron.
- **Volume cycling to manage fatigue.** Y3T's three-week rotation
  exists partly so connective tissue and the nervous system get periodic
  relief from constant heavy loading. Source: BarBend, The Barbell.
- **Frequency without overshoot.** The research caution that returns
  flatten past ~20 weekly sets per muscle and overreaching risk climbs
  applies even more to athletes already near their recovery ceiling.
  Source: PMC umbrella review, AskVora.
- **Peak-week and stress management as recovery, not just aesthetics.**
  Lunsford's 2025 team put explicit weight on peak-week timing and
  stress management, treating the nervous-system and fill-out side of
  recovery as part of why he came in sharper and fuller than rivals.
  Source: Generation Iron, BoxLife.

Audit flag for the Volyume model: the volume figures in section 5 are
calibrated to enhanced, advanced athletes with elite recovery support.
They are not a template for a general user, and any plan logic that
borrows Open volume ranges has to scale them down hard for natural
trainees. The 10-20 set research range is the safer anchor for general
users. [synthesis] / Source: Schoenfeld meta-analyses via AskVora and
PMC.
