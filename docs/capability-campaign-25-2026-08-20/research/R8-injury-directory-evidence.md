## 1. SHOULDER

### Rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain/tendinopathy

- **aliases:** rotator cuff tendinitis, RCT, subacromial pain syndrome, painful arc
- **variability:** Presentations vary significantly by cuff muscle involved (supraspinatus vs infraspinatus); overhead athletes show greater overload variants than occupational cases (T1: JOSPT 2025 guideline).
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Overhead pressing: commonly reported as aggravating, loading needs scapular upward rotation clearance [T1: JOSPT 2025]
  - Bench press: elbow angle and grip width modify rotator cuff load; narrower grip, scapula retraction reduce posterior shear force [T3: Frontiers Physiology 2024]
  - Internal/external rotation strength: deficits in eccentric strength are risk factors; training these movements protective [T3: PMC 2017]
  - Firm bar grip: high-grip tension aggravates cuff in early-stage rehab [T5: practical]
  - Horizontal adduction (cross-body presses): often restricted [T1: JOSPT 2025]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Progressive resistance training at load tolerance is Grade A evidence; athletes continue lower-body work unrestricted; unaffected shoulder movements continue with modification [T1: JOSPT 2025].
- **clinician-territory:** Load progression criteria; imaging indications (guideline discourages early imaging); sport-specific return criteria; individual pain-tolerance thresholds for load advance.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive neurological deficit, severe trauma, or night pain unresponsive to 3 months conservative care warrant imaging/specialist assessment [T1: JOSPT 2025].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Progressive loading at tolerable intensity builds cuff resilience; complete rest delays recovery [T1: JOSPT 2025].
  2. Scapula control during overhead movement clears subacromial space; coordination training is core to rehab [T1: JOSPT 2025].
  3. External and internal rotation strength imbalances increase injury risk; balanced training prevents recurrence [T3: PMC 2017].
- **evidence:**
  1. JOSPT (2025) "Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy Diagnosis, Nonsurgical Medical Care, and Rehabilitation." Grade A progressive resistance training; motor control exercises; individualization per pain tolerance.
  2. Frontiers Physiology (2024) "Effects of bench press technique variations on shoulder loads." Narrow grip, scapula retraction reduce rotator cuff stress.
  3. PMC (2017) "Increased Upper Trapezius Muscle Stiffness in Overhead Athletes." External/internal rotation strength protective.
- **confidence:** HIGH (T1 guideline exists; consistent cross-study findings; movement modification evidence abundant)

### Shoulder instability/dislocation history

- **aliases:** shoulder dislocation, glenohumeral instability, recurrent subluxation, apprehension with movement
- **variability:** Traumatic vs atraumatic; anterior, posterior, multidirectional variants present differently; hypermobility spectrum increases complexity [T1: CSM 2019; T3: Paediatric evidence 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Overhead position: end-range external rotation often triggers apprehension [T2: MASIP survey 2021]
  - Horizontal abduction (external rotation at 90/90): typically restricted early-stage [T2: MASIP 2021]
  - Firm bar grip under load: guarding common; kinetic-chain instability often co-present [T5: practical]
  - Heavy horizontal adduction with momentum: risky in early/mid-stage rehab [T5: practical]
  - Pulling movements (pull-ups, rows): require scapular/rotator cuff readiness [T2: MASIP 2021]
- **keep-training-evidence:** After 1-2 weeks immobilization, ROM exercises begin; resistance training only after full ROM restored; athletes can continue lower-body strength unrestricted [T1: CSM 2019].
- **clinician-territory:** Classification (traumatic/atraumatic); immobilization duration (no consensus in literature); rehabilitation timeline (unclear); return-to-sport decision (sport-dependent, athlete-dependent); surgical vs conservative choice.
- **red-flag-note:** Recurrent dislocation despite conservative rehab, or neurological compromise post-event, warrants specialist assessment [T1: CSM 2019].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Scapular and rotator cuff strengthening reduces recurrence risk more than joint protection alone [T1: CSM 2019].
  2. Progressive kinetic-chain control (core, hip stability) essential to shoulder stability during loading [T2: MASIP 2021].
  3. Proprioceptive training alongside strengthening improves long-term stability outcomes [T1: CSM 2019].
- **evidence:**
  1. CSM (2019) "Shoulder Stability and Movement Coordination Deficits CPG." Scapular/rotator cuff exercises; reassurance; kinetic-chain focus.
  2. MASIP Survey (2021) "Management of Atraumatic Shoulder Instability." No consensus on duration; multidisciplinary approach needed.
  3. PMC (2024) "Paediatric and Adolescent Shoulder Instability." Generalized hyperlaxity assessment; lifetime incidence history critical.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (guidelines exist but evidence sparse on specific exercise superiority; high recurrence rates indicate knowledge gaps)

### AC joint (acromioclavicular) problems

- **aliases:** AC joint pain, AC separation, distal clavicle pain, AC joint sprain/osteoarthritis
- **variability:** Acute sprain (grade I-III) vs chronic osteoarthritis; mechanical vs inflammatory presentations differ in training tolerance [T3: case reports 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Horizontal adduction (cross-body movements): direct AC stress; often aggravating in acute phase [T1: AC OA protocol; T3: case 2024]
  - Overhead pressing: typically restricted acutely due to AC joint compression [T5: practical]
  - Firm bar grip with horizontal adduction: high AC load [T3: Frontiers 2024]
  - Scapular instability: common co-impairment; compounds AC stress [T2: rehabilitation protocols]
  - Anti-rotational movements: lower AC demand; useful early-phase substitute [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Closed-chain exercises limit AC joint stress while permitting muscle activation; once mobility restored, overhead work progresses; lower-body training unrestricted [T3: rehabilitation case reports].
- **clinician-territory:** AC joint mobilization with movement (MWM) indications; imaging rationale (joint degeneration, comminution); surgical consultation criteria; load-progression benchmarks.
- **red-flag-note:** Severe traumatic AC joint injury (grade III+) or comminution may require specialist assessment [T3: case reports 2024].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. AC joint mobilisation with movement combined with strengthening reduces pain and disability more than exercise alone [T3: case 2024].
  2. Periscapular (scapular stabiliser) strengthening reduces AC joint stress during overhead movement [T2: rehabilitation protocols].
  3. Progressive overhead positioning from neutral to end-range allows tissue adaptation without guarding [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. Open Orthopaedics Journal (2023) "Rehabilitation for Shoulder Instability." Scapular stabilisation; closed-chain progressions.
  2. PMC (2024) "Multimodal Exercises for AC Joint Sprain." MWM + therapeutic exercise more effective than exercise alone.
  3. AC OA Protocol (source: OrthoArizona). Four-phase progression; pain-driven activity modification; periscapular focus.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (case evidence abundant; RCT evidence limited; load progressions not standardised)

### Labral/biceps-related considerations

- **aliases:** SLAP tear (superior labrum anterior posterior), labral tear, long head of biceps (LHB) tendinopathy, biceps-labral complex injury
- **variability:** SLAP type (I-IV) and athlete age drastically alter presentation; overhead athletes show high-force variants; older patients often show degenerative patterns [T3: case series 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Biceps-loading movements (chin-ups, pull-ups, heavy curls): directly load LHB tendon and labral attachment; early/mid-stage restrictions common [T2: rehab guidelines; T5: practical]
  - Overhead arm positioning (especially abduction + external rotation): common provocative posture [T3: SLAP tear studies]
  - Horizontal adduction with firm grip (bench press variants): cumulative biceps microtrauma with repeated sets [T3: case series 2024]
  - Front-raise movements: often tolerated better than overhead presses in early phase [T5: practical]
  - Alternating grip (overhand/underhand) in pressing: distributes LHB load; recommended modification [T3: athlete case reports]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Most SLAP tears (type I-II) improve conservative over 3-6 months; athletes continue lower-body, posterior-chain, and scapular work unrestricted; upper-body pressing continues with grip/loading modifications [T3: rehab case series].
- **clinician-territory:** SLAP classification (imaging/arthroscopy); conservative vs surgical decision (type-dependent, age-dependent, sport-dependent); sport-specific return criteria; biceps tenodesis vs repair vs conservative thresholds.
- **red-flag-note:** Persistent apprehension with overhead movement despite 6 weeks rehab, or progressive neurological signs, warrants specialist imaging [T3: case series 2024].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Biceps-dominant exercises (curls, chin-ups) load the labral attachment directly; early-stage modification or avoidance reduces traction strain [T2: rehab guidelines].
  2. Side-lying shoulder flexion minimises biceps activation whilst maintaining rotator cuff stimulus [T3: rehab case series].
  3. Grip-variation progression (overhand to neutral to underhand) distributes cumulative microtrauma across shoulder flexors [T3: athlete case reports].
- **evidence:**
  1. [P]rehab (2024) "Biceps Tendon Pain Exercises and Training Strategies." Avoid heavy curls/pull-ups early; side-lying raises; avoid end-range abduction/external rotation.
  2. BSR Physical Therapy (2020) "Shoulder Labrum Tears: Exercises Before Surgery." Type I-II conservative track; three-phase progression (ROM, rotator cuff, scapular stabilisation).
  3. PMC (2024) "Biceps Tenodesis for Type II SLAP Tears." Case series; improved outcomes post-surgical in overhead athletes under 35.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH (case evidence and rehab protocols abundant; RCT comparisons limited; sport-specific evidence strong for overhead athletes)

### Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis)

- **aliases:** frozen shoulder, adhesive capsulitis, primary frozen shoulder, stiff shoulder
- **variability:** Three phases (freezing, frozen, thawing) progress differently; diabetic or thyroid-associated cases show more severity; presentation can be insidious or post-traumatic [T1: clinical guidelines 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - All overhead positions: severely restricted; movement provokes pain; passive ROM often better tolerated than active [T1: JOSPT 2013, 2009]
  - External rotation: most limited direction; critical in early-stage assessment [T1: guidelines]
  - Floor access (hands-behind-back, floor touch): contraindicated early; end-range pain common [T1: guidelines]
  - Horizontal adduction: restricted; internal rotation more limited than external in some phases [T5: practical]
  - All weight-bearing on arms (push-ups, press-ups): prohibited early; floor access restricted [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Gradual, progressive ROM work is cornerstone; pain-limited active ROM gradually replaces passive; gentle contraction exercises alongside ROM; most resolve in 12-18 months [T1: clinical guidelines 2024].
- **clinician-territory:** Diagnosis confirmation (imaging, clinical tests); suprascapular nerve block/corticosteroid injection indications; hydrodilatation rationale; surgical intervention threshold (rare); progression-phase determination.
- **red-flag-note:** Rapidly progressive loss of ROM, or ROM loss post-injury without preceding pain phase, warrant imaging to exclude rotator cuff tear or post-traumatic stiffness [T1: guidelines].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Frozen shoulder evolves in phases; pain gradually reduces whilst ROM improves over months; active exercise enhances ROM phase [T1: guidelines 2024].
  2. Corticosteroid injection or nerve block may accelerate recovery; combined with physiotherapy shows better outcomes than either alone [T1: guidelines 2024].
  3. Gentle pendulum exercises, self-stretching, and contraction-relaxation techniques support gradual ROM recovery [T3: rehabilitation studies].
- **evidence:**
  1. JOSPT (2013, 2009) "Shoulder Pain and Mobility Deficits: Adhesive Capsulitis CPG." Stretching, mobilisation, exercises; gentle ROM progression; NSAIDs for pain.
  2. E-ARM (2024) "Clinical Practice Guidelines for Non-Surgical Treatment of Primary Frozen Shoulder." Phase-based progression; three phases recognised; 12-18 month resolution typical.
  3. Ochsner Journal (2024) "Frozen Shoulder: Evidence and Model." Corticosteroid + physiotherapy superior to either alone; ROM and pain both targeted.
- **confidence:** HIGH (clinical guidelines well-established; phase-based model consensus; long natural history allows clear evidence accumulation)

## 2. ELBOW/FOREARM

### Lateral elbow tendinopathy (tennis elbow)

- **aliases:** lateral epicondylitis, lateral elbow pain, extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendinopathy, lateral epicondylalgia
- **variability:** Occupational (repetitive gripping, keyboard) vs sport-specific (tennis, throwing) presentations differ in volume tolerance; age and dominance affect severity [T1: JOSPT 2022; T3: recent review 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Wrist extension with grip (e.g. racquet handle, barbell row grip): primary aggravating movement; load and volume directly correlate with pain [T1: JOSPT 2022]
  - Firm bar grip during pulling movements: common aggravation; lighter grip or strap use reduces ECRB demand [T5: practical]
  - Pronation under load (reverse curl, pull-ups underhand): stresses ECRB; grip variation beneficial [T5: practical]
  - Repetitive gripping (kettlebell swings, thick-bar work): cumulative stress; sets x reps matter [T5: practical]
  - Elbow extension work (triceps pushdowns, straight-arm pulldowns): lower lateral elbow demand; often tolerated [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Eccentric strengthening combined with manual therapy reduces pain 42% (VAS improvement); athletes continue lower-body unrestricted; posterior-chain work often tolerated if grip modified [T3: meta-analysis 2024].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging indications (ultrasound, MRI); corticosteroid injection timing and risk-benefit; manipulative therapy integration; load-progression thresholds for sport return.
- **red-flag-note:** Sudden neurological signs (wrist drop, finger weakness) suggest posterior interosseous nerve compression; imaging warranted [T1: JOSPT 2022].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Eccentric strengthening exercises reduce pain and improve function more reliably than passive modalities alone [T3: meta-analysis 2024].
  2. Grip-width and load-volume management enables continued training; complete rest delays recovery [T1: JOSPT 2022].
  3. Wrist extensor and forearm strengthening corrects muscle imbalances that predispose to lateral epicondylitis [T1: JOSPT 2022].
- **evidence:**
  1. JOSPT (2022) "Lateral Elbow Pain and Muscle Function Impairments CPG." Exercise and manual therapy core; eccentric strengthening Grade A; engagement best practices.
  2. PMC (2024) "Kinesio Taping Plus Exercise vs Sham." Eccentric training 42% pain reduction; superiority over passive modalities shown.
  3. Journal of Pioneering Medical Sciences (2025) "Advances in Lateral Epicondylitis." Diagnosis, management, rehabilitation review; eccentric exercise evidence strong.
- **confidence:** HIGH (T1 guideline recent; strong exercise evidence; high recurrence without targeted rehab)

### Medial elbow tendinopathy (golfer's elbow)

- **aliases:** medial epicondylitis, medial epicondylalgia, golfer's elbow, flexor-pronator tendinopathy, thrower's elbow
- **variability:** Occupational (typing, gripping) vs sport-specific (throwing, golf) differ in load pattern; acute vs chronic presentations differ in aggravation threshold [T3: UK review 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Wrist flexion under load (weight in palm, flexion curls): primary aggravator; load and tempo both matter [T3: UK review 2024]
  - Pronation under load (reverse grip pressing, underhand barbell rows): flexor-pronator group involved; modification beneficial [T5: practical]
  - Firm grip with wrist flexion (kettlebell pressing, thick-bar curls): compound stress; grip aids reduce demand [T5: practical]
  - Throwing movements (if applicable): high-speed pronation-flexion compound; progressive return-to-throw needed [T2: overhead athlete protocols]
  - Wrist extension (reverse curls, wrist extension): lower demand; often tolerated as alternative [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Structured stretching and strengthening (particularly eccentric) show moderate short-term benefit; most cases self-limit with activity modification; athletes continue unaffected movements [T1: StatPearls 2024; T3: case series].
- **clinician-territory:** Ultrasound imaging rationale; injection indications and timing; corticosteroid vs PRP decision; return-to-throwing phases (if athlete); load progression benchmarks.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive weakness in grip or pronation, or neurological signs in hand, warrant imaging to exclude ulnar nerve compression [T3: UK review 2024].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Eccentric flexor-pronator strengthening and stretching show better outcomes than passive modalities; improvement takes weeks [T3: case series].
  2. Activity modification (grip technique, volume management) allows continued training whilst tendon adapts [T1: StatPearls 2024].
  3. Persistent symptoms beyond 3 months warrant specialist assessment to exclude nerve compression or partial tear [T1: StatPearls 2024].
- **evidence:**
  1. StatPearls (2024) "Medial Epicondylitis." Conservative management: activity modification, NSAIDs, physio; self-limited most cases.
  2. PMC (2024) "Eccentric Exercise Therapy for Medial Epicondylitis." Systematic review; eccentric exercise effective; stretching and strengthening core.
  3. PMC (2024) "Ultrasound in Differential Diagnosis of Medial Epicondylalgia." Imaging findings; narrative review; differential exclusion.
- **confidence:** HIGH (guidelines well-established; eccentric exercise evidence strong; occupational and sport variants recognised)

### Distal biceps tendinopathy/tear

- **aliases:** distal biceps tendinitis, biceps brachii strain, distal biceps tear, elbow flexor pain
- **variability:** Acute rupture (sudden loss of strength) vs chronic tendinopathy (progressive pain) present very differently; age (>50 years shows higher rupture risk) and load history affect presentation [T3: case literature].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Elbow flexion under load (curls, pulling, chin-ups): primary aggravation; load and speed both matter [T5: practical]
  - Supination under load (supinated grip during curl, vertical pulling): directly loads distal biceps; pronated grip spares tissue [T5: practical]
  - Firm grip combined with flexion (kettlebell curls, farmer's carries): compound stress [T5: practical]
  - Heavy eccentric flexion (lowering phase of curl): high-risk movement; progressive loading safer than shock loads [T5: practical]
  - Horizontal adduction (cross-body pressing): lower biceps demand; alternative often tolerated [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** No well-supported generalisation on whether partial tears heal conservatively vs require surgery; case series suggest some athletes return to training with load modification [T3: limited case reports].
- **clinician-territory:** Tear classification (partial vs complete, retraction degree); surgical vs conservative decision (depends on activity demands, age, patient preference); return-to-lifting criteria post-repair; strength recovery timeline (surgical repair typically 3-6 months).
- **red-flag-note:** Acute rupture presents with sudden "pop," loss of elbow flexion strength, and visible proximal muscle retraction; immediate specialist assessment needed (surgical window typically 2-3 weeks) [T3: case literature].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Distal biceps rupture is a surgical emergency; early repair (within 2-3 weeks) improves long-term strength outcomes [T3: case series].
  2. Partial tears may recover with progressive load management; supinated-grip loading should be avoided early [T5: practical].
  3. Post-repair, progressive supination and flexion strengthening over 3-6 months restores functional strength [T3: case series].
- **evidence:**
  1. [P]rehab (2024) "Biceps Tendon Pain Exercises and Training Strategies." Avoid heavy loading early; modify grip; avoid end-range pronation.
  2. PMC (limited case reports on conservative management; surgical repair literature more abundant but outside evidence scope here).
  3. Orthopedic case series consensus: rupture = surgery; partial tears = trial of conservative management with modification.
- **confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM (limited RCT evidence; mostly case reports; surgical management better-studied than conservative rehab; return-to-training evidence sparse)

### Triceps tendon considerations

- **aliases:** triceps tendinopathy, distal triceps strain, triceps tendinitis, elbow extension pain
- **variability:** Insertional tendinopathy (pain at olecranon insertion) vs mid-belly strain differ in palpation site; occupational (heavy lifting) vs sport-specific (throwing) differ in load pattern [T5: practical].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Elbow extension under load (triceps pushdowns, bench press lockout, overhead press): direct triceps demand; load and tempo both matter [T5: practical]
  - Heavy eccentric extension (lowering phase of dips, press-down): highest mechanical stress; progressive loading safer [T5: practical]
  - Closed-chain pressing (dips, bench press): higher total triceps load than open-chain [T5: practical]
  - Narrow grip pressing: shifts stress medially (towards pectoralis); may reduce triceps but increase shoulder stress [T5: practical]
  - Rope attachments vs bars in triceps work: variation in triceps activation and hand/wrist stress [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** No well-supported generalisation; most triceps strains resolve within weeks with load modification; athletes continue lower-body and unaffected upper-body movements [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Tear classification (insertion vs mid-belly); surgical vs conservative decision (complete tears typically require surgery; partial tears conservative management trial); imaging indications.
- **red-flag-note:** Sudden loss of elbow extension strength or visible proximal muscle gap suggest triceps rupture; specialist assessment needed [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Triceps strain recovers faster than other upper-limb tendon injuries; progressive loading within tolerance enables rapid return [T5: practical].
  2. Hand position variation (grip width, pronation angle) modulates triceps activation; variation management aids continued training [T5: practical].
  3. Complete triceps rupture is rare but requires surgical repair for strength preservation [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. No T1/T2 clinical guidelines specific to triceps tendinopathy found in search; evidence synthesis from orthopedic case literature only.
  2. Standard orthopedic practice: complete ruptures (sudden strength loss) require surgery; partial/chronic strains trial conservative management with load modification.
  3. Case reports (limited) suggest 2-4 week recovery for partial strains with appropriate load management.
- **confidence:** LOW (no formal clinical guidelines; evidence limited to case reports and orthopedic practice norms; training-specific evidence absent)

### Forearm rotation restrictions (pronation/supination limitation)

- **aliases:** forearm pronation/supination loss, radioulnar joint restriction, forearm pronation/supiation dysfunction, pronation/supitation pain
- **variability:** Post-fracture (radius/ulna) vs inflammatory (synovitis) vs positional (muscle tightness) present differently; unilateral (dominant-arm overuse) vs bilateral (systemic) inform pathology [T5: practical].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Supination under load (supinated-grip pulls, curls with rotation): aggravating if supination restricted; grip modification beneficial [T5: practical]
  - Pronation during pressing (bench press, floor press): movement quality affected; compensation common [T5: practical]
  - Gripping with rotation (eg kettlebell cleaning, bottoms-up carries): combines pronation/supitation with load; avoided early-stage [T5: practical]
  - Rapid pronation/supitation cycling (eg shadow boxing): high velocity stresses radioulnar joint; avoided acute phase [T5: practical]
  - Bilateral movements (symmetrical pressing) tolerated better than unilateral if one arm restricted [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** No well-supported generalisation; post-fracture, supervised progressive ROM enables return to loaded training; athletes continue unaffected movement planes [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (X-ray, CT) for post-fracture healing status; synovitis assessment; interosseous membrane integrity post-injury; progressive ROM clearance; load-return criteria.
- **red-flag-note:** Post-fracture neurological signs (weakness, sensory change in forearm/hand) suggest nerve injury (posterior interosseous, anterior interosseous); imaging and specialist assessment needed [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Pronation and supination ROM recovers gradually post-fracture; progressive resistance within ROM improves tissue strength [T5: practical].
  2. Grip-variation training (neutral, pronated, supinated) distributes load; unilateral overload avoidance aids recovery [T5: practical].
  3. Post-fracture return to loaded forearm rotation typically takes 8-12 weeks; progression should be gradual and pain-guided [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. Post-fracture rehabilitation consensus (no T1/T2 guideline identified): progressive ROM, then resistance, then sport-specific loading.
  2. Orthopedic practice: synovitis managed conservatively; radioulnar joint mobility critical to loaded function.
  3. Training literature: limited evidence on return-to-lifting forearm rotation tolerance; case-based progression typical.
- **confidence:** LOW (no formal clinical guidelines; evidence limited to post-fracture orthopedic norms and training experience; training-specific evidence absent)

## 3. WRIST/HAND

### Wrist flexion/extension intolerance

- **aliases:** wrist strain, wrist sprain, wrist pain with movement, dorsal/palmar wrist pain
- **variability:** Flexion-dominant (palmar-side pain) vs extension-dominant (dorsal-side pain) differ in mechanism; post-fracture recovery vs inflammatory tendinopathy differ in timeline and prognosis [T3: wrist imaging studies].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Wrist flexion under load (palms-down pressing, row grip, carrying): aggravating if flexion intolerance; wrist extension relieves pressure [T3: carpal tunnel MRI studies]
  - Wrist extension (reverse curls, ball grip work): aggravates dorsal-side pain; neutral or slight flexion tolerated better [T5: practical]
  - Firm bar grip (deadlift, barbell row): distributes load across wrist; neutral wrist position reduces moment arm [T5: practical]
  - Weight-bearing on palm (push-ups, planks, handstand): contraindicated early; elevated surfaces reduce wrist dorsiflexion demand [T5: practical]
  - Deviation movements (radial/ulnar flexion combined with loaded wrist): avoided early; sagittal-plane movement (flex/extend) more tolerated [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Wrist extension or neutral-wrist programming (assisted grip, straps) enables continued lower-body and rowing work; athletes continue unaffected movements unrestricted [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI) for fracture healing or ligament integrity; functional wrist stability testing; progressive ROM clearance; sport-specific grip testing.
- **red-flag-note:** Post-fracture neurological signs (tingling, weakness in fingers) warrant imaging to exclude nerve injury or compartment syndrome [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Wrist positioning (neutral or slight extension) reduces tendon compression during loading; position awareness aids training modification [T3: carpal tunnel MRI studies].
  2. Progressive wrist ROM and grip strengthening enable return to loaded gripping; early immobilisation should be time-limited [T5: practical].
  3. Strap use and neutral-grip implements reduce wrist demand early; progression to bare-hand gripping as tolerance improves [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. PMC (2020) "Effects of Wrist Extension on Median Nerve and Flexor Tendon Excursions in Carpal Tunnel." Wrist extension 2.2mm nerve clearance; flexion 1.1mm; extension beneficial for CTS patients.
  2. ScienceDirect (historical) "Carpal Tunnel Changes During Wrist Flexion/Extension (MRI)." Flexion decreases space; extension increases; implications for positioning.
  3. NASM (2024) "Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Corrective Exercise." Wrist extensor lengthening; wrist flexor stability; progressive grip tolerance.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (MRI evidence clear on positioning; training-modification evidence limited; post-fracture protocols vary by fracture type)

### Grip limitations

- **aliases:** grip strength loss, hand pain with gripping, finger pain, palmar/metacarpal pain, grip intolerance
- **variability:** Acute strain vs chronic overuse; single-finger vs whole-hand pain; intrinsic (hand muscles) vs extrinsic (forearm) weakness differ in pathology and training tolerance [T5: practical].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Firm bar grip (deadlift, row, pull-up): aggravating; strap use, thicker bars (reduced grip demand), assisted grip reduce load [T5: practical]
  - Sustained grip (carries, loaded holds): cumulative; duration and load both matter [T5: practical]
  - Grip variation (neutral, pronated, supinated): distributes demand; alternating reduces single-hand overload [T5: practical]
  - Pinch-grip movements (plate pinch, thick-bar work): high intrinsic hand demand; avoided early [T5: practical]
  - Open-hand movements (dumbbell work vs barbell): lower grip demand; early progression option [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Assisted-grip training (straps, alternative implements) enables continued deadlift, row, and pulling work; lower-body unrestricted; athletes continue unaffected movements [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Hand imaging (X-ray) for fracture or arthritis; nerve conduction testing (carpal tunnel, ulnar tunnel); functional grip strength testing; sport-specific grip demands assessment.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive neurological signs (tingling, weakness spreading across hand, muscle wasting in thenar/hypothenar eminences) suggest nerve compression; imaging and specialist assessment needed [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Grip-assist devices (straps, wrapped bars, alternative grips) enable continued training whilst hand recovers; progression to bare-hand gripping as tolerance improves [T5: practical].
  2. Grip variation (alternating hands, grip types) distributes cumulative load and reduces single-hand overload injury risk [T5: practical].
  3. Pinch-grip and heavy grip training requires gradual progression; early-stage high-volume grip work delays recovery [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. No formal T1/T2 clinical guidelines on grip intolerance in strength training found.
  2. Orthopedic consensus: carpal tunnel and cubital tunnel compression management includes activity modification and grip load reduction.
  3. Training practice: grip assists and variation common accommodations; progression based on symptom tolerance.
- **confidence:** LOW (no formal clinical guidelines; evidence limited to orthopedic norms and training experience; condition-specific evidence absent)

### Thumb/hand loading restrictions

- **aliases:** thumb pain, thumb sprain, CMC joint (carpometacarpal) arthritis, hand osteoarthritis, thumb grip pain
- **variability:** Thumb CMC arthritis vs acute thumb sprain vs scaphoid fracture differ drastically in recovery timeline (arthritis = chronic, sprain = weeks, scaphoid = months); age and activity level affect prognosis [T3: hand surgery case series].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Pinch-grip and opposition movements (picking up objects, finger flexion): painful with thumb involvement; avoided early [T5: practical]
  - High-load thumb stress (kettlebell handle, axle barbell): contraindicated acute phase; progression slow [T5: practical]
  - Firm-grip activities (pull-ups, rows with thumb in): aggravating; thumb-free grip (thumbless, suicide grip) alternative [T5: practical]
  - Weight-bearing on hand with thumb abducted (handstand, floor press): stresses CMC joint; avoided until cleared [T5: practical]
  - Ulnar-sided hand work (carrying, loaded hold using outside fingers only): lower thumb demand; early progression option [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Thumb-sparing grip and handling (no thumb, or minimal opposition load) enables continued training; lower-body entirely unrestricted; posterior-chain work continues [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI) for fracture, dislocation, or joint degenerative status; arthroscopy indications (rare); CMC joint stability testing; functional grip tolerance assessment.
- **red-flag-note:** Post-traumatic thumb dislocation or significant fracture displacement require specialist assessment for reduction and stabilisation decision [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Scaphoid fractures require prolonged immobilisation (6-12 weeks) due to poor blood supply; early loading risks non-union [T3: hand surgery case series].
  2. Thumb opposition and pinch-grip progressively restore function; early-stage complete avoidance of pinch movements aids healing [T5: practical].
  3. CMC joint arthritis tolerates modified grips (thumb strapping, avoiding opposition load); gradual tolerance improvement occurs over months [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. Hand surgery case series (limited; outside formal guideline scope): scaphoid non-union risk with early loading; CMC arthritis progression unclear but activity-related.
  2. Orthopedic consensus: acute thumb sprain (ligament) managed conservatively 4-6 weeks; arthritis managed with activity modification and anti-inflammatory strategies.
  3. No training-specific return-to-lifting guidelines identified for thumb injuries.
- **confidence:** LOW (no formal clinical guidelines; evidence limited to hand surgery case reports and orthopedic norms; training-specific evidence absent)

## 4. SPINE/TRUNK

### Low back pain (persistent/recurrent)

- **aliases:** chronic low back pain, CLBP, non-specific LBP, mechanical low back pain, persistent LBP
- **variability:** Acute vs chronic presentations differ; mechanical (postural) vs inflammatory (systemic) differ in imaging findings and prognosis; flexion-dominant vs extension-dominant pain patterns inform movement strategy [T1: clinical guidelines].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Spinal loading (axial compression in squat, deadlift): commonly aggravating; load and depth both matter; modified depth or load often tolerated [T1: NSCA guidelines 2024; T3: LBP movement analysis]
  - Flexion loading (touching toes with load, flexion-moment squats): aggravating for flexion-dominant pain; avoided acute phase; extension exercises preferred [T1: guideline consensus]
  - Extension loading (back extensions, deadlift): aggravates extension-dominant pain; flexion and anti-extension core work preferred [T1: consensus]
  - Rotation under load (medicine ball rotations, loaded carries): avoided acute phase; anti-rotational work (Pallof press, dead bugs) preferred [T1: core-stability consensus]
  - Hip extension (deadlift, leg press emphasis on glute drive): often tolerated; hip-hinge pattern preferred over spinal bending [T1: rehab protocol consensus]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Progressive resistance training (deadlifts with load/depth modification, squats with form focus) enables continued training; lower-body work continues with modification; athletes continue unaffected movements [T1: NSCA 2024; T3: weightlifter case studies].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging indications (red-flag screening, specific pathology if clinical findings suggest); pain classification (mechanical vs inflammatory); exercise progression thresholds; return-to-sport decision criteria.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive neurological deficit (leg weakness, foot drop, bowel/bladder dysfunction, night pain), or history of cancer/infection/osteoporosis, warrant urgent imaging [T1: guideline consensus].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Progressive heavy resistance training (including squats and deadlifts with form) is as effective as other interventions for chronic LBP; complete rest delays recovery [T1: NSCA 2024].
  2. Core stability (anti-extension, anti-rotation, anti-lateral flexion) training reduces LBP recurrence and improves sport performance [T1: guideline consensus].
  3. Pain-driven load management (gradual progression rather than avoidance) enables return to full training capacity [T1: NSCA 2024].
- **evidence:**
  1. NSCA Coach (2024) "Risk-Benefit Considerations for Deadlifting in Low-Back Injury Rehab." Progressive deadlift loading safe with form focus; benefits outweigh risks.
  2. ScienceDirect (2024) "Clinical Guidelines Silent on Physical Activity Recommendations for LBP." Meta-analysis; guidelines lack specificity on exercise prescription; heterogeneous interventions all show benefit.
  3. PMC (2024) "Pain-Related Fear of Movement Dynamics in Weightlifters with LBP." Psychosocial factors (fear-avoidance) impede recovery more than pain alone; progressive loading essential.
- **confidence:** HIGH (T1 guidelines establish exercise as core intervention; pain-driven progression consensus; high-quality RCT evidence abundant)

### Cervical restrictions

- **aliases:** neck pain, cervical strain, cervical dysfunction, neck restriction, torticollis
- **variability:** Acute whiplash vs chronic postural pain vs radiculopathy (nerve-root compression) differ drastically in prognosis and training tolerance; age and trauma history affect severity [T5: clinical practice].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Overhead position (overhead press, pull-up): aggravates if cervical extension restricted; neutral-neck alternatives often tolerated [T5: practical]
  - Horizontal pulling (rows, chin-ups): tolerated better if scapular position neutral and cervical strain avoided [T5: practical]
  - Heavy spinal loading with head position (squat with head-up): aggravates if cervical stress compounds axial loading [T5: practical]
  - Rotation or lateral flexion under load (rotational carry, overhead walk): avoided if cervical rotation/flexion restricted [T5: practical]
  - Bilateral symmetrical movement (bilateral pressing, squats) tolerated better than unilateral if postural compensation risk [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Neck pain typically does not restrict lower-body training; athletes continue unaffected movements unrestricted; upper-body modifications often temporary [T5: clinical practice].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (X-ray, MRI, CT) for fracture, nerve compression, disc herniation; neurological testing (radiculopathy screen); cervical ROM assessment; progressive return-to-load criteria.
- **red-flag-note:** Acute severe trauma, progressive neurological signs (arm weakness, hand tingling, balance loss), or signs of myelopathy (bilateral symptoms, gait dysfunction) warrant urgent imaging [T5: clinical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Cervical strain from static postures or whiplash typically resolves within weeks with gradual ROM recovery and gentle strengthening [T5: practical].
  2. Progressive cervical ROM and neck-stabiliser strengthening (isometric, then resistance) prevent chronic recurrence [T5: practical].
  3. Radiculopathy (radiating arm pain) requires specialist assessment; loading modifications depend on nerve involvement [T5: clinical concern].
- **evidence:**
  1. No formal T1/T2 clinical guidelines specific to cervical pain in strength training identified.
  2. Clinical practice consensus: acute cervical strain managed conservatively; ROM progression and gentle strengthening core to recovery.
  3. Radiculopathy management: specialist assessment required; imaging often needed; loading typically restricted until neurological signs resolve.
- **confidence:** LOW (no formal clinical guidelines in strength-training context; evidence limited to general cervical pain management; condition-specific nuance needed)

### Thoracic restrictions

- **aliases:** mid-back pain, thoracic strain, thoracic dysfunction, upper-back pain, postural thoracic restriction
- **variability:** Postural (muscle tightness, poor scapular control) vs traumatic (rib, costovertebral joint involvement) vs pathological (disc, joint) differ in recovery; sedentary populations show different patterns than athletes [T5: clinical practice].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Overhead position: aggravates if thoracic extension restricted; neutral or slightly flexed alternatives often tolerated [T5: practical]
  - Horizontal pulling (rows, especially high rows): often therapeutic if scapular mechanics sound; full ROM potentially aggravating [T5: practical]
  - Spinal rotation (medicine ball rotations, Pallof): restricted if thoracic rotation limited; anti-rotation work preferred [T5: practical]
  - Vertical pulling: tolerated better if scapular control maintained; poor scapular position aggravates thoracic strain [T5: practical]
  - Axial loading with thoracic flexion (deep squat with forward lean): avoided if thoracic mobility poor; upright squat preferred [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Thoracic pain typically does not restrict lower-body; athletes continue squats, deadlifts, and lower-body pulling unrestricted; upper-body modifications often temporary [T5: clinical practice].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (X-ray, MRI, CT) for rib fracture, disc pathology, costochondral joint involvement; scapular dysfunction assessment; postural analysis; ROM progression clearance.
- **red-flag-note:** Acute severe trauma with rib fracture risk, or chest/breathing symptoms, warrant urgent assessment [T5: clinical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Thoracic mobility progressively improves with targeted stretching and movement; rowing and horizontal-pulling variations enhance thoracic extension [T5: practical].
  2. Scapular control during overhead and pulling movements reduces compensatory thoracic stress; scapular-stabiliser training preventive [T5: practical].
  3. Postural thoracic restriction from sedentary habits improves rapidly with movement; progressive load returns to normal [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. No formal T1/T2 clinical guidelines on thoracic pain in strength training identified.
  2. Clinical practice consensus: postural thoracic restriction managed with mobility work and strengthening; trauma requires imaging.
  3. Training consensus: thoracic pain rarely restricts lower-body training; upper-body modifications usually temporary.
- **confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM (no formal guidelines; evidence limited to clinical practice norms; training-specific evidence sparse)

### Axial-loading restriction

- **aliases:** spinal compression intolerance, heavy-load spinal pain, axial-load sensitivity, compressive back pain
- **variability:** Structural (stenosis, disc degeneration) vs non-structural (muscle guarding, fear-avoidance) differ in prognosis and imaging findings; age and cumulative load history affect tolerance [T1: LBP guidelines].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Squat depth: deep squat commonly aggravates; partial-range or elevated squat tolerated; progressive depth increase as tolerance improves [T1: LBP guideline consensus; T3: case studies]
  - Deadlift load: heavy loads trigger symptoms; partial-range or reduced load tolerated; progressive advancement recommended [T1: NSCA 2024]
  - Loaded carry (farmer's, Zercher): axial compression; reduced load or duration initially; progression enables full loading [T5: practical]
  - Bracing/support (belt use): may reduce pain acutely; progressive reduction as core strength builds prevents belt dependency [T5: practical]
  - Unloaded movement: often tolerated better; progressive return to loading enables tissue adaptation [T1: guideline consensus]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Unloaded exercises (bodyweight movement, isometric work) enable continuous training; progressive load return (lighter load, partial range) allows gradual tissue adaptation and strength maintenance [T1: LBP guidelines].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging indications (stenosis, spondylolisthesis, disc herniation screening); load-tolerance testing; progressive ROM clearance; return-to-full-loading criteria.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive neurological deficit (leg weakness, foot drop, bowel/bladder dysfunction) with axial loading suggests spinal cord compression; urgent imaging warranted [T1: guideline consensus].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Progressive loading of the spine (gradual increase in squat depth, deadlift weight, or loaded-carry duration) builds resilience; rapid load jumps risk symptom flare [T1: LBP guideline consensus].
  2. Core stability and neutral-spine positioning during axial-loading movements reduce compressive load on discs [T1: guideline consensus].
  3. Bracing can reduce acute symptoms but should be time-limited; progression to unbraced movement with core engagement prevents dependency [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. NSCA Coach (2024) "Modify Back Squat for Lower Back Pain." Partial-range initial; progressive depth increase; core focus; load management.
  2. ScienceDirect (2024) "Clinical Guidelines Silent on PA and Exercise for LBP." Evidence supports progressive loading over reduction; fear-avoidance harmful.
  3. PMC (2024) "Pain-Related Fear of Movement in Weightlifters." Psychological factors (fear) impede progression more than pain; gradual exposure beneficial.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH (guidelines address axial loading; progressive-load evidence strong; condition-specific nuance exists)

### Loaded flexion/extension/rotation restrictions

- **aliases:** loaded spinal movement intolerance, compound spinal movement pain, directional preference pain
- **variability:** Flexion-dominant vs extension-dominant vs rotation-dominant pain patterns differ in exercise strategy; acute vs chronic presentations differ in aggravation threshold and recovery timeline [T1: LBP guideline consensus].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Flexion loading (forward bending, flexion-based squats, leg-press with flexion): aggravates flexion-dominant pain; extension-based movement preferred; deadlift positioning preferred over squat [T1: guideline consensus]
  - Extension loading (back extensions, heavy deadlift lockout, arched bench press): aggravates extension-dominant pain; flexion-based core work and anti-extension exercises preferred [T1: guideline consensus]
  - Rotation loading (medicine ball rotations, loaded Pallof-press variations): avoided acute phase; anti-rotation (resisted rotation resistance without movement) preferred [T1: core-stability consensus]
  - Bilateral symmetrical movement: generally tolerated better; unilateral (single-leg, single-arm) may expose directional preference [T5: practical]
  - Progressive directional loading: gradual exposure to aggravating direction within pain tolerance aids tissue adaptation [T1: guideline consensus]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Non-aggravating movement planes continue unrestricted; progressive within-pain-tolerance loading of aggravating directions enables recovery and capacity building [T1: guideline consensus].
- **clinician-territory:** Movement screening to establish directional preference; imaging if specific directional movement patterns suggest structural pathology; load-progression criteria individualised to directional tolerance.
- **red-flag-note:** Acute severe trauma, progressive bilateral symptoms, or neurological signs warrant imaging [T1: guideline consensus].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Directional preference (flexion-avoiding vs extension-avoiding) guides exercise selection and load progression; matching strategy to preference optimises outcomes [T1: guideline consensus].
  2. Progressive loading within pain tolerance (not to avoidance) in the non-preferred direction builds resilience [T1: guideline consensus].
  3. Bilateral symmetrical loading often tolerated better than unilateral; progressive unilateral tolerance improves with time and proper load management [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. JOSPT (multiple years) LBP guideline: directional preference assessment; preference-matched exercise prescription; progressive exposure.
  2. ScienceDirect (2024) "Clinical Guidelines on PA for LBP." Diverse interventions all show benefit; heterogeneity suggests individual preference matching matters.
  3. PMC (2024) "Fear-Avoidance Dynamics in Weightlifters." Psychological factors impede directional exposure; gradual loading within tolerance overcomes fear-avoidance.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (guidelines address directional preference; evidence supports matching strategy to preference; high heterogeneity in published outcomes)

## 5. HIP/GROIN

### Hip-related pain (ROM restrictions, FAI-related pain)

- **aliases:** groin pain, hip flexor pain, hip-related groin pain, femoroacetabular impingement (FAI), hip-flexor tightness pain, labral pain, anterior hip pain
- **variability:** FAI-related pain (mechanical impingement) vs labral pain vs iliopsoas tendinopathy vs hip-flexor tightness differ in movement provocation patterns and imaging; age and activity level affect severity [T1: clinical guidelines; T3: FAI case studies].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Deep squat (below parallel): commonly aggravating if hip flexion + internal rotation restricted; partial-depth tolerated; progressive depth increases enable adaptation [T1: FAI guidelines; T3: case studies]
  - Hip-loaded flexion (high-knee drills, seated hip flexion against resistance): direct hip-flexor load; often aggravating; resistance should progress cautiously [T3: FAI rehab protocols]
  - Loaded full-depth leg press: similar to deep squat; reduced range or partial loading initially; progressive depth advancement [T3: FAI case studies]
  - Rotational movements at end-range (rotational lunges, transverse-plane loaded movements): compound flexion + rotation stresses; avoided acute phase [T5: practical]
  - Hip abduction/adduction work: often tolerated; lower-load alternatives during acute phase [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Physical therapy (first-line for FAI) allows athletes to continue training with load modifications; three RCTs find no superiority of surgery vs PT at 2 years; 2-3 sessions/week PT with 3-month minimum duration target all hip muscle groups [T1: FAI clinical practice evidence].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (X-ray, MRI, ultrasound) for FAI morphology, labral tear, osteoarthritis; hip ROM testing (FABER, FADIR); muscle strength asymmetry assessment; return-to-sport decision (sport-dependent loading).
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive hip pain with weight-bearing despite conservative management, or imaging evidence of severe osteoarthritis, may warrant specialist assessment for surgical consultation [T1: FAI guidelines].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Hip abductor, extensor, and flexor strengthening (especially eccentric hip external rotation) reduces FAI symptoms and prevents recurrence [T1: FAI guidelines].
  2. Depth-modified squats and leg-press variations enable continued lower-body training whilst tissues adapt; progressive depth increases as tolerance improves [T1: FAI case studies].
  3. Physical therapy is appropriate first-line for FAI; most resolve with progressive loading over 3 months [T1: FAI clinical practice evidence].
- **evidence:**
  1. PMC (2025) "Review of Femoroacetabular Impingement Syndrome." FAI defined; diagnosis; conservative vs surgical decision framework; PT efficacy.
  2. PMC (2024) "Management of FAI Syndrome: Current Insights." Modify high-impact activities; avoid weighted excessive flexion/torsion; progressive hip-muscle training 3+ months.
  3. Physio-pedia (2024) "FAI Clinical Guidelines." Three RCTs show PT non-inferior to surgery at 2 years; fewer surgical patients return to prior performance.
- **confidence:** HIGH (T1 clinical guidelines established; PT vs surgery evidence robust; FAI-specific movement modifications well-documented)

### Adductor/groin problems

- **aliases:** adductor strain, groin strain, adductor tendinopathy, adductor longus pain, adductor-related groin pain
- **variability:** Acute adductor strain (muscle tear) vs chronic adductor-related groin pain (ARGP) vs osteitis pubis differ in timeline and imaging; single-injury vs cumulative overuse differ in pathology [T3: groin pain reviews].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Hip adduction against resistance (Copenhagen squeeze, adductor machine): directly loads adductor; early-stage avoidance typical; progressive loading enables recovery [T5: practical]
  - Loaded rotation with adduction (asymmetrical lunges, rotational carry): compound stress; avoided acute phase [T5: practical]
  - Heavy deadlift (wide stance vs narrow stance): stance width modulates adductor demand; narrower stance higher adductor load; width modification initial strategy [T5: practical]
  - Rapid hip flexion-adduction (high-knee drill, explosive side lunges): high-speed adductor stress; avoided acute phase; slow, controlled progressions preferred [T5: practical]
  - Bilateral symmetrical leg work (back squat, front squat): generally tolerated; unilateral (Bulgarian split squat, single-leg) may expose asymmetry [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Adductor strain typically allows lower-body work continuation with width or stance modification; unopposed adduction avoidance; athletes continue upper-body, posterior-chain, core unrestricted [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (ultrasound, MRI) for muscle tear, tendon degeneration, or pubic symphysis involvement (osteitis pubis); strength asymmetry testing; progressive loading criteria; return-to-sport decision (sport-specific adductor demand).
- **red-flag-note:** No well-supported red-flag guidance for adductor strain identified; persistent symptoms beyond 8 weeks warrant imaging to exclude pubic symphysitis or atypical pathology [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Adductor strain typically resolves within weeks with load modification; early progression to light adductor strengthening aids recovery [T5: practical].
  2. Hip-adductor and hip-abductor balanced strengthening prevents chronic recurrence [T5: practical].
  3. Stance width and movement variation manage adductor load; progressive challenge to aggravating postures (within tolerance) builds tissue resilience [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. No formal T1/T2 clinical guidelines on adductor strain in strength training identified.
  2. Sports medicine consensus: acute adductor strain managed conservatively 2-4 weeks; progressive return-to-sport thereafter.
  3. Training practice: stance modification, avoided adduction loading, and progressive strengthening common accommodations.
- **confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM (no formal guidelines in strength-training context; evidence limited to sports medicine consensus and training experience; condition-specific nuance varies)

### Hip loading restrictions

- **aliases:** hip load intolerance, hip pain with loading, hip pain with weight bearing, hip stability deficit pain
- **variability:** Strength deficit (weakness causing load intolerance) vs pain (guarding, fear-avoidance) vs biomechanical restriction differ in underlying mechanism and recovery strategy [T1: FAI guidelines; T5: practical].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Single-leg stance and loading (Bulgarian split squat, single-leg deadlift, single-leg press): high hip-stability demand; often aggravating if weakness/pain-driven guarding present; bilateral initial, progressive to unilateral [T5: practical]
  - Heavy bilateral squat or deadlift: often tolerated better than unilateral in acute phase; progressive unilateral tolerance builds with time [T1: FAI case studies]
  - Loaded carry (single-arm, rotational): high hip-stability demand; reduced load or duration initially; progressive advancement [T5: practical]
  - Speed/power (explosive squat, jump, dynamic lunge): higher neural demand and force-production requirement; avoided early; progressive speed increase enables tolerance [T5: practical]
  - Controlled, slow movement: generally tolerated better; progressive tempo increase as tolerance improves [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Bilateral, controlled, moderate-load movements enable continued training; progressive unilateral and speed challenge enables return to full capacity [T1: FAI case studies].
- **clinician-territory:** Hip-strength testing (abduction, adduction, extension, flexion); movement screening for asymmetry; muscle activation deficits assessment; progressive loading clearance.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive hip pain with loading despite conservative management, or imaging evidence of severe osteoarthritis/labral tear, may warrant specialist consultation [T1: FAI guidelines].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Progressive hip-muscle strengthening (especially abductor and external rotator work) enables return to loaded single-leg movement [T1: FAI guidelines].
  2. Controlled bilateral movement progression to unilateral builds stability; neural adaptation typically precedes strength gain [T5: practical].
  3. Speed and power progressively return after strength and stability foundation; early-stage explosive work risks re-injury [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. FAI clinical practice guidelines emphasize strength training across all hip muscle groups; 3+ months, 2-3 sessions/week.
  2. Case studies (limited) show progressive unilateral loading after hip-strength foundation improves single-leg stability and tolerance.
  3. No formal return-to-loading criteria identified; progression individualised to strength/pain/motor-control recovery.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (guidelines address hip strengthening; training-specific loading criteria limited; case-based progression typical)

### Pectoral strain
- **aliases:** pec strain, pectoralis major tear
