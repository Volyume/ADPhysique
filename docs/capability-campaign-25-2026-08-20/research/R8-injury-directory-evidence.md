## 1. SHOULDER

### Rotator-cuff-related shoulder pain/tendinopathy

- **aliases:** rotator cuff tendinitis, RCT, subacromial pain syndrome, painful arc
- **variability:** Presentations vary significantly by cuff muscle involved (supraspinatus vs infraspinatus); overhead athletes show greater overload variants than occupational cases (T1: JOSPT 2025 guideline).
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Overhead pressing: commonly reported as aggravating, loading needs scapular upward rotation clearance [T1: JOSPT 2025]
  - Bench press: elbow angle and grip width modify rotator cuff load; narrower grip, scapula retraction reduce posterior shear force [T3: Frontiers Physiology 2024]
  - Internal/external rotation strength: deficits in eccentric strength are risk factors; training these movements protective [T3: PMC 2017]
  - Firm bar grip: high-grip tension aggravates cuff in early-stage rehab [T5: practical]
  - Horizontal adduction (cross-body presses): often restricted [T1: JOSPT 2025]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Progressive resistance training at load tolerance is Grade A evidence; athletes continue lower-body work unrestricted; unaffected shoulder movements continue with modification [T1: JOSPT 2025].
- **clinician-territory:** Load progression criteria; imaging indications (guideline discourages early imaging); sport-specific return criteria; individual pain-tolerance thresholds for load advance.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive neurological deficit, severe trauma, or night pain unresponsive to 3 months conservative care warrant imaging/specialist assessment [T1: JOSPT 2025].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Progressive loading at tolerable intensity builds cuff resilience; complete rest delays recovery [T1: JOSPT 2025].
  2. Scapula control during overhead movement clears subacromial space; coordination training is core to rehab [T1: JOSPT 2025].
  3. External and internal rotation strength imbalances increase injury risk; balanced training prevents recurrence [T3: PMC 2017].
- **evidence:**
  1. JOSPT (2025) "Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy Diagnosis, Nonsurgical Medical Care, and Rehabilitation." Grade A progressive resistance training; motor control exercises; individualization per pain tolerance.
  2. Frontiers Physiology (2024) "Effects of bench press technique variations on shoulder loads." Narrow grip, scapula retraction reduce rotator cuff stress.
  3. PMC (2017) "Increased Upper Trapezius Muscle Stiffness in Overhead Athletes." External/internal rotation strength protective.
- **confidence:** HIGH (T1 guideline exists; consistent cross-study findings; movement modification evidence abundant)

### Shoulder instability/dislocation history

- **aliases:** shoulder dislocation, glenohumeral instability, recurrent subluxation, apprehension with movement
- **variability:** Traumatic vs atraumatic; anterior, posterior, multidirectional variants present differently; hypermobility spectrum increases complexity [T1: CSM 2019; T3: Paediatric evidence 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Overhead position: end-range external rotation often triggers apprehension [T2: MASIP survey 2021]
  - Horizontal abduction (external rotation at 90/90): typically restricted early-stage [T2: MASIP 2021]
  - Firm bar grip under load: guarding common; kinetic-chain instability often co-present [T5: practical]
  - Heavy horizontal adduction with momentum: risky in early/mid-stage rehab [T5: practical]
  - Pulling movements (pull-ups, rows): require scapular/rotator cuff readiness [T2: MASIP 2021]
- **keep-training-evidence:** After 1-2 weeks immobilization, ROM exercises begin; resistance training only after full ROM restored; athletes can continue lower-body strength unrestricted [T1: CSM 2019].
- **clinician-territory:** Classification (traumatic/atraumatic); immobilization duration (no consensus in literature); rehabilitation timeline (unclear); return-to-sport decision (sport-dependent, athlete-dependent); surgical vs conservative choice.
- **red-flag-note:** Recurrent dislocation despite conservative rehab, or neurological compromise post-event, warrants specialist assessment [T1: CSM 2019].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Scapular and rotator cuff strengthening reduces recurrence risk more than joint protection alone [T1: CSM 2019].
  2. Progressive kinetic-chain control (core, hip stability) essential to shoulder stability during loading [T2: MASIP 2021].
  3. Proprioceptive training alongside strengthening improves long-term stability outcomes [T1: CSM 2019].
- **evidence:**
  1. CSM (2019) "Shoulder Stability and Movement Coordination Deficits CPG." Scapular/rotator cuff exercises; reassurance; kinetic-chain focus.
  2. MASIP Survey (2021) "Management of Atraumatic Shoulder Instability." No consensus on duration; multidisciplinary approach needed.
  3. PMC (2024) "Paediatric and Adolescent Shoulder Instability." Generalized hyperlaxity assessment; lifetime incidence history critical.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (guidelines exist but evidence sparse on specific exercise superiority; high recurrence rates indicate knowledge gaps)

### AC joint (acromioclavicular) problems

- **aliases:** AC joint pain, AC separation, distal clavicle pain, AC joint sprain/osteoarthritis
- **variability:** Acute sprain (grade I-III) vs chronic osteoarthritis; mechanical vs inflammatory presentations differ in training tolerance [T3: case reports 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Horizontal adduction (cross-body movements): direct AC stress; often aggravating in acute phase [T1: AC OA protocol; T3: case 2024]
  - Overhead pressing: typically restricted acutely due to AC joint compression [T5: practical]
  - Firm bar grip with horizontal adduction: high AC load [T3: Frontiers 2024]
  - Scapular instability: common co-impairment; compounds AC stress [T2: rehabilitation protocols]
  - Anti-rotational movements: lower AC demand; useful early-phase substitute [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Closed-chain exercises limit AC joint stress while permitting muscle activation; once mobility restored, overhead work progresses; lower-body training unrestricted [T3: rehabilitation case reports].
- **clinician-territory:** AC joint mobilization with movement (MWM) indications; imaging rationale (joint degeneration, comminution); surgical consultation criteria; load-progression benchmarks.
- **red-flag-note:** Severe traumatic AC joint injury (grade III+) or comminution may require specialist assessment [T3: case reports 2024].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. AC joint mobilisation with movement combined with strengthening reduces pain and disability more than exercise alone [T3: case 2024].
  2. Periscapular (scapular stabiliser) strengthening reduces AC joint stress during overhead movement [T2: rehabilitation protocols].
  3. Progressive overhead positioning from neutral to end-range allows tissue adaptation without guarding [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. Open Orthopaedics Journal (2023) "Rehabilitation for Shoulder Instability." Scapular stabilisation; closed-chain progressions.
  2. PMC (2024) "Multimodal Exercises for AC Joint Sprain." MWM + therapeutic exercise more effective than exercise alone.
  3. AC OA Protocol (source: OrthoArizona). Four-phase progression; pain-driven activity modification; periscapular focus.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (case evidence abundant; RCT evidence limited; load progressions not standardised)

### Labral/biceps-related considerations

- **aliases:** SLAP tear (superior labrum anterior posterior), labral tear, long head of biceps (LHB) tendinopathy, biceps-labral complex injury
- **variability:** SLAP type (I-IV) and athlete age drastically alter presentation; overhead athletes show high-force variants; older patients often show degenerative patterns [T3: case series 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Biceps-loading movements (chin-ups, pull-ups, heavy curls): directly load LHB tendon and labral attachment; early/mid-stage restrictions common [T2: rehab guidelines; T5: practical]
  - Overhead arm positioning (especially abduction + external rotation): common provocative posture [T3: SLAP tear studies]
  - Horizontal adduction with firm grip (bench press variants): cumulative biceps microtrauma with repeated sets [T3: case series 2024]
  - Front-raise movements: often tolerated better than overhead presses in early phase [T5: practical]
  - Alternating grip (overhand/underhand) in pressing: distributes LHB load; recommended modification [T3: athlete case reports]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Most SLAP tears (type I-II) improve conservative over 3-6 months; athletes continue lower-body, posterior-chain, and scapular work unrestricted; upper-body pressing continues with grip/loading modifications [T3: rehab case series].
- **clinician-territory:** SLAP classification (imaging/arthroscopy); conservative vs surgical decision (type-dependent, age-dependent, sport-dependent); sport-specific return criteria; biceps tenodesis vs repair vs conservative thresholds.
- **red-flag-note:** Persistent apprehension with overhead movement despite 6 weeks rehab, or progressive neurological signs, warrants specialist imaging [T3: case series 2024].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Biceps-dominant exercises (curls, chin-ups) load the labral attachment directly; early-stage modification or avoidance reduces traction strain [T2: rehab guidelines].
  2. Side-lying shoulder flexion minimises biceps activation whilst maintaining rotator cuff stimulus [T3: rehab case series].
  3. Grip-variation progression (overhand to neutral to underhand) distributes cumulative microtrauma across shoulder flexors [T3: athlete case reports].
- **evidence:**
  1. [P]rehab (2024) "Biceps Tendon Pain Exercises and Training Strategies." Avoid heavy curls/pull-ups early; side-lying raises; avoid end-range abduction/external rotation.
  2. BSR Physical Therapy (2020) "Shoulder Labrum Tears: Exercises Before Surgery." Type I-II conservative track; three-phase progression (ROM, rotator cuff, scapular stabilisation).
  3. PMC (2024) "Biceps Tenodesis for Type II SLAP Tears." Case series; improved outcomes post-surgical in overhead athletes under 35.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH (case evidence and rehab protocols abundant; RCT comparisons limited; sport-specific evidence strong for overhead athletes)

### Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis)

- **aliases:** frozen shoulder, adhesive capsulitis, primary frozen shoulder, stiff shoulder
- **variability:** Three phases (freezing, frozen, thawing) progress differently; diabetic or thyroid-associated cases show more severity; presentation can be insidious or post-traumatic [T1: clinical guidelines 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - All overhead positions: severely restricted; movement provokes pain; passive ROM often better tolerated than active [T1: JOSPT 2013, 2009]
  - External rotation: most limited direction; critical in early-stage assessment [T1: guidelines]
  - Floor access (hands-behind-back, floor touch): contraindicated early; end-range pain common [T1: guidelines]
  - Horizontal adduction: restricted; internal rotation more limited than external in some phases [T5: practical]
  - All weight-bearing on arms (push-ups, press-ups): prohibited early; floor access restricted [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Gradual, progressive ROM work is cornerstone; pain-limited active ROM gradually replaces passive; gentle contraction exercises alongside ROM; most resolve in 12-18 months [T1: clinical guidelines 2024].
- **clinician-territory:** Diagnosis confirmation (imaging, clinical tests); suprascapular nerve block/corticosteroid injection indications; hydrodilatation rationale; surgical intervention threshold (rare); progression-phase determination.
- **red-flag-note:** Rapidly progressive loss of ROM, or ROM loss post-injury without preceding pain phase, warrant imaging to exclude rotator cuff tear or post-traumatic stiffness [T1: guidelines].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Frozen shoulder evolves in phases; pain gradually reduces whilst ROM improves over months; active exercise enhances ROM phase [T1: guidelines 2024].
  2. Corticosteroid injection or nerve block may accelerate recovery; combined with physiotherapy shows better outcomes than either alone [T1: guidelines 2024].
  3. Gentle pendulum exercises, self-stretching, and contraction-relaxation techniques support gradual ROM recovery [T3: rehabilitation studies].
- **evidence:**
  1. JOSPT (2013, 2009) "Shoulder Pain and Mobility Deficits: Adhesive Capsulitis CPG." Stretching, mobilisation, exercises; gentle ROM progression; NSAIDs for pain.
  2. E-ARM (2024) "Clinical Practice Guidelines for Non-Surgical Treatment of Primary Frozen Shoulder." Phase-based progression; three phases recognised; 12-18 month resolution typical.
  3. Ochsner Journal (2024) "Frozen Shoulder: Evidence and Model." Corticosteroid + physiotherapy superior to either alone; ROM and pain both targeted.
- **confidence:** HIGH (clinical guidelines well-established; phase-based model consensus; long natural history allows clear evidence accumulation)

## 2. ELBOW/FOREARM

### Lateral elbow tendinopathy (tennis elbow)

- **aliases:** lateral epicondylitis, lateral elbow pain, extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendinopathy, lateral epicondylalgia
- **variability:** Occupational (repetitive gripping, keyboard) vs sport-specific (tennis, throwing) presentations differ in volume tolerance; age and dominance affect severity [T1: JOSPT 2022; T3: recent review 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Wrist extension with grip (e.g. racquet handle, barbell row grip): primary aggravating movement; load and volume directly correlate with pain [T1: JOSPT 2022]
  - Firm bar grip during pulling movements: common aggravation; lighter grip or strap use reduces ECRB demand [T5: practical]
  - Pronation under load (reverse curl, pull-ups underhand): stresses ECRB; grip variation beneficial [T5: practical]
  - Repetitive gripping (kettlebell swings, thick-bar work): cumulative stress; sets x reps matter [T5: practical]
  - Elbow extension work (triceps pushdowns, straight-arm pulldowns): lower lateral elbow demand; often tolerated [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Eccentric strengthening combined with manual therapy reduces pain 42% (VAS improvement); athletes continue lower-body unrestricted; posterior-chain work often tolerated if grip modified [T3: meta-analysis 2024].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging indications (ultrasound, MRI); corticosteroid injection timing and risk-benefit; manipulative therapy integration; load-progression thresholds for sport return.
- **red-flag-note:** Sudden neurological signs (wrist drop, finger weakness) suggest posterior interosseous nerve compression; imaging warranted [T1: JOSPT 2022].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Eccentric strengthening exercises reduce pain and improve function more reliably than passive modalities alone [T3: meta-analysis 2024].
  2. Grip-width and load-volume management enables continued training; complete rest delays recovery [T1: JOSPT 2022].
  3. Wrist extensor and forearm strengthening corrects muscle imbalances that predispose to lateral epicondylitis [T1: JOSPT 2022].
- **evidence:**
  1. JOSPT (2022) "Lateral Elbow Pain and Muscle Function Impairments CPG." Exercise and manual therapy core; eccentric strengthening Grade A; engagement best practices.
  2. PMC (2024) "Kinesio Taping Plus Exercise vs Sham." Eccentric training 42% pain reduction; superiority over passive modalities shown.
  3. Journal of Pioneering Medical Sciences (2025) "Advances in Lateral Epicondylitis." Diagnosis, management, rehabilitation review; eccentric exercise evidence strong.
- **confidence:** HIGH (T1 guideline recent; strong exercise evidence; high recurrence without targeted rehab)

### Medial elbow tendinopathy (golfer's elbow)

- **aliases:** medial epicondylitis, medial epicondylalgia, golfer's elbow, flexor-pronator tendinopathy, thrower's elbow
- **variability:** Occupational (typing, gripping) vs sport-specific (throwing, golf) differ in load pattern; acute vs chronic presentations differ in aggravation threshold [T3: UK review 2024].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Wrist flexion under load (weight in palm, flexion curls): primary aggravator; load and tempo both matter [T3: UK review 2024]
  - Pronation under load (reverse grip pressing, underhand barbell rows): flexor-pronator group involved; modification beneficial [T5: practical]
  - Firm grip with wrist flexion (kettlebell pressing, thick-bar curls): compound stress; grip aids reduce demand [T5: practical]
  - Throwing movements (if applicable): high-speed pronation-flexion compound; progressive return-to-throw needed [T2: overhead athlete protocols]
  - Wrist extension (reverse curls, wrist extension): lower demand; often tolerated as alternative [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** Structured stretching and strengthening (particularly eccentric) show moderate short-term benefit; most cases self-limit with activity modification; athletes continue unaffected movements [T1: StatPearls 2024; T3: case series].
- **clinician-territory:** Ultrasound imaging rationale; injection indications and timing; corticosteroid vs PRP decision; return-to-throwing phases (if athlete); load progression benchmarks.
- **red-flag-note:** Progressive weakness in grip or pronation, or neurological signs in hand, warrant imaging to exclude ulnar nerve compression [T3: UK review 2024].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Eccentric flexor-pronator strengthening and stretching show better outcomes than passive modalities; improvement takes weeks [T3: case series].
  2. Activity modification (grip technique, volume management) allows continued training whilst tendon adapts [T1: StatPearls 2024].
  3. Persistent symptoms beyond 3 months warrant specialist assessment to exclude nerve compression or partial tear [T1: StatPearls 2024].
- **evidence:**
  1. StatPearls (2024) "Medial Epicondylitis." Conservative management: activity modification, NSAIDs, physio; self-limited most cases.
  2. PMC (2024) "Eccentric Exercise Therapy for Medial Epicondylitis." Systematic review; eccentric exercise effective; stretching and strengthening core.
  3. PMC (2024) "Ultrasound in Differential Diagnosis of Medial Epicondylalgia." Imaging findings; narrative review; differential exclusion.
- **confidence:** HIGH (guidelines well-established; eccentric exercise evidence strong; occupational and sport variants recognised)

### Distal biceps tendinopathy/tear

- **aliases:** distal biceps tendinitis, biceps brachii strain, distal biceps tear, elbow flexor pain
- **variability:** Acute rupture (sudden loss of strength) vs chronic tendinopathy (progressive pain) present very differently; age (>50 years shows higher rupture risk) and load history affect presentation [T3: case literature].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Elbow flexion under load (curls, pulling, chin-ups): primary aggravation; load and speed both matter [T5: practical]
  - Supination under load (supinated grip during curl, vertical pulling): directly loads distal biceps; pronated grip spares tissue [T5: practical]
  - Firm grip combined with flexion (kettlebell curls, farmer's carries): compound stress [T5: practical]
  - Heavy eccentric flexion (lowering phase of curl): high-risk movement; progressive loading safer than shock loads [T5: practical]
  - Horizontal adduction (cross-body pressing): lower biceps demand; alternative often tolerated [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** No well-supported generalisation on whether partial tears heal conservatively vs require surgery; case series suggest some athletes return to training with load modification [T3: limited case reports].
- **clinician-territory:** Tear classification (partial vs complete, retraction degree); surgical vs conservative decision (depends on activity demands, age, patient preference); return-to-lifting criteria post-repair; strength recovery timeline (surgical repair typically 3-6 months).
- **red-flag-note:** Acute rupture presents with sudden "pop," loss of elbow flexion strength, and visible proximal muscle retraction; immediate specialist assessment needed (surgical window typically 2-3 weeks) [T3: case literature].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Distal biceps rupture is a surgical emergency; early repair (within 2-3 weeks) improves long-term strength outcomes [T3: case series].
  2. Partial tears may recover with progressive load management; supinated-grip loading should be avoided early [T5: practical].
  3. Post-repair, progressive supination and flexion strengthening over 3-6 months restores functional strength [T3: case series].
- **evidence:**
  1. [P]rehab (2024) "Biceps Tendon Pain Exercises and Training Strategies." Avoid heavy loading early; modify grip; avoid end-range pronation.
  2. PMC (limited case reports on conservative management; surgical repair literature more abundant but outside evidence scope here).
  3. Orthopedic case series consensus: rupture = surgery; partial tears = trial of conservative management with modification.
- **confidence:** LOW-MEDIUM (limited RCT evidence; mostly case reports; surgical management better-studied than conservative rehab; return-to-training evidence sparse)

### Triceps tendon considerations

- **aliases:** triceps tendinopathy, distal triceps strain, triceps tendinitis, elbow extension pain
- **variability:** Insertional tendinopathy (pain at olecranon insertion) vs mid-belly strain differ in palpation site; occupational (heavy lifting) vs sport-specific (throwing) differ in load pattern [T5: practical].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Elbow extension under load (triceps pushdowns, bench press lockout, overhead press): direct triceps demand; load and tempo both matter [T5: practical]
  - Heavy eccentric extension (lowering phase of dips, press-down): highest mechanical stress; progressive loading safer [T5: practical]
  - Closed-chain pressing (dips, bench press): higher total triceps load than open-chain [T5: practical]
  - Narrow grip pressing: shifts stress medially (towards pectoralis); may reduce triceps but increase shoulder stress [T5: practical]
  - Rope attachments vs bars in triceps work: variation in triceps activation and hand/wrist stress [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** No well-supported generalisation; most triceps strains resolve within weeks with load modification; athletes continue lower-body and unaffected upper-body movements [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Tear classification (insertion vs mid-belly); surgical vs conservative decision (complete tears typically require surgery; partial tears conservative management trial); imaging indications.
- **red-flag-note:** Sudden loss of elbow extension strength or visible proximal muscle gap suggest triceps rupture; specialist assessment needed [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Triceps strain recovers faster than other upper-limb tendon injuries; progressive loading within tolerance enables rapid return [T5: practical].
  2. Hand position variation (grip width, pronation angle) modulates triceps activation; variation management aids continued training [T5: practical].
  3. Complete triceps rupture is rare but requires surgical repair for strength preservation [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. No T1/T2 clinical guidelines specific to triceps tendinopathy found in search; evidence synthesis from orthopedic case literature only.
  2. Standard orthopedic practice: complete ruptures (sudden strength loss) require surgery; partial/chronic strains trial conservative management with load modification.
  3. Case reports (limited) suggest 2-4 week recovery for partial strains with appropriate load management.
- **confidence:** LOW (no formal clinical guidelines; evidence limited to case reports and orthopedic practice norms; training-specific evidence absent)

### Forearm rotation restrictions (pronation/supination limitation)

- **aliases:** forearm pronation/supination loss, radioulnar joint restriction, forearm pronation/supiation dysfunction, pronation/supitation pain
- **variability:** Post-fracture (radius/ulna) vs inflammatory (synovitis) vs positional (muscle tightness) present differently; unilateral (dominant-arm overuse) vs bilateral (systemic) inform pathology [T5: practical].
- **movement-questions-evidence:**
  - Supination under load (supinated-grip pulls, curls with rotation): aggravating if supination restricted; grip modification beneficial [T5: practical]
  - Pronation during pressing (bench press, floor press): movement quality affected; compensation common [T5: practical]
  - Gripping with rotation (eg kettlebell cleaning, bottoms-up carries): combines pronation/supitation with load; avoided early-stage [T5: practical]
  - Rapid pronation/supitation cycling (eg shadow boxing): high velocity stresses radioulnar joint; avoided acute phase [T5: practical]
  - Bilateral movements (symmetrical pressing) tolerated better than unilateral if one arm restricted [T5: practical]
- **keep-training-evidence:** No well-supported generalisation; post-fracture, supervised progressive ROM enables return to loaded training; athletes continue unaffected movement planes [T5: practical].
- **clinician-territory:** Imaging (X-ray, CT) for post-fracture healing status; synovitis assessment; interosseous membrane integrity post-injury; progressive ROM clearance; load-return criteria.
- **red-flag-note:** Post-fracture neurological signs (weakness, sensory change in forearm/hand) suggest nerve injury (posterior interosseous, anterior interosseous); imaging and specialist assessment needed [T5: practical concern].
- **education-candidates:**
  1. Pronation and supination ROM recovers gradually post-fracture; progressive resistance within ROM improves tissue strength [T5: practical].
  2. Grip-variation training (neutral, pronated, supinated) distributes load; unilateral overload avoidance aids recovery [T5: practical].
  3. Post-fracture return to loaded forearm rotation typically takes 8-12 weeks; progression should be gradual and pain-guided [T5: practical].
- **evidence:**
  1. Post-fracture rehabilitation consensus (no T1/T2 guideline identified): progressive ROM, then resistance, then sport-specific loading.
  2. Orthopedic practice: synovitis managed conservatively; radioulnar joint mobility critical to loaded function.
  3. Training literature: limited evidence on return-to-lifting forearm rotation tolerance; case-based progression typical.
- **confidence:** LOW (no formal clinical guidelines; evidence limited to post-fracture orthopedic norms and training experience; training-specific evidence absent)
