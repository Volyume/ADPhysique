# R5 - Population-Specific Resistance-Training Evidence Survey

**Agent:** R5 (research, read-only)
**Date:** 2026-08-20
**Campaign:** capability-campaign-25-2026-08-20
**Purpose:** Survey the population-specific resistance-training (RT) evidence honestly, so
the product knows where a Layer 2 population-informed curated routine is supportable, where
capability-led programming (Layer 1) is the only defensible approach, and where anything
beyond respecting user-declared restrictions would cross into rehabilitation or medicine.

**This report classifies evidence. It does not decide product scope.** Verdict lines are an
evidence classification only. Every verdict remains subject to the Section 2 inviolables and
to founder/lead product rulings.

---

## Tag key

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE** | Supported by peer-reviewed systematic review, meta-analysis, or a formally graded guideline. |
| **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS** | Named professional/clinical body recommendation, but not itself a graded evidence synthesis. |
| **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE** | Charity, advocacy or national disability-sport body practice resource. Practitioner wisdom, not graded evidence. |
| **PRODUCT INFERENCE** | My reasoning about what this means for a fitness product. Not a finding from the source. |
| **NEEDS CLINICAL REVIEW** | Real clinical risk or boundary. Must not be encoded in product behaviour without qualified clinical sign-off. |

## Verification levels used below

Because several key papers sit behind publisher redirects or paywalls, I distinguish:

- **[READ]** - I fetched and read the source page/document directly.
- **[SECONDARY]** - I could not fetch the primary text; the figure comes from search-engine
  summarisation of the source and/or corroborating pages. Treat the wording as approximate
  and re-verify before it is quoted publicly or drives a safety decision.

I have not paraphrased a **[SECONDARY]** source into a stronger claim than the snippet
supported. Where two independent secondary routes agreed, I say so.

## 1. UK CMO guidance for disabled adults, and Activity Alliance

### 1a. Founder's belief: verified, but with an important correction

**Founder's stated belief:** a 2026 refresh of the UK CMO physical activity guidelines says
strength/balance at least twice weekly for disabled adults.

**Finding: the 2026 refresh is REAL and the twice-weekly strength/balance line is REAL, but
the twice-weekly line is NOT new in 2026. It has been the disabled-adults recommendation
since 7 September 2019.** [READ]

Verified facts:

- **A refreshed UK CMO physical activity guidelines set was published on 10 July 2026.** The
  GOV.UK guidelines page carries "Last updated: 10 July 2026" and is labelled a 2026 update
  of the 2019 document. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE (national guidance).**
  (UK Chief Medical Officers, 2026. *UK Chief Medical Officers' physical activity guidelines*.
  https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/physical-activity-guidelines-uk-chief-medical-officers-report/uk-chief-medical-officers-physical-activity-guidelines)

- **Adults 19-64, general:** "Muscle strengthening activities should be done at least 2 days a
  week, but any strengthening activity is better than none", using "all major muscle groups,
  including upper and lower body". **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [READ]

- **Disabled adults specifically:** the disabled-adults infographic text states "Do strength
  and balance activities on at least 2 days per week", alongside "aim for at least 150
  minutes each week of moderate intensity activity". **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [READ]
  (Department of Health and Social Care, *Physical activity for disabled adults: text of the
  infographic*, updated 16 July 2026.
  https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/physical-activity-guidelines-disabled-adults/physical-activity-for-disabled-adults-text-of-the-infographic)

- **The correction.** The disabled-adults publication page records: **first published 7
  September 2019**; updated 10 July 2026 ("Replaced the infographic with an updated version
  to reflect the physical activity guidelines"); updated 16 July 2026 ("Added Welsh language
  version"). So the twice-weekly strength and balance recommendation for disabled adults
  dates from **2019**, was carried through the 2026 refresh, and is current. [READ]
  (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/physical-activity-guidelines-disabled-adults)

- **What actually changed in 2026** is emphasis, not the strength dose: far greater weight on
  light activity, on building movement into everyday life, on reducing prolonged sitting, and
  on "any activity is better than none"; explicit statements that benefits outweigh risks for
  most disabled people and people with long-term conditions; and explicit recognition of
  wheeling, household tasks and active travel as counting activity. The press release also
  notes the strength-and-balance evidence "has strengthened", including for people on
  GLP-1 agonists. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE (national guidance).** [READ]
  (DHSC press release, 10 July 2026.
  https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-uk-cmo-activity-guidelines-show-every-movement-counts)

- **Provenance of the disabled-adults infographic.** The 2011 CMO guidelines "omitted
  disabilities at any age"; in 2018 Public Health England tasked Get Yourself Active
  (Disability Rights UK) to review the evidence and co-produce recommendations, noting
  "sparse physical activity evidence on disability at the time". The infographic is
  explicitly a **co-produced** artefact. **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE** (about a piece of national
  guidance). [READ] (https://getyourselfactive.org/resource/cmo-physical-activity-guidelines/)

**PRODUCT INFERENCE.** The national base is a *dose*, not a *template*: at least two days a
week of strength for disabled adults, all major muscle groups, any amount better than none.
It contains nothing that specifies which exercises, which impairment, or which adaptation.
It supports a capability-led core directly and supports no population template on its own.

### 1b. Activity Alliance: what the 2025-26 survey actually reports

**Finding: the claimed 2025-26 annual survey exists and is published.** [READ]
(Activity Alliance, *Annual Disability and Activity Survey 2025-26*, fieldwork
September-October 2025, conducted by IFF Research; results webinar 29 July 2026.
https://www.activityalliance.org.uk/how-we-help/research/annual-survey)

Figures I verified on Activity Alliance's own pages. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE (population
survey; self-report).**

From the 2025-26 survey: [READ]
- 52% of disabled people feel they have the opportunity to be as active as they want, vs 74%
  of non-disabled people.
- 46% report reduced activity because of cost-of-living increases.
- 46% of disabled people say it is easy to physically access outdoor spaces, vs 81% of
  non-disabled people.
- 57% of disabled people would like to be more active (vs 54% non-disabled).
- Activity setting preferences: green space 39%, home/online 31%, blue space 28%, indoor
  leisure centre 28%.
- Poorer wellbeing outcomes and higher anxiety than non-disabled people; 68% of those
  experiencing loneliness believe activity could help.
- Preferred sources of advice on sport and activity are **healthcare professionals**: physios
  and OTs 38%, specialist doctors/consultants 38%, GP or nurse 33%. Information is most often
  found online (53%), then personal contacts (38%), then sport/leisure organisations (37%).
- The survey page characterises disabled people as choosing activity settings "less on
  preference alone and more on what felt manageable, predictable and safe".

Adjacent context from Activity Alliance's facts page: [READ]
- Sport England Active Lives Adult Survey (Nov 2024-25): "Disabled adults are almost twice as
  likely to be physically inactive (doing less than 30 minutes of activity per day) than
  non-disabled adults (39.3% vs 19.7%)."
- From the 2023-24 survey (still quoted as current on that page): 79% of disabled people say
  their impairment or health condition stops them being as active as they would like; 20% had
  taken part in an organised activity session in the last year; 66% are motivated to be active
  to improve or maintain physical health.

**Caveat I must flag.** Activity Alliance's own facts page mixes 2023-24 and 2025-26 figures
on the same screen, and one search route attributed the "one in five (20%) organised activity"
figure to 2025-26 while the facts page attributes it to 2023-24. **Do not cite a year against
an Activity Alliance number without re-opening the specific report.** [READ]

**Nothing in the Activity Alliance material I read reports on strength training, gyms as a
modality, or resistance-exercise preferences specifically.** That is a gap, not a silence I
can interpret. **PRODUCT INFERENCE:** the "manageable, predictable and safe" finding and the
31% home/online preference are the most product-relevant lines, and they argue for
predictability and home-viability in UX rather than for population templates.

### 1c. Verdict line - national base layer

> **Capability-led programming is the defensible approach; population layer limited to
> education/UX.** The UK CMO base prescribes a dose (strength and balance on at least 2 days
> per week, all major muscle groups) with explicitly no impairment-specific content, and was
> co-produced precisely because impairment-specific evidence was sparse; it therefore
> authorises a capability-led core and authorises no population template by itself.

## 2. Spinal cord injury (chronic)

### Current guideline version

**Martin Ginis, K.A., van der Scheer, J.W., Latimer-Cheung, A.E., et al. (2018).
"Evidence-based scientific exercise guidelines for adults with spinal cord injury: an update
and a new guideline." *Spinal Cord*, 56(4), 308-321.**
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41393-017-0017-3

**This 2018 paper is still the current international scientific version.** I searched for a
post-2018 revision and found none. What I found instead were downstream products of it:
a Canadian community/clinical translation (Martin Ginis et al., 2020, PubMed 31949284), a
2023 physiological-considerations review (below), a 2025 consensus statement on how to
*design and report* SCI exercise research (Arch Phys Med Rehabil, April 2025), and an
implementation trial protocol (Spinal Cord, 2021). None of those replace the 2018 dose.
**ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY for the paper itself - nature.com redirects to an
identity provider; corroborated by three independent routes below.]

### The resistance-training recommendation, verbatim as reported by corroborating sources

**Fitness guideline:** "at least 20 min of moderate to vigorous intensity aerobic exercise 2
times per week AND 3 sets of strength exercises for each major functioning muscle group, at a
moderate to vigorous intensity, 2 times per week."

**Cardiometabolic health guideline:** at least 30 min of moderate to vigorous intensity
aerobic exercise 3 times per week.

Corroboration: SCIRE Community's guidelines page (updated 30 June 2023) quotes "3 sets of
strength exercises for each major functioning muscle group, at a moderate to vigorous
intensity, 2 times per week" [READ]; the 2023 *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism*
review restates the same progression [SECONDARY]; a third search route returned identical
wording [SECONDARY]. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.**

**Note a real difference from the 2011 predecessor.** The 2011 guideline specified "3 sets of
8-10 repetitions". The 2018 update drops the explicit rep range and specifies sets, muscle
groups, intensity and frequency only. Several secondary pages still quote the 2011 "8-10
reps" wording as if current. **If a dossier is written, the rep range must be sourced to 2011
or omitted, not attributed to 2018.** [SECONDARY, but consistently so across sources.]

### Population the guideline actually covers

"adults (aged 18-64) with chronic SCI (at least one year post-onset), neurological level of
injury C3 and below, from traumatic or non-traumatic causes, including tetraplegia and
paraplegia." (SCIRE Community, updated 30 June 2023.
https://community.scireproject.com/topic/exercise-guidelines/) **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [READ]

That scope statement is doing a lot of work: it excludes acute/subacute SCI, excludes over-65s,
and excludes injury above C3.

### Evidence strength

The underlying systematic review screened 211 studies (22 acute, 189 chronic); for chronic
SCI, GRADE confidence ratings were **moderate to high** for evidence that exercise improves
all reviewed outcomes **except bone health**. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY]

### What is generalisable vs what is individual

Generalisable: the *dose shape* (2x/week, 3 sets, major functioning muscle groups, moderate to
vigorous) and the conclusion that exercise improves fitness and cardiometabolic health in
chronic SCI.

Individual: **which muscle groups are "functioning"**. That is set by neurological level and
completeness of injury, and it is the single largest determinant of what any SCI user can
actually train. The guideline itself encodes this by saying "each major **functioning** muscle
group" rather than naming muscle groups. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE (guideline wording).**

**PRODUCT INFERENCE.** The guideline is unusually template-friendly for a disability
population, but the phrase "functioning muscle group" is precisely a capability query, not a
population constant. A defensible SCI routine is a capability-led routine with an SCI-derived
dose, not a fixed exercise list.

### Clinical boundaries

- **Autonomic dysreflexia. NEEDS CLINICAL REVIEW.** Occurs in individuals with SCI above T6,
  with greater risk at higher cervical levels; an uncoordinated, sudden, exaggerated
  sympathetic response that can cause life-threatening hypertension. SCIRE states individuals
  with cervical or high thoracic injuries "should be aware of warning signs during exercise".
  **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE** for the phenomenon [SECONDARY for the review; READ for SCIRE].
  (Hutchinson, M.J. et al. / see: *Exercise prescription for persons with spinal cord injury:
  a review of physiological considerations and evidence-based guidelines*, Applied Physiology,
  Nutrition, and Metabolism, 2023. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37816259/)
- **Thermoregulatory dysfunction. NEEDS CLINICAL REVIEW.** More common in tetraplegia; impaired
  sweating and vasoconstriction mean higher core temperatures when exercising in warm
  environments and inability to warm in cool ones. Same source. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.**
  [SECONDARY]
- **Upper-body overuse injury, skin breakdown, overheating.** SCIRE names these as the
  SCI-specific concerns and states risks "are minimal when managed in consultation with a
  health care professional who is knowledgeable in SCI". **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE / PROFESSIONAL
  CONSENSUS.** [READ]

### Verdict line

> **Population-informed curated routine plausibly supportable (with dossier).** A formally
> GRADE-appraised international guideline states an explicit resistance dose (3 sets per major
> functioning muscle group, moderate to vigorous, twice weekly) for a defined population
> (chronic SCI, 18-64, C3 and below), which is the strongest population-specific RT
> prescription found in this survey; the dossier must carry the population scope verbatim, must
> treat "functioning muscle group" as a capability input rather than a fixed list, and must
> route autonomic dysreflexia and thermoregulation to clinical review rather than encode them.

## 3. Wheelchair users generally (distinct from SCI)

### Heterogeneity: "wheelchair user" is not a clinical population

- Around **1.2 million wheelchair users in the UK**, of whom roughly two thirds are regular
  users and roughly a third (about 400,000) are **ambulatory** wheelchair users. **COMMUNITY
  GUIDANCE / secondary statistics.** [SECONDARY - figure is widely repeated across UK
  disability-sector sources; I did not reach an ONS/NHS primary table.]
- UK SCI prevalence is estimated at about **105,000**, with roughly 4,400 new cases a year.
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY, WheelPower citing Spinal Injuries Association data.
  https://www.wheelpower.org.uk/news/new-spinal-injury-data/]
- **Therefore SCI accounts for on the order of one in ten UK wheelchair users at most.** The
  remainder is stroke, MS, cerebral palsy, Parkinson's, severe osteoarthritis, hip fracture,
  rheumatoid arthritis, COPD, heart failure, cancer, amputation, and a long tail. **PRODUCT
  INFERENCE from the two figures above.** This is the central finding of this section: a
  "wheelchair users" routine would be aimed at a group whose members share a *seated posture*
  and share almost nothing else clinically.
- Up to a third of children and adults with cerebral palsy are non-ambulant and use manual or
  powered chairs. **[SECONDARY]**
- Manual and powered chair use are materially different training situations, and the ambulatory
  wheelchair user (a third of the population) may train standing. **PRODUCT INFERENCE.**

### The one genuine cross-cutting programming input: shoulder health

- Shoulder pain prevalence is roughly **44% in the general wheelchair population and 40-78% in
  wheelchair athletes**; one large Danish SCI cohort reported 63% with shoulder pain in the
  past three months. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY]
- Imaging studies report **rotator cuff tears in around 60%** of manual wheelchair users with
  SCI, and **rotator cuff tendinopathy in 73-100%** on MRI or ultrasound; tear prevalence
  roughly four times an age- and sex-matched control group (63% vs 15%). **ESTABLISHED
  EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY]
- **Exercise is part of the answer, not a contraindication.** A scoping review of conservative
  treatment reports most interventions were strengthening and stretching programmes using
  bands or weights, with good short-term benefit on shoulder pain but long-term effects not
  evaluated. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE with an explicit durability gap.** [SECONDARY]
  (Mason, B., Warner, M., Briley, S., Goosey-Tolfrey, V., Vegter, R., 2020. "Managing shoulder
  pain in manual wheelchair users: a scoping review of conservative treatment interventions."
  *Clinical Rehabilitation*. https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0269215520917437)
  See also Nature/*Spinal Cord* 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis on treatment of
  shoulder pain in manual wheelchair users with SCI:
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41393-021-00673-x
- The Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine / Paralyzed Veterans of America clinical practice
  guideline *Preservation of Upper Limb Function Following Spinal Cord Injury* (2005) is the
  reference document; its thrust is **minimising repetitive upper-extremity load** (fewer
  transfers, fewer propulsion strokes, power chairs where appropriate). **PROFESSIONAL
  CONSENSUS.** [SECONDARY] (https://pva.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/cpg_upperlimb.pdf)

**PRODUCT INFERENCE, stated as inference.** There is real tension between "the upper limb is
also this person's legs, so protect it" and "strengthen the shoulder girdle to protect it".
Resolving that tension into specific programming rules (pressing volume caps, pull-to-push
ratios, overhead-work policy) is a clinical judgement this evidence does not make for us.

### Best-available cross-condition evidence synthesis

**Selph, S.S., Skelly, A.C., Wasson, N., et al. (2021). *Physical Activity and the Health of
Wheelchair Users: A Systematic Review in Multiple Sclerosis, Cerebral Palsy, and Spinal Cord
Injury*. Comparative Effectiveness Review No. 241. Rockville (MD): Agency for Healthcare
Research and Quality.** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK574878/ **ESTABLISHED
EVIDENCE.** [READ]

What it found: physical activity was associated with improvements in walking ability, general
function, balance (including fall risk), depression, sleep, activities of daily living,
aerobic capacity and female sexual function, but "improvements varied by condition and
activity type". Limitations named: "small sample sizes; few trials (in spinal cord injury);
inadequate descriptions of population characteristics, control group activities, and intensity
of physical activity". Literature was dominated by MS (44% of studies) with SCI at 18%. "No
studies reported long-term cardiovascular or metabolic disease health outcomes."

Two things this report notably does **not** do, and I checked: it does **not** isolate
resistance training as a separate modality with its own strength-of-evidence rating, and it
does **not** claim its conclusions generalise to wheelchair users beyond the three conditions
it studied. [READ]

### Practice resources that exist for wheelchair-based gym strength training

- **NCHPAD** (US National Center on Health, Physical Activity and Disability): seated upper-body
  strength sessions, "Discover Accessible Fitness" wheelchair users' guide to gym equipment,
  wheelchair fitness testing protocols, and a Power Wheelchair Exercise Series with Lakeshore
  Foundation. Practical notes include chest straps for trunk stability, difficulty maintaining
  form on overhead work, arm ergometers with removable seats, grip aids and straps.
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY] https://www.nchpad.org/resources/upper-body-workout-for-wheelchair-users-seated-strength-training/
- **WheelPower** (UK national wheelchair sport body, Stoke Mandeville): "Getting started with
  health and fitness" restates the national dose ("at least 150 minutes", "muscle-strengthening
  activity on two or more days"), advises building from 10-minute sessions, recommends
  yoga/tai chi/pilates/swimming, and closes with "Always seek advice from your GP, Physio, or a
  trained exercise professional regarding the type and amount of physical activity and
  exercise you should be doing." It cites "The Department of Health" and nothing else.
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [READ] https://www.wheelpower.org.uk/getting-started/getting-started-health-and-fitness/
- **Back Up Trust** (UK, SCI): wheelchair skills training rather than gym programming.
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY]
- **Mount Sinai Bryce Lab** SCI Adaptive and Inclusive Workout Guide. **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.**
  [SECONDARY]

**Honest characterisation:** these are exercise libraries and access guides. None of them is a
progressive, load-managed strength programme with a dose rationale. The UK material is thinner
than the US material.

### Verdict line

> **Capability-led programming is the defensible approach; population layer limited to
> education/UX.** "Wheelchair user" describes a posture and an equipment context shared by
> roughly 1.2 million UK people whose underlying conditions differ completely, and the best
> cross-condition synthesis (AHRQ CER 241) explicitly declines to generalise beyond the three
> conditions it studied and does not rate resistance training separately; the seated context
> and shoulder-load history are capability and history inputs, not a population.

## 4. Upper-limb loss / difference

### Peer-reviewed RT evidence

**There is essentially none specific to resistance training in upper-limb amputation.**

- A systematic review of rehabilitation in upper-extremity amputees found that **only one
  included study was a randomised controlled trial**; the remainder were case reports and
  non-controlled clinical series. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE that the evidence base is thin.**
  [SECONDARY] (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5103156/)
- The one RCT identified (Ulger et al.) concerned proprioceptive and dynamic/isometric exercise
  for **phantom limb pain** in prosthetic rehabilitation, not strength outcomes. **[SECONDARY]**
- A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of exercise in limb amputation (BMC Musculoskeletal
  Disorders, https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-025-09128-3) states there is "still no consensus on
  the most effective training protocols or how to tailor interventions for diverse subgroups"
  and calls for large multicentre RCTs. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE about the gap.** [SECONDARY]
- The lower-limb literature dominates; upper-limb amputation is a recognised gap. **[SECONDARY,
  consistent across multiple search routes.]**

### Community and practice resources (this is where the actual content lives)

- **LimbPower** (UK charity for amputees and people with limb difference) is the strongest UK
  source. It publishes a dedicated **Gym Resource Guide** (PDF), a video toolkit of 62 exercise
  videos produced with Sport England split across Introduction and Warm Up, Agility/Balance/
  Coordination, Dynamic Exercises, Strength and Conditioning, and Circuit Training and
  Stretching, plus **ReVAMP**, a 12-week online adapted circuit and nutrition programme, and
  twice-weekly online "Adaptive Fitness Fun" sessions for young people with limb difference.
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY - the Gym Resource Guide PDF would not convert to text in
  this environment; I verified its existence and the resource inventory from LimbPower's own
  pages but did NOT read the guide's contents. **Anyone building on it must read the PDF.**]
  https://www.limbpower.com/resources | https://www.limbpower.com/exercise/toolkit-videos
  | Gym Resource Guide: https://www.limbpower.com/application/themes/newTheme/download/files/GYM_Resource_Guide_9.pdf
- Prosthetics-industry practice sources (Arm Dynamics, MCOP) describe activity-specific
  prostheses for weightlifting, band-based "tug of war" resistance work, shoulder shrugs, and
  the argument that training both the sound and affected side prevents overuse injury.
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE (commercial provider), lowest tier of the sources here.** [SECONDARY]

### Unilateral and prosthetic considerations

- **Asymmetric load and sound-side overuse** is the recurring practical theme across community
  sources. I found **no** peer-reviewed quantification of overuse-injury risk in upper-limb
  amputees performing resistance training. **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE only; the underlying risk claim
  is NOT established evidence.**
- **Prosthetic vs non-prosthetic training** differ materially in what the source material
  describes: a prosthesis may permit bilateral barbell work via activity-specific terminal
  devices, whereas non-prosthetic training is band-, cable-, machine- and unilateral-dumbbell
  based, with grip and attachment as the limiting factor. **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY]
- **PRODUCT INFERENCE.** Almost everything real here is an *equipment and grip* question
  (attachment method, terminal device, cuff, strap) and a *loading-symmetry* question. Both are
  capability attributes the Layer 1 core would already need. The population adds context, not
  a distinct dose.

### Verdict line

> **Capability-led programming is the defensible approach; population layer limited to
> education/UX.** The peer-reviewed base is a single RCT on phantom limb pain inside a
> systematic review otherwise made of case reports, with 2025 reviewers stating there is no
> consensus on protocols; everything useful that exists is charity practice guidance about grip,
> attachment and symmetry, which are capability attributes rather than a population dose.

## 5. Lower-limb loss / difference

### Peer-reviewed RT evidence: exists, and is honest about being weak

**Rosario, M.L.V.V., Costa, P.B., da Silveira, A.L.B., Florentino, K.R.C., Casimiro-Lopes, G.,
Pimenta, R.A., Dias, I., Bentes, C.M. (2023). "Effects of Resistance Training in Individuals
with Lower Limb Amputation: A Systematic Review." *Journal of Functional Morphology and
Kinesiology*, 8(1), 23.** https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk8010023 **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [READ]

- 10 eligible studies from 156 screened. **Only 3 RCTs**; remainder case studies, pilot studies
  and quasi-experimental designs. Sample sizes 1 to 63.
- Protocols: 3-20 weeks (most commonly 8), 1-3 sessions/week (50% at 2x/week), 30 min to 2 h
  per session, 1-3 sets, 10-15 reps. **Only two studies reported intensity at all** (10-RM).
- Authors attribute the protocol variability to a "lack of standardized guidelines in the
  studies", noting the absence of ACSM amputation-specific guidance.
- Outcomes improved: strength, balance confidence, gait speed and pattern, and reduced chronic
  low back pain severity. Transtibial amputees improved more than transfemoral.
- **The decisive limitation, in the authors' own words:** 70% of studies combined RT with
  aerobic, flexibility and balance work, so "it is impossible to identify resistance training as
  the main factor responsible for these benefits".
- Authors also cite "poor methodological quality of the majority of the studies", missing
  protocol detail, small heterogeneous samples, and short durations compromising replicability.

Adjacent: Abou, L. et al. (2022), "The Effectiveness of Exercise Interventions to Improve Gait
and Balance in Individuals with Lower Limb Amputations: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis",
*Clinical Rehabilitation*, https://doi.org/10.1177/02692155221086204 **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.**
[SECONDARY]; and *Arch Phys Med Rehabil* 2023, "Do Exercise Programs Improve Fitness, Mobility,
and Functional Capacity in Adults With Lower Limb Amputation? A Systematic Review on the Type
and Minimal Dose Needed", https://www.archives-pmr.org/article/S0003-9993(23)00605-6/abstract
**[SECONDARY]**.

### Community and practice resources

- **LimbPower** as above (Gym Resource Guide, 62-video toolkit, ReVAMP 12-week programme).
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.**
- **NCHPAD** publishes a "Lower Limb Amputee 10 Minute Strength Routine". **COMMUNITY
  GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY]

### Unilateral, prosthetic and level-of-amputation considerations

- **Level of amputation changes the response**: the systematic review reports transtibial
  amputees showed greater improvements than transfemoral. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [READ]
- **Prosthetic vs non-prosthetic training** is a real fork (loaded standing work with a
  prosthesis vs seated/machine/floor work without), but I found **no peer-reviewed comparison of
  training with versus without a prosthesis**. **Gap, stated as a gap.**
- Residual limb and socket tolerance, and skin integrity under load, appear only in community
  material. **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.**

### Verdict line

> **Capability-led programming is the defensible approach; population layer limited to
> education/UX.** A published systematic review exists but rests on 3 RCTs among 10 mostly
> low-quality studies with no reported intensity in 8 of them, and its authors state outright
> that resistance training cannot be isolated as the cause of the observed benefits, so there is
> no dose to template; amputation level, prosthesis use and socket tolerance are capability and
> equipment attributes the Layer 1 core must carry anyway.

## 6. Cerebral palsy (adults)

### Best current synthesis

**Bania, T.A., Taylor, N.F., Chiu, H.S., Charitaki, G. (2023). "What are the optimum training
parameters of progressive resistance exercise for changes in muscle function, activity and
participation in people with cerebral palsy? A systematic review and meta-regression."
*Physiotherapy*, 119, 1-16.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36696699/ **ESTABLISHED
EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY - ScienceDirect and PubMed both blocked; figures corroborated by PEDro's
independent summary of the same review, https://pedro.org.au/english/systematic-review-progressive-resistance-exercise-training-cerebral-palsy/]

- 20 reports of **16 RCTs, 504 participants**.
- **Low certainty** evidence that progressive resistance exercise (PRE) improves muscle strength
  versus no intervention.
- **High certainty** evidence (5 trials) that the strength improvement is maintained an average
  of **11 weeks** after training stops.
- **Moderate certainty evidence of NO difference** between PRE and no intervention for
  **activity and participation**.
- **Meta-regression found no association between training intensity or volume and muscle
  strength change.**
- No serious adverse events reported; PRE is safe in this population.

### The spasticity question

**Resistance training does not increase spasticity in cerebral palsy.** Reviews report no
negative effects such as reduced range of motion or increased spasticity, countering the older
clinical concern. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY, consistent across the Bania review
summary and the older Dodd/Taylor/Graham 2002 review,
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12161840/]

### Adults specifically

- Adult-specific CP trial evidence is **thin**. The RCT base is dominated by children and
  adolescents. Adult-specific work found: Andersson et al. (2003), "Adults with cerebral palsy
  benefit from participating in a strength training programme at a community gymnasium"
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15371025/); "Effects of Strength Training on Mobility in
  Adults with Cerebral Palsy: A Systematic Review" (*Disability and Health Journal*, 2016,
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1936657416300449), whose functional
  findings were inconsistent, with only 2 of 6 studies observing increased self-selected
  walking speed; and a 2024 scoping review of community-based physical activity interventions
  for adolescents and adults with **complex** CP (GMFCS IV-V),
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10952332/. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE with an
  explicit adult gap.** [SECONDARY]
- CP heterogeneity is structural: GMFCS I-V, spastic vs dyskinetic/athetoid vs ataxic,
  unilateral vs bilateral. The one adult athetoid-CP study located is a single-subject design.
  **PRODUCT INFERENCE:** GMFCS level alone spans "trains like a non-disabled adult with a
  balance caveat" to "non-ambulant, needs seated and supported work".

### UK community resources

- **CP Sport (Cerebral Palsy Sport)**, the leading UK CP disability-sport charity, publishes
  resources at https://cpsport.org/resources/ and advocacy content on the importance of regular
  exercise. **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY]
- **UP - The Adult Cerebral Palsy Movement**, https://upmovement.org.uk/live-well/being-active/
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY]
- **UCLH** patient information, "Exercise and activity for adults with cerebral palsy".
  **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS (NHS trust patient information).** [SECONDARY]

### Verdict line

> **Capability-led programming is the defensible approach; population layer limited to
> education/UX.** The meta-regression's central finding is that neither intensity nor volume
> predicted strength gain in CP, which actively argues against a CP-specific dose, and the same
> review found moderate-certainty evidence of no effect on activity or participation while the
> adult-specific trial base remains thin; the population's real value here is the safety message
> that resistance training does not worsen spasticity, which is education, not a template.

## 7. Multiple sclerosis

### RT evidence

MS has the **largest and most consistent RT evidence base of any population in this survey**.
**ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY throughout this subsection.]

- McManaman, C. et al. (2025). "Changes in walking speed following resistance training in
  people with multiple sclerosis: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *PM&R*.
  https://doi.org/10.1002/pmrj.13255 - **12 RCTs, 425 participants**;
  RT may improve walking speed. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11826970/
- "Is Resistance Training an Option to Improve Functionality and Muscle Strength in Middle-Aged
  People with Multiple Sclerosis? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis" (2024), *Journal of
  Clinical Medicine*, 13(5), 1378. https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/13/5/1378 - 12 RCTs,
  significant advantage for RT.
- "The effect of resistance training on quality of life in people with multiple sclerosis: A
  systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression", *The American Journal of Medicine*
  (2026), https://www.amjmed.com/article/S0002-9343(26)00271-8/fulltext - 13 studies; RT
  improved quality of life.
- AHRQ CER 241 (Selph et al., 2021) notes MS supplied **44% of the wheelchair-user physical
  activity literature**, the largest share of the three conditions studied. [READ]

### Charity position: there is no single "MS exercise"

- **MS Trust:** "Everyone's MS is different, and you know best how your MS affects what you can
  do." For fatigue it suggests focusing on "exercise to strengthen your muscles, or resistance
  exercise", starting with about two exercises done daily and adding "one or two repetitions
  for at least three days before you do anything more". **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [READ]
  https://mstrust.org.uk/information-support/exercise-ms/starting-exercise
- **MS Society** publishes *Physical activity and managing MS: Review of the evidence* (2020),
  https://www.mssociety.org.uk/sites/default/files/2021-01/Physical%20activity%20evidence%20review_2020.pdf,
  and an MS-and-exercise hub, https://www.mssociety.org.uk/living-with-ms/physical-and-mental-health/staying-active/ms-and-exercise
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [The MS Society pages returned HTTP 403 to this environment; I did
  **not** read them. **The founder's brief asked specifically for the MS Society position and I
  could not verify it directly.** The "no single MS exercise" framing is verified from the MS
  Trust, a different charity.]

### Programming-relevant factors, reported and tagged

- **Fatigue: do not push through.** "It is not a good idea to 'work through' fatigue - you may
  end up feeling worse, often for days afterwards", and recovery takes longer with MS.
  **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [READ, MS Trust] This is a clinically meaningful inversion of ordinary
  training advice and is the single most product-relevant line in this section.
- **Heat sensitivity.** Widely reported; advice is to cool the environment. One repeatedly cited

- Spasticity and balance are named as tailoring factors in MS strength-training practice

- A dose of "resistance training two to three times weekly" alongside 150 min/week appears in

### Verdict line

> **Population-informed curated routine plausibly supportable (with dossier).** Multiple
> independent 2024-2026 meta-analyses of 12-13 RCTs each show consistent RT benefit for

> dossier must carry the charity position that MS presentation varies person to person, and must
> encode the fatigue rule (never push through, expect delayed payback) and heat sensitivity as
> first-class programming modifiers rather than footnotes.

## 8. Parkinson's disease

### RT evidence

**This is the strongest combination of graded evidence and named professional prescription in
the survey.** **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE + PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS.** [SECONDARY throughout.]

- **Gollan, R., Ernst, M., Lieker, E., Caro-Valenzuela, J., Monsef, I., Dresen, A., Roheger, M.,
  Skoetz, N., Kalbe, E., Folkerts, A.-K. (2022). "Effects of Resistance Training on Motor- and
  Non-Motor Symptoms in Patients with Parkinson's Disease: A Systematic Review and
  Meta-Analysis." *Journal of Parkinson's Disease*.** https://doi.org/10.3233/JPD-223252
  - **18 studies.** RT versus passive controls showed a **large effect on muscle strength (SMD
    -0.84, 95% CI -1.29 to -0.39)**, plus significant improvement in motor impairment and
    mobility/balance, and improvement in depression and quality of life.
  - **Important nuance:** **no significant difference between RT and other active physical
    interventions** (including yoga). The benefit is against doing nothing, not against
    alternatives.
  - GRADE was used; I could not retrieve the per-outcome certainty ratings. **Flagged as
    unverified detail.**
- Supporting syntheses: lower-limb RT meta-analysis of RCTs (Frontiers in Neurology, 2020,
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2020.591605/full);
  network meta-analysis of aerobic and resistance training on motor symptoms
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9376630/); RT and muscular strength meta-analysis
  (PLOS One, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0132135).
- Consistently reported practical envelope: **moderate-intensity progressive RT, 2-3 times per
  week over 8-10 weeks**, with gains in strength, balance and motor symptoms in early to
  moderate PD. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY]

### Named professional prescription

**Parkinson's Foundation and American College of Sports Medicine (2021) exercise
recommendations for Parkinson's disease.** **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS.** [SECONDARY]
https://www.parkinson.org/about-us/news/parkinsons-foundation-american-college-of-sports-medicine-exercise-recommendations
| infographic: https://www.parkinson.org/sites/default/files/documents/parkinsons_exercise_recommendations_infographic_2025.pdf

- **Strength training: 2-3 non-consecutive days per week, at least 30 minutes per session,
  10-15 reps for major muscle groups, with a resistance, speed or power focus.**
- Aerobic: 3 days/week, at least 30 min/session. Balance/agility/multitasking: 2-3 days/week,
  daily integration if possible. Stretching: 2-3 days/week, daily most effective. Overall 150
  min/week moderate to vigorous.
- Two further recommendations that matter to a product: see a **PD-specialist physical
  therapist** for full functional evaluation, and **exercise during "on" periods**, when
  medication is working. **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS.** [SECONDARY]

### Verdict line

> **Population-informed curated routine plausibly supportable (with dossier).** A named
> professional body jointly with ACSM publishes an explicit, quantified strength prescription
> (2-3 non-consecutive days, 10-15 reps, major muscle groups, resistance/speed/power emphasis),
> backed by an 18-study meta-analysis showing a large effect on strength versus inactive
> controls; the dossier must carry the two honest caveats, that RT was not superior to other
> active interventions, and that the medication "on" period is part of the prescription.

## 9. Stroke (chronic phase, hemiparesis / long-term asymmetry)

### RT evidence

**Veldema, J., Jansen, P. (2020). "Resistance training in stroke rehabilitation: systematic
review and meta-analysis." *Clinical Rehabilitation*, 34(9), 1173-1197.**
https://doi.org/10.1177/0269215520932964 **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE.** [SECONDARY]

- **30 trials, 1,051 participants** (search to 30 April 2020).
- RT after stroke improves recovery, and is **superior to other therapies on muscular force and
  motor function**.
- **The authors' own limiting conclusion:** "the current evidence is insufficient for
  evidence-based rehabilitation". That is, benefit is established; **optimal parameters are
  not**. An active PLOS One protocol, "Optimal resistance exercise training parameters for
  stroke recovery" (https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0295680), exists precisely because
  that question is open.

Supporting:
- Ouellette, M.M. et al. (2004), "High-Intensity Resistance Training Improves Muscle Strength,
  Self-Reported Function, and Disability in Long-Term Stroke Survivors", *Stroke*.
  https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.str.0000127785.73065.34 - 12-week supervised
  high-intensity programme (bilateral leg press, unilateral paretic and non-paretic knee
  extension, ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion) in **chronic** stroke. **ESTABLISHED
  EVIDENCE**, and directly on the chronic phase this section asks about. [SECONDARY]
- Morris, S.L., Dodd, K.J., Morris, M.E. (2004), "Outcomes of progressive resistance strength
  training following stroke: a systematic review", *Clinical Rehabilitation*.
  https://doi.org/10.1191/0269215504cr699oa **[SECONDARY]**
- Combined aerobic and RT meta-analysis, *J Stroke Cerebrovasc Dis* (2019),
  https://www.strokejournal.org/article/S1052-3057(19)30582-8/abstract **[SECONDARY]**
- Strength training protocols in hemiparetic individuals post stroke: systematic review of 12
  accepted articles from 562 screened, concluding "wide variation in previously studied
  protocols makes the most suitable choice difficult in clinical practice".
  https://www.scielo.br/j/fm/a/CyRYBg4KNP6wrXVr4ynnJKM/?lang=en **[SECONDARY]**

### The spasticity question, again

**"Effects of Resistance Training on Spasticity in People with Stroke: A Systematic Review"
(2024), *Brain Sciences*, 14(1), 57.** https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3425/14/1/57 -
9 articles, 225 participants (mean age 59.4). RT was **superior to or at least equal to**
conventional therapy, other therapies or no intervention for spasticity, as well as function,
strength, gait and balance. Authors caution about protocol heterogeneity.
**ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE that RT does not worsen spasticity after stroke.** [SECONDARY]

### Asymmetric capability implications

- The Ouellette protocol is explicitly **per-side**: unilateral paretic and non-paretic knee
  extension were prescribed separately, alongside a bilateral leg press. That is the clearest
  signal in this survey that **the unit of prescription in hemiparesis is the limb, not the
  person.** **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE (from the protocol design).** [SECONDARY]
- **PRODUCT INFERENCE.** Asymmetry after stroke is continuous and individual: degree of paresis,
  which side, upper vs lower predominance, spasticity distribution, and sensory/proprioceptive
  loss. No population template can encode a person's specific asymmetry; only per-side capability
  capture can.

### UK guidance

- **Stroke Association:** promotes strength training after stroke (improved quality of life,
  help with household tasks, mood benefit), offers a video programme with A Stroke of Luck
  covering strength, stability, stamina and flexibility, each led by a qualified physiotherapist
  with written coaching notes, and advises speaking with a GP, physiotherapist and occupational
  therapist before starting. **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.** [SECONDARY]
  https://www.stroke.org.uk/blog/getting-active-benefits-strength-training
- NHS Highland *Exercise after Stroke Pathway* and the Exercise After Stroke / FRETS training
  frameworks indicate that in the UK, stroke exercise instruction is a **credentialed
  speciality**. **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS.** [SECONDARY] http://www.exerciseafterstroke.org.uk/about.asp

### Verdict line

> **Capability-led programming is the defensible approach; population layer limited to
> education/UX.** RT benefit in chronic stroke is well established across 30 trials, but the
> same meta-analysis states the evidence is "insufficient for evidence-based rehabilitation",
> meaning there is a proven direction and no agreed dose; and the defining feature of the
> population, hemiparetic asymmetry, is by definition per-person and per-limb, which is exactly
> what capability capture handles and a template cannot.

## 10. Short stature / dwarfism (skeletal dysplasia, chiefly achondroplasia)

### Published resistance-training guidance: there is none

**I found no published resistance-training guideline, no RT trial, and no RT systematic review
for people with achondroplasia or other skeletal dysplasias.** This is the thinnest evidence
base in the survey by a wide margin, and I want that stated plainly rather than filled in with
adjacent material.

What does exist:

- **Alves et al. (2024). "Facilitators and constraints of physical activity in adults with
  achondroplasia: a scoping review." *Journal of Rare Diseases*.**
  https://doi.org/10.1007/s44162-024-00048-9 - 50 sources; 31 addressed physical activity
  capacity or motivation; 15 reported reduced physical functioning and physical quality of life
  versus average-stature people; 13 highlighted biomechanical limitations and gait alterations
  that may constrain exercise; 12 noted high rates of pain, chronic conditions and obesity
  limiting activity tolerance; 10 suggested adapted exercise programmes and sport may give
  physical and psychosocial benefits. Findings organised into physical/physiologic,
  biomechanical, psychological/psychosocial and nutritional factors. **ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE
  that the field is a scoping review, i.e. mapping rather than dosing.** [SECONDARY - Springer
  redirects to an identity provider; figures corroborated via the publishing institution's
  repository listing.]
- "Physical Activity and Psychosocial Outcomes in Adults with Achondroplasia: An Exploratory
  Study" (2024), *IJERPH*, 21(9), 1160. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11431674/
  **[SECONDARY]** - exploratory, as titled.

### Setup and range-of-motion differences that are documented

**ESTABLISHED EVIDENCE / PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS**, from clinical management literature rather
than exercise science. [SECONDARY]

- Altered biomechanics from shorter limbs; joint laxity; **limited range of motion especially at
  the elbow**; genu varum; abnormal spinal curvature (lumbar lordosis).
- Rib cage deformity and small thoracic cavity may restrict lung expansion and volumes, limiting
  oxygen intake during exertion; shortened limbs may raise systemic vascular resistance.
- Smaller hands and short fingers are explicitly named as factors requiring adapted assessment
  and prescription. **PRODUCT INFERENCE:** this is a direct equipment-fit issue (grip diameter,
  bar spacing, machine seat and pad geometry, footplate reach) which capability capture and
  equipment modelling can hold.
- Chronic pain from musculoskeletal deformity or nerve compression may limit exercise capacity.

### Spinal and neurological considerations

**NEEDS CLINICAL REVIEW - this is the hard boundary in this section.**

- Clinical management literature recommends **neurological evaluation to assess degree of
  lordosis and spinal stenosis prior to exercise prescription**. **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS.**
  [SECONDARY]
- Symptomatic spinal stenosis is a recognised complication of achondroplasia; foramen magnum
  stenosis has its own European Achondroplasia Forum guiding principles
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10375694/). **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS.**
  [SECONDARY]
- Health supervision guidance (American Academy of Pediatrics, *Health Supervision for People
  With Achondroplasia*, Pediatrics 2020, https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2020-1010) advises
  avoiding collision and high-impact body-contact sports, trampolines, diving from boards,
  gymnastic vaulting, and hanging upside down. **This guidance is written for children.**
  Extending it to adult gym training is an extrapolation I am **not** making.
  **PROFESSIONAL CONSENSUS, paediatric scope.** [SECONDARY]

### Community resources

- **Dwarf Sports Association UK (DSAuk)** is the National Disability Sports Organisation for
  people with restricted growth and dwarfism, supported by Sport England. It publishes exercise
  programmes including a Beginners Program, Seated Program, Intermediate Workout and Gym Ball
  programmes, and coaching tips. Its framing: regular exercise maintains muscle tone around
  problem areas so those joints become more stable; "there is no reason why someone with
  Achondroplasia shouldn't participate in most activities", and people with dwarfism "find out
  their own limits and these aren't imposed upon them by society". **COMMUNITY GUIDANCE.**
  [SECONDARY] https://www.dsauk.org/2008/07/tips-for-coaches/
- **Restricted Growth Association UK (RGA)** provides medical information and support but I

### Verdict line

> **Capability-led programming is the defensible approach; population layer limited to
> education/UX - and any advice touching axial spinal loading crosses into "beyond fitness-
> product scope without clinical partnership".** There is no published resistance-training
> guidance, trial, or review for skeletal dysplasia at all, and the only clinically sourced

> exercise prescription; documented setup differences (limited elbow ROM, grip size, limb
> proportion, reach) are equipment-fit capability attributes the Layer 1 core can hold honestly,
> whereas anything specifying loaded spinal flexion/extension, axial compression or overhead
> pressing policy requires clinical partnership.

## 11. Cross-cutting findings

**1. The national base is a dose, not a template, and it is the same dose for everyone.**
Strength on at least two days a week, all major muscle groups, any amount better than none. It
is impairment-blind by design, because it was co-produced in 2018-19 precisely when
impairment-specific evidence was "sparse". Every population in this survey that has a
quantified strength prescription lands in the same 2-3 sessions per week envelope: SCI 2x,
Parkinson's 2-3x, MS practice guidance 2-3x, CMO disabled adults 2x. **PRODUCT INFERENCE: the
frequency is not the differentiator. Exercise selection, setup, and load progression are.**

**2. Only two populations have a quantified, named, population-specific strength prescription:
SCI (Martin Ginis et al. 2018) and Parkinson's (Parkinson's Foundation/ACSM 2021).** Everything
else in this survey either has benefit evidence without agreed parameters (stroke, MS, CP,
lower-limb amputation) or has essentially no RT evidence at all (upper-limb amputation,
skeletal dysplasia).

**3. Three separate reviews independently found that the parameters do not template.** Bania
2023 (CP): no association between intensity or volume and strength gain. Veldema 2020 (stroke):
"insufficient for evidence-based rehabilitation". Rosario 2023 (lower-limb amputation): 8 of 10
studies did not report intensity, and RT could not be isolated from co-interventions. This
convergence is itself the finding: **in most disability populations, "how much" is not yet
answerable from the literature, whereas "is it safe and does it help" mostly is.**

**4. The strongest population-specific content is safety reassurance, not dosing.** Resistance
training does not increase spasticity in cerebral palsy, and does not increase spasticity after
stroke (and may improve it). Both findings overturn an older clinical concern that still
suppresses participation. That is high-value **education** content with graded evidence behind
it, and it belongs in a population layer that is educational rather than prescriptive.

**5. Two populations carry programming-relevant modifiers that are not intensity or volume.**
MS fatigue (never push through; delayed payback over days) and MS/SCI heat sensitivity invert
or constrain ordinary training logic. These are the clearest case in the survey for a genuine
population-informed behaviour beyond exercise selection.

**6. "Wheelchair user" is the most misleading grouping in the whole space.** About 1.2 million
UK wheelchair users, of whom SCI is on the order of a tenth, a third are ambulatory, and the
rest span stroke, MS, CP, arthritis, cardiorespiratory disease and more. The best available
cross-condition synthesis (AHRQ CER 241) studied only three conditions, declined to generalise
beyond them, and did not rate resistance training separately.

**7. Shoulder health in manual wheelchair users is the one genuinely cross-cutting physical
finding, and it is unresolved.** Rotator cuff tendinopathy at 73-100% on imaging, tears around
60%, four times matched controls; strengthening interventions help short-term with unevaluated
long-term effects; the reference clinical guideline's thrust is load *reduction*. **NEEDS
CLINICAL REVIEW** before any specific pressing/pulling volume policy is encoded.

**8. Where users say they want advice from.** Activity Alliance 2025-26: healthcare
professionals are the preferred advice source (physio/OT 38%, specialist doctor 38%, GP/nurse
33%); the internet is where information is actually found (53%). Every charity resource I read,
without exception, ends by advising the reader to consult a GP, physiotherapist or qualified
exercise professional. **PRODUCT INFERENCE: a signposting posture is consistent with both what
users say they want and what every credible source in this space does.**

**9. The UK community resource layer is real but thin, and it is exercise libraries, not
programmes.** LimbPower (gym guide, 62 videos, ReVAMP 12-week), WheelPower, CP Sport, DSAuk,
Stroke Association/A Stroke of Luck, MS Trust, Get Yourself Active. US NCHPAD is more developed.
None of these is a progressive load-managed strength programme.

**10. Nothing I found reports what disabled people want from *strength training* specifically.**
Activity Alliance's survey does not break out gyms or resistance training as a modality. That is
an unfilled research question, not a silence to interpret.

| CR-1 | SCI above T6 (esp. cervical) | **Autonomic dysreflexia** | A sudden exaggerated sympathetic response that can cause life-threatening hypertension. Triggers, warning signs, and what to do are clinical content. A fitness product must not teach recognition or management, and must not imply it has screened for risk. |
| CR-2 | SCI (esp. tetraplegia) | **Thermoregulatory dysfunction** | Impaired sweating and vasoconstriction mean higher core temperature in warm environments and inability to warm in cool ones. Any environmental/session-duration guidance is clinical. |
| CR-3 | SCI | **Skin breakdown under load and during seated training** | Named by SCIRE as an SCI-specific exercise risk. Pressure care is clinical, not programming. |
| CR-4 | Manual wheelchair users (all causes) | **Shoulder overuse policy** | Tendinopathy 73-100%, tears ~60% on imaging. Evidence says strengthening helps short-term; the reference clinical guideline says reduce repetitive upper-limb load. Any specific pressing volume cap, push/pull ratio, or overhead policy is a clinical judgement not settled by the literature. |
| CR-5 | Achondroplasia / skeletal dysplasia | **Spinal stenosis and lordosis; foramen magnum stenosis** | Clinical management literature says neurological evaluation should precede exercise prescription. Any advice on axial loading, loaded spinal flexion/extension, overhead pressing, or cervical positioning requires clinical partnership. |
| CR-6 | Achondroplasia | **Reduced thoracic volume / restricted lung expansion; possible raised systemic vascular resistance** | Affects exertional tolerance. Intensity guidance in this population is not something the fitness literature supports. |
| CR-7 | Multiple sclerosis | **Fatigue: "do not push through"** | Inverts standard training logic, with symptom payback lasting days. Encoding it as an autoregulation rule changes what the app tells a person to do about their symptoms. Needs clinical framing. |
| CR-8 | Multiple sclerosis | **Heat sensitivity** | Widely reported; the specific mitigations (pre-cooling, iced fluid, environmental control) come from charity practice and single studies not traced to primary source here. |
| CR-9 | Parkinson's | **Medication "on" period timing** | The professional recommendation ties exercise timing to medication state. A product referencing medication timing is giving advice adjacent to a prescribed regimen. |
| CR-10 | Stroke, CP | **Spasticity messaging** | The evidence that RT does not worsen spasticity is good and worth communicating, but it directly contradicts advice some users will have been given clinically. How it is worded matters. |
| CR-11 | Amputation (upper and lower) | **Residual limb / socket tolerance and skin integrity under load** | Appears only in community material with no evidence base. Load tolerance of a residuum is a prosthetist/physio judgement. |
| CR-12 | All | **The distinction between "respecting a user-declared restriction" and "prescribing for a condition"** | Every population in this survey has a credible route into rehabilitation territory. Where the line sits is a decision requiring clinical and regulatory input, not an evidence question this report can settle. |

## Limitations of this survey

1. **Paywalls and identity-provider redirects blocked direct reading of several primary papers**
   (Nature/*Spinal Cord*, SAGE, ScienceDirect, Springer, PubMed, physio-pedia, MS Society). Every
   such item is marked **[SECONDARY]**. Where possible I corroborated across two independent
   routes and said so. **Before any of these figures is quoted publicly or used in a safety
   decision, it must be re-verified against the primary text.**
2. **PDF sources would not convert to text in this environment**, including the LimbPower Gym
   Resource Guide and the 2019 CMO PDF. I verified the LimbPower guide's existence and resource
   inventory but did not read its contents.
3. **This is an evidence survey, not a clinical review.** Nothing here has been reviewed by a
   clinician. The NEEDS CLINICAL REVIEW register is a list of things I identified as requiring
   review, not a list I have any authority to clear.
4. **I did not search non-English literature**, and search access was US-region, which may
   under-represent UK and European sources.
5. **Populations not covered** because they were not in scope but plausibly matter to the same
   product surface: acquired brain injury, spina bifida, muscular dystrophy and other
   neuromuscular conditions, visual and hearing impairment, learning disability, autism, chronic
   pain and fibromyalgia, ME/CFS and long COVID (where graded exercise carries its own serious
   controversy and would need its own dossier), and hypermobility/EDS.
