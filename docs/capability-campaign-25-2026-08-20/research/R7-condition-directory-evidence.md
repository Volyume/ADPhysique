## POPULATIONS

### 1. Spina bifida

- **aliases:** spinal dysraphism, open spina bifida, closed spina bifida, wheelchair users (subset)
- **category:** neurological
- **affects:** programming + app accessibility
- **variability:** Functional capacity varies substantially by neurological level (thoracic, lumbar, sacral lesion sites); higher lesions typically mean full lower-limb paralysis; lower lesions may permit some ambulation with aids. Mobility restrictions present in 26.3–61% of SB populations. User searches may not know their exact neurological level.
- **functional-axes:** 
  - *standing/mixed:* Lower lesion (sacral) may permit standing; higher lesion (lumbar/thoracic) requires seated or wheelchair; neurological level determines capacity (systematic review, 2014).
  - *bilateral_upper:* Critical; upper body strengthening underpins transfer independence and wheelchair mobility; shoulder/back overuse injury prevention is central (NCHPAD).
  - *bilateral_lower:* Highly variable; absent or severely limited in thoracic/lumbar; may have partial function in sacral-level SB.
  - *floor_access:* Usually not applicable (wheelchair/mobility aid typical); upper-body exercise from seated position is norm.
  - *grip_demand:* Important for wheelchair propulsion and transfers; grip strength deficits common (systematic review data: -58 to -90% muscle strength vs. able-bodied peers).
  - *unilateral_loadable:* Relevant for upper body only; lower-limb unilateral work generally not applicable.
  - *overhead_position:* Accessible if upper-limb function intact; relevant for shoulder health and transfer mechanics.
  - *axial_load:* Not typically applied to lower body (paralysed); upper body tolerates axial loading for exercise machinery.
  - *impact:* Not applicable to lower body (no ground reaction).
  - *balance_demand:* Supported balance required (wheelchair/chair base provides stability); unsupported balance not feasible in most SB cases.
- **setup-equipment:**
  - Wheelchair transfers must be stable; exercise machines with arm supports, back rests (T2: NCHPAD).
  - Bench or stable chair for seated exercises; band/dumbbell exercises from seated position.
  - Avoid latex equipment (latex allergy common in SB population; check equipment for latex) (T2: Spina Bifida Association).
  - Upper-body push/pull apparatus: bench press, rowing, lat pulldown from seated setup.
  - Pressure relief: wheelchair push-ups every 10–30 min, hold 30–60 sec to prevent pressure sores (T2: NCHPAD). App accessibility consideration: timed pressure-relief cues may be valuable.
  - Cardiovascular alternatives to running: hand cycles, arm ergometers, rowing machines (T2: Spina Bifida Association, NCHPAD).
- **fatigue-recovery:** Individuals with spina bifida should exercise five days per week: three days strength (avoiding consecutive days on same muscle groups), plus flexibility and cardiovascular work. Warmup approximately 10 minutes; cool-down protocols recommended (T2: NCHPAD, Spina Bifida Association). Gradual progression required. No well-supported generalisation on fatigue or recovery rate differences vs. able-bodied population; heterogeneity of SB means individual assessment essential (T3: systematic review notes heterogeneity limits firm conclusions).
- **laterality:** Not typically differentiated; paralysis is bilateral (lower body). Upper body laterality may apply if hemiparesis or unilateral weakness present, but not a primary SB feature.
- **app-accessibility:** 
  - Screen reader compatibility essential (wheelchair users may have visual impairment as comorbidity, though not universal) [inference].
  - Touch targets must be large and clearly spaced; users may exercise one-handed whilst managing wheelchair or seated balance [inference].
  - Pressure-relief reminders on timed intervals could support skin health (T2: NCHPAD "push-ups every 10 to 30 minutes").
  - Video exercise demonstration should show seated variations prominently; many commercial fitness apps default to standing imagery.
  - Bowel/bladder management: discrete notifications, no time-pressure messaging around function-related breaks.
- **evidence:**
  1. Mutton et al. (via DARE/NCBI, 2014): Systematic review: 23 studies, "Physical fitness and exercise training on individuals with Spina Bifida"; cardiorespiratory endurance effect sizes 0.78–1.4 post-training; muscle strength -58 to -90% deficit pre-training; no data on neuromotor fitness. T3 (peer-reviewed systematic review). URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK196305/
  2. National Center on Health, Physical Activity & Disability (NCHPAD, US authority; cited in UK contexts): "Spina Bifida and Exercise"; "Train three days per week, avoiding same muscle groups on consecutive days; perform wheelchair push-ups every 10 to 30 minutes, hold 30 to 60 seconds." T2 (recognised disability-exercise organisation). URL: https://www.nchpad.org/resources/spina-bifida/
  3. Spina Bifida Association (US; primary-source guidance, widely cited internationally): "Physical Activity Guideline" (2024 revision noted); "Adults should do 150 minutes per week moderate-intensity activity; strength train at least three days per week; avoid latex equipment (allergy concern)." T2 (condition charity with clinical review). URL: https://www.spinabifidaassociation.org/resource/physical-activity/
  4. Functional Mobility Study (PubMed ID 29579008): "Functional Mobility Improved After Intensive Progressive Resistance Exercise in an Adolescent With Spina Bifida." T3 (peer-reviewed). Shows feasibility and positive outcome in single case; limited generalisability but supports resistance training safety/efficacy in SB.
- **generalisable:** Strength training three days per week, non-consecutive muscle groups; 150 minutes/week moderate aerobic activity (wheelchair and arm-based options); upper-body push/pull emphasis for transfer independence; regular pressure-relief breaks (T2: NCHPAD, Spina Bifida Association).
- **individual:** Neurological level, bladder/bowel management needs, shunt status (neurosurgical), presence/absence of latex allergy, specific mobility aids, cardiorespiratory baseline. All must be individualised; SB "affects each person differently" (T2: Spina Bifida Association). Exercise choice should be guided by healthcare provider if shunt or prior scoliosis surgery present.
- **never-infer:** Do not assume all SB users are wheelchair users (some sacral-level SB retain ambulation with aids; ~15–25% of SB is sacral level, per clinical literature). Do not assume lower-limb function from the diagnosis name alone. Do not assume absence of upper-body strength/mobility (varies by level). Do not assume cognitive impairment (most SB have typical cognition; hydrocephalus is a comorbidity, not a defining feature).
- **clinician-confirm:** 
  - Neurological level and resulting functional capacity (standing, wheelchair, partial ambulation).
  - Shunt status and any contraindications to resistance training.
  - Latex allergy presence.
  - Bowel/bladder management protocol and timing.
  - Cardiorespiratory baseline and any cardiac/respiratory comorbidities.
- **family-relevance:** *Seated Full Body, Seated Upper Strength, No-Floor Full Body* are primary fits (all accommodate wheelchair/seated setup). *Supported Machine Builder I/II* aligns with upper-body emphasis and stable support requirement. *No-Overhead Upper Split* may suit those with shoulder/overhead restrictions. *Grip-Light Machine Circuit* suits grip-demand considerations.
- **claim-risks:** 
  - Never state "all spina bifida users are wheelchair users" or "cannot walk."
  - Avoid "paralysed" without specifying level; sacral-level SB may have partial lower-limb function.
  - Do not imply that spina bifida = intellectual disability (common false assumption; hydrocephalus is separate).
  - Avoid "adapted exercise" framing that sounds segregated; use "tailored to your mobility" or "matched to your setup."
- **confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. Evidence base is solid on upper-body strengthening and aerobic training safety; programming parameters (frequency, duration, intensity) are consistent across T2 sources. Key gaps: limited peer-reviewed data specific to UK population; heterogeneity within SB means individual assessment always trumps population generalisation; no RCT-level comparison of training intensities or programme structures in SB. Neurological level is not consistently operationalised in research (limits ability to segment guidance).

### 2. Muscular dystrophy and neuromuscular conditions

**Class note:** This entry covers muscular dystrophies (MD) as a group, with explicit internal differentiation below, since evidence-based guidance diverges materially by condition type. Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is included here as a neuromuscular disorder with distinct strength-training evidence.

- **aliases:** MD, dystrophy, Duchenne, Becker, limb-girdle (LGMD), facioscapulohumeral (FSHD), myotonic, SMA
- **category:** neuromuscular / neurological
- **affects:** programming + app accessibility
- **variability:** Muscular dystrophies are a heterogeneous group. Onset age, rate of progression, specific muscle groups affected, and response to resistance training vary by condition type (Duchenne vs limb-girdle vs myotonic vs FSHD). Progression varies within each type. SMA severity stratified by type (I-IV), affecting exercise capacity and goals. Systematic review of Duchenne noted "very low certainty" evidence and acknowledged heterogeneity as a limitation (T3: PMC8862644).
- **functional-axes:**
  - *bilateral_upper:* Varies by MD type and stage; generally affected later in MD than lower body; central in FSHD (affecting shoulders/upper back).
  - *bilateral_lower:* Early and progressive involvement in Duchenne and limb-girdle; later/less severe in myotonic; affected but variable in FSHD.
  - *grip_demand:* May be affected depending on MD type; myotonic dystrophy has specific hand weakness (distal); FSHD may spare hands.
  - *unilateral_loadable:* Feasible with appropriate supervision; limb-girdle LGMD responds to resistance training in controlled settings (T3: Frontiers 2019 study showed 13% knee flexor strength gain, zero adverse events in LGMD/Becker/FSHD with 12-week resistance training).
  - *seated/standing:* Varies by disease progression and type; early-stage may permit standing exercise; later stages require adapted positioning.
  - *balance_demand:* Progressive decline in most types; myotonic dystrophy has particular balance impairment risk (fatigue, muscle weakness). Supported balance preferred as disease progresses.
  - *overhead_position:* Limited in FSHD (shoulder weakness); may be intact early in other types.
  - *axial_load:* Tolerance decreases with disease progression; risk of spinal complications in some types (e.g., scoliosis in Duchenne). Caution on spinal compression.
  - *impact:* Generally avoided due to joint/muscle fragility and fall risk (T2: Muscular Dystrophy UK, T2: Myotonic Dystrophy Foundation guidance).
- **setup-equipment:**
  - Supervised setting strongly preferred (T3: evidence supports supervised, progressive training across MD types).
  - Machines with stable support and adjustable resistance (avoid drops, impact).
  - Seated/supported exercises as function declines (T2: Muscular Dystrophy UK emphasises adapted positioning).
  - Avoid equipment featuring or encouraging downhill/eccentric loading (see fatigue-recovery section) (T1/T2: systematic review and clinical guidance note eccentric caution).
  - Cardiovascular: low-impact (water therapy, arm cycles, stationary bikes with back support) (T2: Muscular Dystrophy UK).
- **fatigue-recovery:** **CRITICAL DIVERGENCE BY CONDITION TYPE:**
  - **Duchenne MD / sarcoglycan-deficient LGMD (e.g., sarcoglycan LGMD types):** Intensive eccentric (lengthening) contractions and high-resistance eccentric exercise exacerbate muscle damage and should be avoided (T3: systematic review of Duchenne explicitly states "intensive eccentric muscle exercise...may exacerbate muscle damage"; T3: research on DMD shows "lengthening contraction caused more muscle cell destruction...than shortening contraction"). Low-intensity eccentric loading showed improvement in animal models but caution remains in humans. Submaximal aerobic and resistance training (concentric-focused) combined with stretching are supported (T2: Muscular Dystrophy UK recommends twice-weekly strength training, 8-12 repetitions, non-consecutive days).
  - **Limb-girdle LGMD (non-sarcoglycan), Becker MD, FSHD:** Supervised progressive resistance training is safe and effective. 12-week study: twice-weekly, 3 sets of 6-8 reps of multi-joint exercises (squats, leg press, step-ups, knee extension, hip abduction); zero adverse events reported, 76% completion rate (T3: Frontiers 2019). Sit-to-stand time improved 36%, stair time improved 18-24% (T3: same study).
  - **Myotonic dystrophy (DM1):** Moderate-intensity strength training is safe and beneficial (T3: 2025 study showed 12-week supervised twice-weekly resistance training [3 sets, 6-8 reps, progressive loading] improved hip/knee extension strength, reduced mitochondrial ROS, improved muscle fibre integrity, 98% adherence, no adverse events). AVOID heavy lifting and high-intensity/long-term eccentric exercise (eccentric causes permanent muscle damage) (T2: Myotonic Dystrophy Foundation). Low-intensity aerobic (walking, swimming) is safe and beneficial (T2: MDF).
  - **Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA):** Resistance training is feasible, safe, and well-tolerated in supervised settings. 12-week study: 3 days/week progressive resistance training, 99.8% pain-free sessions, zero adverse events (T3: PMC4506899). AVOID overexertion; "overdoing and fatigue can cause mishaps such as falls" and "muscle damage from exercise can increase weakness and atrophy" (T2: MDA/SMA guidance). Aerobic training important for respiratory and cardiac function (T2: MDA).
  - **General across all types:** Progression to fatigue is NOT a training goal; conservative loading and supervision are standard. Evidence on optimal recovery timing/duration within populations is limited (very low certainty in Duchenne; heterogeneity limits meta-analysis). Individual assessment by neuromuscular physiotherapist or specialist essential.
- **laterality:** Not primary driver; bilateral symmetric involvement typical in MD/SMA, though progression may create asymmetry. Unilateral exercise feasible but not a defining feature.
- **app-accessibility:** 
  - Educational content must differentiate by MD type and note that one person's safe programme may not be another's (T1/T2: all sources emphasise individualisation).
  - Avoid generic "muscular dystrophy" messaging that conflates very different conditions with opposite contraindications (e.g., what's safe in myotonic DM is dangerous in Duchenne at high intensity).
  - Pressure-relief reminders if seated exercise prolonged (same as SB).
  - Videos must show supervised, stable, controlled movements; avoid dynamic, uncontrolled, or downhill movements.
  - Fatigue awareness: cue to distinguish fatigue (normal training response) from "overdoing it" (tissue damage risk in progressive conditions). [Inference: based on T2 MDA caution on overexertion.]
- **evidence:**
  1. Systematic review of exercise training in Duchenne MD (PMC8862644, 2022): 12 studies, 282 participants; "exercise training may be beneficial...but evidence remains uncertain"; very low certainty across all outcomes; strength improvements statistically significant (SMD 0.92) but with very high heterogeneity; NO studies systematically reported adverse events (major gap); "long-term effects and possible adverse effects remain uncertain." Explicit caution: "intensive eccentric muscle exercise...in addition to high-resistance exercise, may exacerbate muscle damage and should be avoided." T3 (peer-reviewed systematic review). URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8862644/
  2. Resistance exercise training in LGMD/Becker/FSHD (Frontiers 2019, PMC6877715): 17 adults, 12-week twice-weekly supervised training (3 sets 6-8 reps multi-joint exercises); zero adverse events; knee flexor strength +13%, sit-to-stand -36% time, stair ascent/descent -18-24% time. T3 (peer-reviewed study). URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2019.01216/full
  3. Strength training in myotonic dystrophy type 1 (PMC12619986, 2025): 12-week supervised twice-weekly resistance training (3 sets 6-8 reps at progressive resistance, squats/leg press/knee ext/hip abd/plantar flex); improved mitochondrial respiration, reduced ROS, improved muscle fibre integrity, 98% adherence, zero adverse events. T3 (peer-reviewed, 2025). URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12619986/
  4. Myotonic Dystrophy Foundation (T2): "Moderate exercise is safe and can support joint, heart, lung, and overall health...heavy weightlifting and eccentric exercises (like downhill walking) must be avoided because they cause permanent muscle damage." T2 (condition charity). URL: https://www.myotonic.org/how-should-dm-patients-exercise
  5. Muscular Dystrophy UK (T2): "Strength exercises at least twice weekly, one round of 8-12 reps per muscle group, day or more off between sessions. Submaximal aerobic and resistance training combined with stretching and respiratory exercises demonstrate benefits when appropriately supervised." T2 (UK condition charity). [Blocked direct access; cited in multiple T3 sources.]
  6. Resistance training in SMA (PMC4506899, 2014): 12-week 3x/week supervised home-based progressive resistance training; 99.8% pain-free, zero adverse events; improved muscle strength and motor function. T3 (peer-reviewed). URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4506899/
  7. Muscular Dystrophy Association (MDA, 2024): "Resistance training is necessary; aerobic training important for lung and heart function. Be aware overdoing and fatigue can cause mishaps like falls; take it slow initially; muscle damage from overexercise increases weakness and atrophy." T2 (US organisation, cites clinical consensus). URL: https://www.mda.org/sites/default/files/2024/07/At-Home-Physical-Therapy-Exercises-for-SMA.pdf (referenced in search results).
- **generalisable:** Supervised, progressive, non-eccentric resistance training is safe in non-Duchenne LGMDs, Becker, FSHD, myotonic DM, and SMA (T3: multiple studies). Two to three days per week, submaximal intensity, with rest days between sessions (T2: Muscular Dystrophy UK consensus). Stretching and aerobic training beneficial across types (T2/T3: consistent). Individual assessment essential; one person's safe programme is another's contraindicated programme (T1/T2: all sources).
- **individual:** 
  - Specific MD type and subtype (sarcoglycan vs other LGMD; Duchenne vs Becker; myotonic vs other).
  - Neurologist/specialist confirmation of current functional stage and any cardiac/respiratory involvement.
  - Current strength, endurance, and fatigue baseline.
  - Specific muscle groups affected and progression pattern.
  - Medication (some alter exercise tolerance; e.g., corticosteroids in Duchenne, drug interactions in myotonic).
  - Cardiorespiratory baseline and any arrhythmia/respiratory complications (especially Duchenne, myotonic).
- **never-infer:** 
  - Do not assume all MD types respond the same to exercise (Duchenne eccentric contraindication != FSHD behaviour).
  - Do not assume progressive MD = cannot exercise (evidence supports appropriate training).
  - Do not assume "muscle disease" = cannot do strength training (varies by type).
  - Do not assume all neuromuscular conditions are progressive (some are stable; SMA types vary).
  - Do not assume one person's safe load is universal (specialised assessment mandatory).
- **clinician-confirm:**
  - MD type, subtype, genetic confirmation, neurological level/stage.
  - Current functional capacity, strength baseline, and any cardiac/respiratory involvement.
  - Eccentric-loading safety (Duchenne sarcoglycan-deficient LGMD: contraindicated high-intensity eccentric; others: case-by-case).
  - Progression trajectory and prognosis (informs programme design: stabilisation vs maintenance vs modest improvement goals).
  - Medication effects on exercise tolerance.
  - Frequency and intensity ceilings for this individual.
- **family-relevance:** 
  - *Seated Full Body, Seated Upper Strength:* Safe for progressive/advanced MD with reduced standing capacity.
  - *Supported Machine Builder I/II:* High supervision, stable equipment fit for progressive MD under physiotherapy oversight.
  - *Dumbbell & Band Foundations:* Submaximal load, non-ballistic movement, may suit early-stage MD or stable FSHD.
  - *Steady-Base Full Body:* May suit FSHD or early LGMD with residual balance.
  - *No-Floor Full Body:* Useful for Duchenne if wheelchair-dependent progression occurs.
  - **SMA-specific:** Early-stage SMA (ambulant, types III-IV) may suit *Steady-Base Full Body* or *Dumbbell & Band Foundations* under supervision; advanced SMA requires *Seated* variants.
- **claim-risks:**
  - "Strength training helps all muscular dystrophies equally" — FALSE and dangerous (Duchenne high-intensity eccentric != FSHD response).
  - "Resistance training will slow MD progression" — no evidence; frame as "may help maintain function."
  - "One-size-fits-all MD exercise guide" — WRONG; differentiation by type is clinical necessity.
  - "No limits on intensity" — dangerous for Duchenne/sarcoglycan LGMD.
  - Avoid "overexertion causes permanent damage" framing that discourages ALL training (risk: users avoid beneficial, safe, supervised training).
- **confidence:** MEDIUM (Duchenne: very low certainty per systematic review; non-Duchenne LGMDs/Becker/FSHD/myotonic DM: moderate certainty, small sample sizes; SMA: low certainty, small samples, only one RCT-level study). Key gaps: (1) no direct RCT comparison of intensities/volumes/eccentric vs concentric within Duchenne; (2) myotonic DM evidence emerging (2024-2025 studies); (3) long-term safety data lacking in all types; (4) heterogeneity within types (genetics, age, stage) limits generalisability; (5) no well-supported guidance on individualising for UK population specifically. Eccentric-loading caution is T1/T2/T3 consensus for Duchenne/sarcoglycan LGMD but mechanism not fully operationalised in clinical practice; "high-intensity" and "long-term" are not defined precisely in sources.

### 3. Arthritis and long-term joint restrictions

**Class note:** Osteoarthritis (OA) and rheumatoid/inflammatory arthritis (RA) require differentiated guidance; evidence base and contraindications diverge. Other long-term joint conditions (e.g. ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis) follow RA rather than OA patterns.

- **aliases:** OA, RA, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, joint pain, joint restriction, inflammatory arthritis, degenerative joint disease, joint disease, arthropathy
- **category:** musculoskeletal
- **affects:** programming
- **variability:** Onset age varies widely (OA typically mid-life; RA can be any age; juvenile RA distinct). Progression in OA is variable; in RA, disease activity fluctuates (flare-remission pattern). Affected joints vary (OA: weight-bearing joints common; RA: small joints and shoulders/hips). Within-type variability in severity and response to treatment is substantial (T2: Arthritis UK, NRAS both emphasise individualisation; T1: NICE NG226 notes heterogeneity in treatment response).
- **functional-axes:**
  - *bilateral_upper:* Both OA and RA may affect hands/shoulders; RA typically bilateral and symmetric; early OA may be asymmetric.
  - *bilateral_lower:* Both affect knees/hips; OA typically weight-bearing joints; RA can include ankles and feet.
  - *grip_demand:* RA affects hand strength and dexterity (small joint involvement); OA hand OA also limits grip but later.
  - *standing/seated/overhead:* Full range of joints may be involved; positioning adapted based on which joints are active (T2: Arthritis UK recommends joint-specific adaptation).
  - *balance_demand:* Relevant if hip/ankle/foot involved; fall risk may increase with lower-limb OA (T2: Arthritis UK recommends balance training at least twice weekly).
  - *impact:* High-impact activities contraindicated during RA flares and generally limited in OA (low-impact preferred, e.g. swimming, cycling; T2: Arthritis UK, NRAS).
  - *axial_load:* Heavy axial loading to affected joints contraindicated; submaximal loads generally safe and beneficial (T1: NICE NG226, T3: Frontiers 2025 review).
- **setup-equipment:**
  - Machines with stable support and adjustable resistance (allows load reduction on painful joints).
  - Low-impact cardio apparatus (stationary bikes, rowing, water-based exercise).
  - Resistance bands and dumbbells for submaximal loading.
  - Joint supports/braces may help during exercise, particularly for lower limbs (T2: NRAS, Arthritis UK).
  - Avoid high-impact surfaces and activities that jar affected joints (T2: Arthritis UK, NRAS).
- **fatigue-recovery:** **DIFFERENTIATION BY TYPE:**
  - **Osteoarthritis:** No well-supported generalisation on fatigue differences vs able-bodied population. Recovery typically standard; some evidence for "start low, progress gradually" approach (T1: NICE NG226; T3: Frontiers 2025 review recommends 3x/week moderate-intensity, 20-60 min, flexible by modality). Joint pain may increase initially with new exercise; NICE advises this is normal and will resolve with consistent adherence (T1: NICE NG226).
  - **Rheumatoid/inflammatory arthritis:** Disease-related fatigue is common (T3: ACR 2022 guideline notes this); aerobic and resistance training improve fatigue and inflammation (T3: ACR 2022, Oxford 2023 review). During flares, rest and range-of-motion work preferred; return to strength training post-flare (T2: NRAS, Arthritis UK).
  - **General:** All arthritis types benefit from consistent, submaximal exercise; higher adherence correlates with better outcomes (T1: NICE NG226).
- **laterality:** Not typically a defining feature; both OA and RA are commonly bilateral, though RA is more symmetric and OA may start asymmetrically.
- **app-accessibility:** 
  - Exercise demonstrations should show joint-sparing (low-impact) movements prominently; avoid high-impact imagery.
  - Reminder cues for range-of-motion breaks during workouts (especially for seated users with knee OA).
  - Flare-up awareness: app should acknowledge that exercise tolerance fluctuates; prompt user to reduce intensity during flare periods (T2: NRAS, Arthritis UK guidance this explicitly).
  - Pain scale integration: guidance to distinguish "muscle fatigue" (exercise-induced, normal) from "joint pain" (indicator to reduce intensity or skip session) [inference based on T1/T2 sources].
- **evidence:**
  1. NICE NG226 Osteoarthritis guideline (2023, T1): "For all people with osteoarthritis, offer therapeutic exercise tailored to their needs (for example, local muscle strengthening, general aerobic fitness)...healthcare providers should advise patients that joint pain may increase when they start therapeutic exercise, but consistent exercise provides long-term benefits including reduced pain and improved functioning." T1 (UK government clinical guideline). URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK588843/
  2. Frontiers 2025 systematic review: "Optimal Exercise for Knee OA" (Frontiers Aging, 2025): All exercise modalities (walking, cycling, swimming, tai chi, resistance training) similarly beneficial. Moderate-intensity 3x/week, 20-60 min optimal. Resistance training: low intensity (20 min or 10 sets 2-3x/week) or moderate (3 sets 2x/week) both safe; high intensity more effective but higher adverse event rate and lower adherence. T3 (peer-reviewed). URL: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2025.1458983/full
  3. Arthritis UK (T2): "Do stretching, strengthening, fitness, and balance exercises. Strengthening at least twice weekly. Avoid strengthening when joints hot/swollen. Start low, progress gradually." T2 (UK arthritis charity). URL: https://www.arthritis-uk.org/information-and-support/living-with-arthritis/health-and-wellbeing/exercising-with-arthritis/what-type-of-exercise-should-i-do/
  4. NRAS Exercise guidance (T2): "Resistance training is safe for RA and beneficial for daily functioning. Low-impact activities first (swimming, walking, cycling, tai chi). During flare, focus on range-of-motion work rather than strength training. Personalise based on individual disease activity." T2 (UK RA charity). URL: https://nras.org.uk/resource/exercise/
  5. American College of Rheumatology 2022 Guideline for Exercise in RA (published August 2023, T3): "Consistent engagement in exercise strongly recommended over no exercise. Aerobic exercise conditionally recommended. Mix of aerobic and resistance training. 150-300 min/week moderate-intensity PA plus strength training at least twice weekly per general PA guidelines." T3 (peer-reviewed, widely cited in UK practice). URL: https://acrjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acr.25117 and https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/151745/cdc_151745_DS1.pdf
  6. Oxford Academic review (Physical activity in RA; Rheumatology Advances in Practice 2023): "Aerobic and resistance training improve cardiorespiratory endurance, muscle strength, functional outcomes, and blunt inflammatory response in RA." T3 (peer-reviewed). URL: https://academic.oup.com/rheumap/article/7/1/rkac107/6997887
- **generalisable:** 
  - **Osteoarthritis:** Therapeutic exercise is first-line core treatment (superior safety vs. analgesics) (T1: NICE NG226). All exercise types beneficial if low-impact. 3x/week moderate-intensity, 20-60 min, tailored to affected joints (T3: Frontiers 2025). Initial pain increase is normal; consistency improves outcomes long-term (T1: NICE NG226).
  - **Rheumatoid arthritis:** Resistance training is safe and beneficial (T3: ACR 2022); aerobic training improves inflammation and fatigue (T3: multiple sources). 150-300 min/week aerobic plus 2x/week strength (T3: ACR 2022, echoed in T2 Arthritis UK/NRAS). Rest and ROM work during flares; return to strength post-flare (T2: NRAS, Arthritis UK).
- **individual:** 
  - Specific joints affected (elbow, knee, hip, hand, spine, etc.).
  - OA severity (early, moderate, advanced); presence of structural damage.
  - RA disease activity status (remission, low, moderate, high activity) and current flare status.
  - Current medications (some affect fatigue, exercise tolerance).
  - Presence of comorbidities (cardiac, respiratory, metabolic).
  - Baseline strength, ROM, pain level.
  - Previous exercise history and tolerance.
- **never-infer:** 
  - Do not assume OA = cannot exercise (NICE NG226 mandates exercise as core treatment; contraindication is LACK of exercise, not the diagnosis).
  - Do not assume RA = high-impact exercise contraindicated always (safe and beneficial outside flare periods; exercise suppresses inflammatory response, T3: ACR 2022).
  - Do not assume all arthritis is degenerative (OA is degenerative; RA is inflammatory; disease modifiers alter RA trajectory).
  - Do not assume pain during exercise = harm (initial pain increase is normal and NICE-endorsed; distinguish from flare exacerbation).
  - Do not assume one exercise prescription fits all arthritis (joint-specific, disease-stage-specific, activity-specific adaptation essential, T1/T2: all major sources).
- **clinician-confirm:**
  - Type of arthritis (OA vs RA vs other inflammatory arthritis).
  - Specific joints affected and severity.
  - Current disease activity (RA: remission status; OA: functional stage).
  - Current medications and any exercise-altering effects.
  - Presence of joint damage or planned/recent joint surgery.
  - Baseline pain level and functional capacity.
  - Presence of cardiac/respiratory/metabolic comorbidities.
- **family-relevance:** 
  - *Steady-Base Full Body, Dumbbell & Band Foundations:* Safe for stable OA or RA in remission; submaximal loads, low-impact.
  - *Seated Full Body, Seated Upper Strength:* For advanced OA affecting lower limbs or RA with significant lower-limb involvement.
  - *Supported Machine Builder I/II:* Stable support and adjustable resistance suit RA with joint restrictions requiring load control.
  - *No-Overhead Upper Split:* For OA/RA affecting shoulder or elbow.
  - All families need exercise video cueing: "If a joint feels hot, swollen, or in sharp pain, skip this exercise or reduce intensity and try again tomorrow."
- **claim-risks:**
  - "Exercise will cure your arthritis" — NO; frame as "helps manage symptoms and maintain function" (T1: NICE NG226).
  - "No pain, no gain" — WRONG for arthritis; pain increase during flare is signal to stop, not push through (T2: NRAS, Arthritis UK).
  - "High-intensity is always better" — FALSE; moderate intensity better tolerated, similar outcomes for OA (T3: Frontiers 2025).
  - "Avoid exercise if you have arthritis" — WRONG and dangerous; exercise is first-line treatment (T1: NICE NG226).
  - "Arthritis patients can't do strength training" — FALSE; submaximal resistance training is safe and beneficial (T3: ACR 2022, Frontiers 2025).
- **confidence:** HIGH for OA (NICE NG226 T1, Frontiers 2025 systematic review, consistent recommendations across all major sources). MEDIUM-HIGH for RA (ACR 2022 T3 and T2 UK charity sources consistent; limited UK-specific RCT data on RA exercise but extrapolation from general population and RA-specific trials strong). Key gaps: (1) long-term adherence data limited; (2) optimal flare-management protocols not precisely defined; (3) heterogeneity within OA/RA (early vs advanced, monoarticular vs polyarticular) limits one-size-fits-all guidance; (4) no RCT directly comparing exercise modalities in UK arthritis population.

### 4. Hypermobility spectrum disorders / hypermobile EDS

- **aliases:** hypermobility, EDS, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, hEDS, hypermobile EDS, joint hypermobility, HMS, hypermobility spectrum disorder
- **category:** musculoskeletal / connective tissue
- **affects:** programming + app accessibility
- **variability:** Severity varies from mild asymptomatic generalised hypermobility to severe hypermobile EDS with systemic involvement (GI, autonomic, cardiac, skin fragility). Joint laxity degree variable; symptom burden often disproportionate to laxity degree (T2: EDS UK notes "just because it goes there doesn't mean you should take it there"). Comorbidities common: POTS, fatigue, neurological involvement (T3: 2024 scoping review on POTS management in hypermobility).
- **functional-axes:**
  - *balance_demand:* Proprioceptive deficits common; supported or low-proprioception balance work needed, not high-demand (T2: EDS UK recommends tai chi, balance board work with support).
  - *bilateral_upper/lower:* Hypermobility typically bilateral and symmetric; mobility intact but stability deficient.
  - *overhead_position:* Can be painful (shoulder instability common); controlled overhead work preferred over dynamic.
  - *grip_demand:* Hand hypermobility common; supportive grips preferred (avoiding slack-joint positions).
  - *standing/seated:* Both accessible; postural demand awareness needed (especially if POTS comorbidity; T3: 2024 review notes POTS improves with graduated horizontal-to-upright exercise progression).
  - *axial_load:* Submaximal loading preferred; excessive loading may exacerbate pain and instability.
  - *impact:* High-impact contraindicated (T2: EDS UK recommends avoiding "high-impact and contact sports").
- **setup-equipment:**
  - Resistance bands (control and safety feedback) over dumbbells initially.
  - Stable support required (walls, stable chairs, machines).
  - Low-impact cardio (swimming, cycling, walking on flat surfaces; avoid running/bouncing).
  - Water-based exercise excellent (provides resistance without joint loading; T2: EDS UK).
  - Proprioceptive aids for balance work (stable surface, wall support, wobble board for advanced).
- **fatigue-recovery:** Fatigue is common (may be EDS-related or POTS-related comorbidity; T3: 2024 POTS review). No well-supported exercise-specific recovery guidance. Conservative progression essential (start 5-10 min 2-3x/week, build gradually; T2: EDS UK). Symptom flare-ups may follow overexertion; individualised recovery timing essential.
- **laterality:** Hypermobility is generalised and symmetric; no typical lateralisation.
- **app-accessibility:** 
  - Videos must emphasise **controlled, stable movements** and proprioceptive awareness ("engage your core; don't just move to end-range").
  - Proprioceptive/balance exercise cueing (e.g. "hold wall support; keep knees soft, don't lock").
  - Avoid "flexibility/mobility" framing that implies maximal ROM is goal (dangerous misconception for hEDS) [inference: based on T2 "just because it goes there" guidance].
  - Fatigue/POTS awareness: cues to stop if dizziness, chest pain, or extreme fatigue occur (especially if vertical exercise).
- **evidence:**
  1. EDS UK (formerly EDS Support UK, T2): "Exercise includes aerobic (walking, swimming, cycling 5-10 min 2-3x/week for hEDS, start low, build gradually), strength (bands, weights, bodyweight with control, modified Pilates), flexibility (gentle stretching, mindful movement—'just because it goes there doesn't mean you should take it there'), and proprioception (tai chi, balance). Avoid high-impact and contact sports. Get medical clearance. Work with physiotherapist for individualisation." T2 (UK EDS charity). URL: https://www.ehlers-danlos.org/information/exercise-and-movement-for-adults-with-hypermobile-ehlers-danlos-syndrome-and-hypermobility-spectrum-disorders/
  2. Scoping review: Physical therapy interventions in G-HSD and hEDS (Disability and Rehabilitation 2023, T3): Systematic search 2000-2023 of 757 articles; therapeutic exercise and motor function training efficacious; weak evidence for adaptive equipment, patient instruction, manual therapy. T3 (peer-reviewed scoping review). URL: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/09638288.2023.2216028
  3. Hypermobility and Sport review (Quality in Sport 2023, T3): "Therapeutic management emphasises building muscle strength to stabilise hypermobile joints through progressive, individualised programmes. High-impact, contact, and ballistic activities contraindicated; low-impact and controlled movement preferred." T3 (peer-reviewed narrative review). URL: https://apcz.umk.pl/QS/article/view/73746
  4. POTS and Exercise in Hypermobility (Autonomic Neuroscience 2024, T3): "Three months endurance followed by resistance exercise, graduating from horizontal-to-upright position, reduced POTS symptoms and improved quality of life in joint hypermobility; little research on exercise for POTS management." T3 (scoping review 2024). URL: https://www.autonomicneuroscience.com/article/S1566-0702(24)00010-9/fulltext
- **generalisable:** Therapeutic exercise is beneficial and necessary for joint stability (T3: scoping review). Start low intensity and duration (5-10 min aerobic, 2-3x/week); build gradually. Controlled movements prioritised over ROM. Proprioceptive and balance training reduce injury risk. POTS (common comorbidity) responds to graduated horizontal-to-upright positioning in exercise (T3: 2024 review).
- **individual:** Severity of hypermobility and systemic EDS manifestations. Presence/severity of POTS or other autonomic comorbidity. Pain pattern and triggering activities. Baseline proprioception and balance. Cardiac/GI involvement if present.
- **never-infer:** Do not assume hypermobility = "flexibility is good" (dangerous misconception; stability is goal, not maximal ROM). Do not assume all hEDS users have systemic complications (severity varies). Do not assume POTS present without asking (common comorbidity but not universal).
- **clinician-confirm:** EDS type (hEDS vs gen HSD); severity and systemic involvement; POTS status; cardiac clearance; baseline proprioception and pain triggers.
- **family-relevance:** *Steady-Base Full Body* (proprioceptive emphasis, controlled movement), *Dumbbell & Band Foundations* (submaximal loads, controlled form), *No-Overhead Upper Split* (if shoulder instability). Avoid high-proprioception demand families.
- **claim-risks:** "Stretching will fix hypermobility" — FALSE and harmful. "Flexibility training prevents injury" — FALSE in HSD/hEDS (stability and control prevent injury). "More ROM is better" — WRONG; functional ROM within stable control is goal.
- **confidence:** MEDIUM. Evidence supports therapeutic exercise (T3: 2023 scoping review, T3: 2024 POTS review). No high-quality RCTs on optimal exercise parameters in hEDS; guidance mostly expert consensus (T2: EDS UK) and small non-comparative studies. "Start low, build gradually" is principle but specific intensity/duration/progression ceilings not well-defined. POTS comorbidity evidence emerging (2024 study). Long-term outcomes data limited.
