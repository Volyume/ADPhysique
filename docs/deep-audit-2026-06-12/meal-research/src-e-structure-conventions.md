## CLAIM 1 — Meals per day in bodybuilding practice (typical range + what the literature recommends)

**Convention:** Bodybuilders eat roughly 3–6 protein-containing meals per day, spaced through the day.

- **HELMS-2014 (verbatim):** "Eating three to six meals per day with a meal containing 0.4-0.5 g/kg bodyweight of protein prior and subsequent to resistance training"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/

- **IRAKI-2019 (verbatim):** "Dietary protein intake is recommended to be 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day with a focus on sufficient protein at each meal (0.40–0.55 g/kg/meal) and an even distribution throughout the day (3–6 meals)."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/

- **ISSN-NT (verbatim, Position point 6):** "Meeting the total daily intake of protein, preferably with evenly spaced protein feedings (approximately every 3 h during the day), should be viewed as a primary area of emphasis"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

- **ISSN-NT (verbatim, Position point 11):** "Ingesting a 20–40 g protein dose (0.25–0.40 g/kg body mass/dose) of a high-quality source every three to 4 h appears to most favorably affect MPS rates"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

**Important caveat — meal frequency does NOT itself change metabolic rate / fat loss:**

- **HELMS-2014 (verbatim):** "disparate feeding patterns ranging from two to seven meals per day have been compared in tightly controlled studies using metabolic chambers, and no significant differences in 24-hour thermogenesis have been detected"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/

- **HELMS-2014 (verbatim):** "alterations in nutrient timing and frequency appear to have little effect on fat loss or lean mass retention"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/

- **IRAKI-2019 (verbatim, honest about the evidence gap):** "There are no studies looking specifically at what might be an optimal meal frequency for this population. This high frequency of meals is based on the belief of a greater state of anabolism and even a better use of nutrients during the day, which could translate into an improvement in body composition."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/

- **ISSN-DBC:** The diets/body-composition stand contains **no numbered position statement on within-day meal frequency**. The closest is in "Perspectives and future directions" — (verbatim): "Studies on the effect of different within-day meal frequencies and nutrient distributions in varying energetic balances combined with resistance or endurance training are still rather scarce."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5470183/

**Verdict:** SOLIDLY SOURCED (4+ independent sources for the 3–6 meals / ~every 3 h convention). The "more meals = more anabolic / faster metabolism" rationale is explicitly NOT supported — Helms says frequency has little effect on body composition; Iraki says the high-frequency practice rests on belief, not evidence. The app should present 3–6 meals as a practical/adherence convention, not a metabolic necessity.

## CLAIM 2 — Per-meal protein dosing (g/kg/meal)

**Convention:** ~0.4–0.55 g/kg per meal (or 20–40 g / 0.25–0.40 g/kg per dose), spaced every 3–4 h.

- **HELMS-2014 (verbatim):** "a meal containing 0.4-0.5 g/kg bodyweight of protein prior and subsequent to resistance training"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/

- **IRAKI-2019 (verbatim):** "divide their daily intake of 1.6–2.2 g/kg of protein per day into multiple meals each containing ~0.40–0.55 g/kg"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/

- **A&S-2013 (verbatim):** "high-quality protein dosed at 0.4–0.5 g/kg of LBM at both pre- and post-exercise is a simple, relatively fail-safe general guideline"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577439/

- **ISSN-NT (verbatim, point 11):** "Ingesting a 20–40 g protein dose (0.25–0.40 g/kg body mass/dose) of a high-quality source every three to 4 h appears to most favorably affect MPS rates"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

- **ISSN-NT (verbatim, point 7):** "Ingestion of essential amino acids (EAA; approximately 10 g) either in free form or as part of a protein bolus of approximately 20–40 g has been shown to maximally stimulate muscle protein synthesis (MPS)."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

**Note on units:** Helms/Aragon express the per-meal target as 0.4–0.5 g/kg (bodyweight or LBM); Iraki as 0.40–0.55 g/kg; ISSN-NT as 0.25–0.40 g/kg/dose or 20–40 g absolute. These overlap but are not identical — the app should use a range (≈0.4–0.5 g/kg/meal) rather than a single figure, and note the bodyweight-vs-LBM distinction.

**Verdict:** SOLIDLY SOURCED (4 independent sources). The ~0.4–0.5 g/kg/meal figure is one of the best-corroborated numbers in the whole audit.

## CLAIM 3 — Training-day vs rest-day carbohydrate handling (carb cycling)

**3a. Total daily protein / carb / fat targets (the macro frame carb-cycling sits inside):**

- **HELMS-2014 protein (verbatim):** "2.3-3.1 g/kg of lean body mass per day of protein" during contest prep. Also: "the lower the body fat of the individual, the greater the imposed caloric deficit and when the primary goal is to retain LBM, the higher the protein intake (within the range of 2.3-3.1 g/kg of LBM) should be."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/

- **HELMS-2014 fat (verbatim):** "15-30% of calories from fat" with carbohydrate taking "the reminder of calories" [sic — original typo for "remainder"].
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4033492/

- **IRAKI-2019 off-season carb (verbatim):** "the remaining calories should come from carbohydrates with focus on consuming sufficient amounts (≥3–5 g/kg/day)"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/

- **IRAKI-2019 off-season fat (verbatim):** "Fat should be consumed in moderate amounts (0.5–1.5 g/kg/day)"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/

- **ISSN-DBC carb (verbatim):** "A wide range of dietary approaches (low-fat to low-carbohydrate/ketogenic, and all points between) can be similarly effective for improving body composition."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5470183/

**3b. Carbohydrate timing matters far less than total daily intake when daily needs are met:**

- **ISSN-NT (verbatim):** "Outside of situations where rapid recovery is truly needed, and daily carbohydrate intake is matching energy demands, the importance of timed carbohydrate ingestion is notably decreased."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

- **A&S-2013 (verbatim):** "For the goal of maximizing rates of muscle gain, these findings support the broader objective of meeting total daily carbohydrate need instead of specifically timing its constituent doses."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577439/

**3c. Does carb cycling / putting more carbs on training days have outcome evidence?**

The published position stands do NOT endorse day-to-day carb cycling as superior for body composition. The closest controlled test is the intermittent-restriction + 2-day carb refeed model (Campbell 2020), covered in CLAIM 5. The practice of "more carbs on training days, fewer on rest days" is a coach-practice / adherence-and-glycogen rationale, not an outcome-proven advantage in the literature. The rationale published by Trexler/SBS is that refeeds may transiently raise leptin/energy expenditure and that elevated muscle glycogen may support training volume — these are mechanistic, not proven body-composition wins. (See CLAIM 5 for the numbers and the fabricated-figure check.)

**Verdict:**
- Total macro targets: SOLIDLY SOURCED (Helms + Iraki + ISSN-DBC).
- "Carb timing matters less than total daily intake": SOLIDLY SOURCED (ISSN-NT + A&S-2013).
- "Carb cycling (more carbs on training days) produces better body composition": COACH-PRACTICE ONLY — no position stand supports a day-to-day carb-cycling outcome benefit; the controlled refeed evidence is equivocal (CLAIM 5).

## CLAIM 4 — Pre/post-workout meal composition conventions

**4a. Timing and the "the pre-workout meal can double as post-workout" point — SUPPORTED:**

- **A&S-2013 (verbatim):** "In practice, it is common for those with the primary goal of increasing muscular size and/or strength to make a concerted effort to consume a pre-exercise meal within 1-2 hours prior to the bout"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577439/

- **A&S-2013 (verbatim):** "Depending on its size and composition, this meal can conceivably function as both a pre- and an immediate post-exercise meal, since the time course of its digestion/absorption can persist well into the recovery period."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577439/

- **IRAKI-2019 (verbatim):** protein meals timed "within 1–2 hours before or after training, and one feeding consisting of a non-whey protein source is consumed 1–2 hours prior to sleep"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/

**4b. Post-exercise protein/MPS — SUPPORTED:**

- **ISSN-NT (verbatim, point 9):** "Post-exercise ingestion (immediately to 2-h post) of high-quality protein sources stimulates robust increases in MPS."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

- **ISSN-NT (verbatim, point 8):** "Pre- and/or post-exercise nutritional interventions (carbohydrate + protein or protein alone) may operate as an effective strategy"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

**4c. The "pre-workout meal should be LOW IN FAT and FIBRE (fat/fibre slow gastric emptying)" claim — NOT SUPPORTED BY THE PRIMARY SOURCES:**

This widely-repeated convention is **NOT stated** in the authoritative published sources checked:

- **ISSN-NT:** explicitly checked — "does not discuss how dietary fat or fiber affects gastric emptying or digestion of pre-workout meals" and "does not recommend that pre-exercise meals be lower in fat/fiber."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596471/

- **A&S-2013:** explicitly checked — "No explicit mention of fat or fiber slowing gastric emptying. The paper does not discuss how dietary fat or fiber affects digestion rate in relation to pre- or post-workout meals."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577439/

- **IRAKI-2019:** explicitly checked — "does not contain statements specifying that pre-workout meals should be lower in fat and fiber, or discussing how fat/fiber slow gastric emptying for meal timing purposes."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680710/

The low-fat/low-fibre pre-workout advice appears only in **secondary/coaching blog sources** (e.g. The Bodybuilding Dietitians, GymBeam, Healthline) found in search — none is a position stand or named-coach peer-reviewed paper. The underlying physiology (fat and fibre slow gastric emptying) is real and uncontroversial in GI physiology, but the specific *bodybuilding pre-workout recommendation* is coach practice, not something the cited position stands assert.

**Verdict:**
- Pre-workout meal timing (1–2 h before; can double as post-workout): SOLIDLY SOURCED (A&S-2013 + Iraki).
- Post-workout protein for MPS: SOLIDLY SOURCED (ISSN-NT).
- "Pre-workout meal must be low fat/low fibre": COACH-PRACTICE / BLOG ONLY — do not cite a position stand for this. If the app states it, attribute it to general GI-physiology/coaching convention, not to ISSN/Helms/Aragon.

## CLAIM 5 — The "3.1% better results from carb cycling" figure (FABRICATION CHECK)

**The flagged claim:** that day-to-day carb cycling gives "3.1%" better results. This was UNTRACEABLE and treated as fabricated.

**Finding: NO published study supports a "3.1% better body composition from carb cycling" figure.** No source located in this research states any such percentage. The "3.1" almost certainly collides with an unrelated real number — **Helms 2014's protein recommendation of "2.3-3.1 g/kg of lean body mass"** (CLAIM 3a) — but that is a protein dose, not a carb-cycling outcome percentage. The fabrication assessment stands: there is no traceable "3.1% better results" figure for carb cycling.

**What the actual controlled refeed/carb-cycling evidence says (Campbell 2020 and its reanalysis):**

- **Design (CAMPBELL-2020 via PMC commentary):** 27 resistance-trained males and females, 7 weeks. Refeed group: ~35% calorie reduction for 5 days/week plus 2 consecutive days at maintenance (carbohydrate-led refeed); continuous group: matched average weekly deficit, no refeeds.
  URL (paper, blocked by 403 but cited): https://www.mdpi.com/2411-5142/5/1/19

- **Original authors' revised conclusion (CAMPBELL-REPLY, verbatim):** "A 2-day carbohydrate refeed preserves dry fat-free mass. The changes in fat-free mass and RMR were not significantly different between the two diet treatments, although it bears noting that both fat-free mass and RMR were more effectively maintained in the group of subjects who were given 2-day refeeds, despite having the same weekly average deficit as the group who dieted continuously."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739255/

- **Reanalysis (PEOS-2020, verbatim):** "only one of these outcomes (dry FFM) was significantly different between the intermittent/refeed and continuous diets." For dry fat-free mass (intention-to-treat): "0.9 kg (95% confidence interval 0.33 to 1.46 kg; p = 0.0028)" greater preservation in the refeed group — about half the completers-only estimate of ~1.7 kg. For regular fat-free mass and RMR: "we saw no significant differences between diet groups in change scores."
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739336/

- **Author acknowledgement (CAMPBELL-REPLY, verbatim):** the reanalysis "found no significant difference between the intermittent/refeed and continuous diet groups for the change in FFM or RMR, albeit we did also observe significantly greater retention of dry FFM"
  URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7739255/

**What the named-coach methodology actually claims (mechanism, not a percentage):** Trexler/Stronger By Science describe refeeds' *proposed* goal as a temporary leptin rise and metabolic-rate stimulation, and that intermittent carbohydrate refeeds may help preserve fat-free mass by allowing greater training volume/intensity via raised muscle glycogen. One cited study found a 3-day carbohydrate refeed raised leptin ~28% and total daily energy expenditure ~7%, but that required 3 days of ~40% overfeeding — i.e. the expenditure bump is dwarfed by the surplus. (Source: Stronger By Science "Metabolic Adaptation Manual", https://www.strongerbyscience.com/metabolic-adaptation/ — note: the page returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch; the figures above are from the search-surfaced summary of that article and should be re-verified against the live page before being quoted as load-bearing.)

**Verdict on the carb-cycling evidence:**
- "3.1% better results from carb cycling": FABRICATED — no source supports it; likely a corruption of Helms' 2.3–3.1 g/kg protein figure.
- Real evidence: a 2-day carbohydrate refeed showed **no significant advantage in total fat-free mass or RMR**; the only between-group win was *dry* fat-free mass (~0.9 kg ITT), of debatable practical significance. Carb cycling/refeeds are at best a marginal, contested edge — the app must not claim a specific percentage body-composition benefit.

## SUMMARY

**Solidly sourced (2+ independent authoritative sources):**
1. 3–6 meals/day, protein roughly every 3 h — Helms 2014, Iraki 2019, ISSN-NT, ISSN-DBC framing.
2. Per-meal protein ~0.4–0.5 g/kg (20–40 g / 0.25–0.40 g/kg/dose) — Helms 2014, Iraki 2019, Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013, ISSN-NT. (Best-corroborated number in the set.)
3. Meal frequency does NOT change 24-h thermogenesis or materially affect fat loss / LBM retention — Helms 2014 (metabolic-chamber data); Iraki 2019 flags the high-frequency rationale as belief-based.
4. Total daily intake outweighs nutrient timing once daily needs are met (carbs especially) — ISSN-NT, Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013.
5. Pre-workout meal 1–2 h before, can double as post-workout; post-workout protein supports MPS — Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013, Iraki 2019, ISSN-NT.
6. Off-season/contest macro frames (protein 1.6–2.2 g/kg off-season / 2.3–3.1 g/kg LBM prep; fat 0.5–1.5 g/kg or 15–30% kcal; carbs ≥3–5 g/kg as remainder) — Helms 2014, Iraki 2019, ISSN-DBC.
7. UK supermarket staple naming — anchored across Sainsbury's/Tesco/ASDA.

**Coach-practice / blog only (NOT in the position stands — flag accordingly):**
1. "More carbs on training days, fewer on rest days produces better body composition" — no position-stand support; rationale is adherence + glycogen + transient leptin, not a proven outcome edge.
2. "Pre-workout meal must be low fat / low fibre" — physiology is real (fat/fibre slow gastric emptying) but the *bodybuilding recommendation* appears only in coaching blogs; ISSN-NT, Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013 and Iraki 2019 were each checked and do NOT state it.

**What the carb-cycling evidence actually says:**
- The "3.1% better results from carb cycling" figure is FABRICATED — untraceable to any study; most likely a corruption of Helms' 2.3–3.1 g/kg protein recommendation.
- The strongest controlled test (Campbell 2020, 2-day carbohydrate refeed vs continuous deficit, matched weekly deficit) found NO significant difference in total fat-free mass or RMR between groups. The reanalysis (Peos 2020) confirmed the only significant between-group difference was *dry* fat-free mass (~0.9 kg ITT, p=0.0028), of contested practical importance. Net: carb cycling/refeeds are a marginal, contested edge at best — the app must NOT claim any specific percentage body-composition advantage.

**Method-honesty notes:**
- MDPI (Campbell original) and Stronger By Science returned HTTP 403 to direct WebFetch; Campbell data was therefore taken from the PMC commentary (Peos 2020) and the authors' PMC reply, which restate the conclusions verbatim. The SBS leptin/EE figures (~28% / ~7%) are from a search-surfaced summary of the SBS article and should be re-verified against the live page before being quoted as load-bearing.
- The ISSN-NT carbohydrate dose figures (8–12 g/kg/day glycogen maximisation; 1.2 g/kg/h aggressive refeed; 5–12 g/kg/day general) are oriented to endurance/glycogen recovery; for bodybuilding the relevant figure is Iraki's ≥3–5 g/kg/day. Do not transplant the endurance figures into bodybuilding meal targets.
