When to change your calories, and when to hold

Reading time about 6 minutes

Most people who track what they eat change their calories too often. The scale goes up after a big meal, or does not move for three days, and the number gets cut. A week later the scale drops sharply, so the number goes back up. That is chasing noise, and it tends to leave you no further forward while feeling as though you are always reacting.

The alternative is a simple habit: change your calories only when the evidence is strong enough to justify it, and hold the rest of the time. This article sets out how to make that call. It is general guidance on reading your own numbers and promises no particular result.

Start from energy balance

Over time, your body weight follows energy balance: the energy you take in from food and drink, compared with the energy your body uses. When you take in less than your body uses over a sustained period, stored energy is drawn down and body mass tends to fall. When you take in more, it tends to rise. This is a well-established principle, and it is the backbone of any sensible calorie decision. A consensus statement on energy balance explains that as your weight changes, the calories your body uses in a day change too [1].

So your calorie target is a working estimate that you test against what actually happens. The question to ask is whether the latest numbers give you a strong enough reason to move it.

The core problem: a trend or just noise

Your weight on a single morning is a poor guide to fat change, because it is dominated by things that have nothing to do with fat. Water, salt, how many carbohydrates you ate, how full your gut is and hormone changes can each move the scale from one day to the next. Daily weights from 80 adults followed a weekly pattern, higher on Sunday and Monday and lower later in the week, and the authors concluded that this kind of swing is normal [2].

The way through is to look at the trend of your readings. If you weigh yourself most mornings in similar conditions and take a weekly average, the daily ups and downs largely cancel out. What is left is a far cleaner signal of the direction you are really moving in. Comparing this week’s average with last week’s, and the week before, tells you what your body is doing in a way no single morning can.

This is why the honest unit for a decision is the week. A day does not hold enough information to act on. A week, averaged, usually does.

Do you have enough weigh-ins to judge?

Before you change anything, ask whether you have the numbers to support a change at all. A weekly average built from one or two weigh-ins is barely more reliable than a single reading. The more days you add to the average, the more the noise cancels and the more you can trust the result.

A reasonable working standard looks like this:

  • Weigh in on most days of the week, first thing, in similar conditions.
  • Build a weekly average from those readings rather than acting on any one day.
  • Compare at least two, and preferably three, weekly averages before you decide there is a trend.
  • If you are missing most of a week’s weigh-ins, the honest answer is that you cannot judge yet. Hold, and gather more.

Holding when you have little to go on is the right decision for what you know. Acting on too little information just turns chance into a change you will probably have to reverse.

What actually justifies a change

Once you have two or three clean weekly averages, the decision gets much simpler. Write down what you expect from your current phase, then compare it with what the trend shows.

  • The trend is moving at about the rate you intended. Hold. The plan is working, and changing it only adds noise.
  • The trend has stalled for two or three weeks, well beyond what one week of water changes could explain, and you are confident you have logged consistently. This is a real reason to adjust.
  • The trend is moving faster than you intended, for instance weight falling more quickly than a sensible rate. This is also a reason to adjust, in the other direction, because very fast change has costs of its own.
  • One week looks odd but the wider picture does not. Hold. A single surprising week is usually noise.

Notice that consistent logging is part of the test. If your food records for the week were incomplete or loosely estimated, a stall may say more about the records than about your body. The right first move then is to log more carefully before you cut any calories.

Why a weekly review protects you

Reviewing once a week gives you a cleaner number to judge, and it takes the emotional pull out of the daily reading. When you know you will only make a decision once a week, from an average, a heavy number on a Tuesday stops being a crisis. It is one reading that feeds into a number you will look at on Sunday. That distance is good for consistency, and good for your relationship with the scale.

A weekly review keeps changes small and rare. Big, frequent swings in what you eat make it impossible to learn anything, because you never keep a target long enough to see what it does. A steady target, reviewed weekly and changed only when the trend clearly calls for it, is what lets you see cause and effect.

A short checklist

  • Weigh in most mornings in similar conditions.
  • Work from a weekly average, never a single day.
  • Wait for two or three weekly averages before you read a trend.
  • Change only when the trend, not one week, clearly departs from the rate you intended.
  • Check your logging honestly before you blame your body.
  • When in doubt, hold. Holding when you have little to go on is a decision.

None of this guarantees a particular result, and no framework can. It stops you making changes you cannot justify, so that the changes you do make mean something.
