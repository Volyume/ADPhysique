Why a coach that shows its reasons beats a black box

Reading time about 5 minutes

When an app tells you to change your calories or add a set, it can have reached that answer in two very different ways. It can follow a set of stated rules applied to your numbers, so you can read exactly why it decided what it did. Or it can produce the answer from a large statistical model whose reasoning is not laid out, so you get the answer but no way to check how it got there. The second kind is often called a black box: an answer goes in one side and comes out the other, and what happened inside is hidden.

For a coaching decision, that difference matters more than it first seems. This article makes the case for a coach that follows fixed rules and shows its reasons. It tries to be fair to the alternative, because chat assistants built on large language models are genuinely useful for some things, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

What each approach is good at

Chat assistants built on large language models are remarkable with words. They can explain an idea a dozen ways, answer a rambling question, and write fluent text on demand. For learning, for exploring ideas, and for turning a vague question into a readable answer, they are a real advance.

They have two properties that matter here, though. First, their answers are a matter of probability. The same question can get different answers, and a fluent answer is not always a correct one. Researchers call the habit of producing confident statements with nothing behind them hallucination. It is well documented, and it comes with how these systems work rather than being a fault that has been fully fixed [1].

Second, their reasoning is not visible. You can ask a model to explain itself, but the explanation is itself generated text, not a guaranteed record of what the model actually did. The gap between a believable explanation and the real basis for a decision is exactly what the field of explainable machine learning exists to address [2].

Why a coaching decision is a special case

A coaching decision is not the same as an explanation. When you ask software whether to cut your calories this week, you are asking it to act on your body, over weeks and months, based on your own health information. Three things follow.

  • You need to be able to check it. A recommendation to change what you eat is one you should be able to question: on what trend, over how many weeks, against which target. If the reasoning is hidden, you cannot tell a sound decision from a confident mistake.
  • It should be consistent. The same information should give the same decision. If identical numbers can lead to different advice on different days, you cannot learn what works, and you cannot tell whether a change reflects your body or just the model’s variation.
  • You should be able to get it fixed. Rules you can read are rules that someone can find, correct and test. With a black box, you often cannot even say what went wrong.

None of this means a chat assistant can never help around the edges of coaching. It means the core decision, the one that acts on your body, is a poor fit for a process nobody can inspect or repeat.

What fixed rules mean in practice

A coach that follows fixed rules applies stated rules to your numbers and gives a decision that can be repeated and inspected.

Give it the same information and it returns the same answer, every time. There is no variation from one run to the next to second-guess.

The reason is written down. Every decision comes with its basis: the trend it read, the figure it compared that against, and the rule it applied. You can agree or disagree, but you can always see the working.

It can also wait. Because the rules are explicit, they can include a rule that says do not decide yet. The coach holds until there is enough to go on, instead of producing a confident answer from too little.

This is not a claim that rules are cleverer than a large model. They are not. The claim is narrower: for a decision that acts on your health, week after week, being repeatable and open to inspection is worth more than being fluent.

The fair objection, and the honest answer

The reasonable objection to fixed rules is that they are only as good as their authors. A rule-based coach cannot invent an insight its designers never wrote down, and a badly designed rule will give bad advice with total confidence. That is true, and it is the real cost of the approach.

The honest answer is that this cost is visible and fixable in a way a black box’s is not. When a rule is wrong, you can find it, read it and correct it, because it is written down. When an opaque model gives poor advice, you often cannot establish why, let alone promise the same mistake will not happen again. Both kinds of coach can be wrong. Only one of them lets you see how.

Where this leaves you

If you want to explore ideas, learn the theory or talk something through, a chat assistant is a good tool, and it would be dishonest to say otherwise. If you want a repeated decision about your training and food that you can check, count on to be consistent and get corrected when it is wrong, a coach that shows its reasons is the better fit. The two are good at different jobs.

This is how Volyume makes its weekly coaching decision. Your coach isn’t an AI chatbot. It follows fixed rules, so the same information gives the same decision, and the reason is written down for you to read.

The point is not that one method wins every contest. It is that for the specific job of deciding, again and again, what to do next with your own body, being able to see the reason is the whole point.
