How to know when to add sets, and when to hold

Reading time about 6 minutes

The number of hard sets you do for a muscle each week is one of the strongest levers you have for building muscle. Coaches call this your training volume: the number of hard sets you do for each muscle in a week. It is also one of the easiest things to get wrong. When progress slows, the instinct is to add more sets. Sometimes that is right. Often it is not, and the better move is to keep things as they are, or even to ease off.

This article gives you a plain way to decide, week by week, whether to add sets or leave them where they are.

More sets help, up to a point

The evidence that weekly sets matter is solid. Reviews of resistance training find that, across a sensible range, doing more hard sets per muscle per week tends to build more muscle than doing very few [1]. That is the case for adding sets. It is not a case for piling them on without limit. In trained lifters, a review found that going above about 12 to 20 hard sets per muscle a week made no clear difference to the front of the thigh or the biceps, although the triceps did gain more from higher amounts [2].

So the number of weekly sets is a lever with a useful range. The skill is knowing where you are within that range.

Four landmarks

A helpful way to picture the range is with four landmarks. You do not need exact numbers to use the idea, and the right figures differ from person to person and from muscle to muscle. What matters is the shape of the map.

  • Holding on. The amount of work that keeps the muscle you already have, without necessarily adding more. It is the floor you can drop to when life is busy.
  • The least that builds. Roughly the smallest amount of weekly work that still builds new muscle. Below this you are holding what you have, not progressing.
  • The productive middle. The broad zone where most good training happens, and where each added set still tends to buy added progress.
  • The ceiling. The most you can recover from. Beyond it, extra sets make your results worse, not better.

The aim is to spend most of your training between the least that builds and the ceiling. Start nearer the lower end at the beginning of a few weeks of training, and add work as you adapt. Adding sets is how you climb through that zone. The mistake is treating the ceiling as a target.

Signs that it is time to add sets

Add sets when the evidence says you are recovering well from your current work and your progress has levelled off at that amount. Look for several of these signs together, rather than any single one:

  • Progress has stalled for two or more weeks at your current amount. The reps or weight on your main lifts for that muscle have stopped creeping up, despite good effort.
  • Recovery is comfortable. Soreness from a session clears well before the next one, and you feel ready to train that muscle again rather than dreading it.
  • Sessions feel too easy. You finish your sets with plenty left, and the muscle is not being challenged in a meaningful way.
  • Your sleep, stress and food are in reasonable shape, so you can absorb more work rather than just piling up tiredness.

When those line up, adding a set or two per muscle per week is a reasonable, measured step. Add a little, then watch the next couple of weeks to see whether your performance responds.

Signs to hold, or to ease off

Just as important is knowing when not to add. Once you are near your ceiling, more is not better. Pushing past it tends to stall progress, and it raises the chance of injury and burnout. Hold where you are, or reduce, when you see:

  • Heavy soreness that has not settled by the next session for that muscle.
  • Performance falling week on week, with reps or weight slipping despite honest effort.
  • Joints and tendons complaining, or nagging aches that build up over a few weeks.
  • Worse sleep, lower motivation, or a general run-down feeling. These are signs of tiredness across your whole body, not just one muscle.

If several of these show up together, you are probably at or beyond what you can recover from. Keep your sets where they are so you can recover into them, or take a planned step back.

The lighter recovery week

Training builds up tiredness as well as progress. A lighter recovery week is a short planned drop in the amount of work or the weight you lift. Planned drops in load are a common strategy in strength and physique training, although they have been little researched. A panel of expert coaches agreed that their purpose is to reduce tiredness and help recovery [3].

A practical pattern is to build your weekly sets over several weeks, from the lower end of your productive range towards the upper end. Then take a lighter week before starting the next few weeks a little higher than last time. A lighter week lets tiredness clear. Research has not shown that it adds muscle.

Why more is not always better

The most useful idea here is that your weekly sets have a productive window. Below the window you do too little to build much. Above it you out-run your recovery, and progress stalls or reverses. Good training is mostly about staying in that window and moving through it on purpose: starting lower, adding sets when the signs justify it, holding when they do not, and taking a lighter week before tiredness forces the issue.

You make this decision again and again, with evidence. Read your performance and your recovery honestly, change one thing at a time, and give it a couple of weeks before you judge.
