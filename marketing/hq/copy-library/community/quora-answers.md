## Q1. "How do I know when to lower my calories when cutting?"

*(No Volyume mention. Pure value.)*

The honest answer is that you lower calories when your weight has actually
stopped moving, not when the scale has a bad morning.

Pick a rate of loss you are aiming for. Something around 0.5 to 1 per cent of
bodyweight a week is a common range that keeps most of your strength and
muscle. Then judge it on a weekly average, not a single reading. Weigh yourself
most mornings under the same conditions, first thing, after the loo, before
food and drink, and take the average across the whole week. Day to day the
scale swings with water, salt, sleep, and where you are in your training week,
so any one number is close to meaningless.

Compare this week's average to the last week or two. If it is trending down at
roughly the rate you wanted, do nothing. Your current intake is working, and
changing it just adds noise. If the average has genuinely flattened across two
weeks or more, that is your signal to drop intake a little. A cut of around 100
to 200 calories a day is usually enough. Take one step, then wait and watch for
a couple of weeks before touching it again.

Two things worth adding. Give it real time before reacting, because one flat
week is often just water masking fat loss underneath. And do not chase faster
loss by slashing calories hard. Aggressive deficits cost you strength, mood,
and muscle, and they rarely stick. Small adjustment, patience, reassess. That
is the whole loop.

## Q2. "How many sets per muscle group per week should I do?"

*(No Volyume mention. Pure value.)*

There is no single right number, but there is a sensible range, and the useful
idea is to treat it as a range you move within rather than a fixed target.

For most people training to build muscle, somewhere around 10 to 20 hard sets
per muscle group per week is the working zone. Beginners tend to grow well at
the lower end, often nearer 10, because almost any decent stimulus is new.
More experienced lifters usually need more to keep progressing and sit higher
in that band. "Hard sets" means sets taken close to failure, roughly one to
three reps in reserve. Junk sets you could stop halfway through do not really
count.

The better way to use this is to start low and add only when you need to.
Begin around 10 to 12 sets for a muscle, run it for a few weeks, and watch
whether your performance is improving. If your reps or loads are still climbing
week to week, there is no reason to add volume. If progress has clearly
stalled and your recovery is fine, add a couple of sets and reassess. More is
not automatically better. Past the point you can recover from, extra sets just
cost you fatigue with nothing to show for it.

Spread the sets across two or sometimes three sessions in the week rather than
cramming them into one. And watch recovery signals as much as the number:
sleep, joint aches, and whether your lifts are stalling everywhere at once. The
volume that works is the most you can recover from and keep progressing on, not
the biggest number you can survive.

*(Volyume named; founder disclosure. One article link.)*

so let me split them by job.

If you mainly want fast, clean set logging with good history and a nice
interface, Hevy and Strong are both genuinely good and worth trying. Hevy has a

## Q4. "Can I trust AI fitness coaches?"

*(Volyume named; founder disclosure. No link.)*

It depends entirely on what is under the hood, and most people never ask.

There are two very different things being sold as "AI coaching". The first is
generative AI, the same kind of model behind chatbots. It produces plausible
text, but it can be confidently wrong, it can give you a different answer to
the same question on two different days, and you usually cannot see why it told
you what it did. For something like your training and nutrition, that
unpredictability is a real problem. You want to know why a change was
recommended, not just receive one.

The second kind is deterministic and rule-based. It applies a fixed set of
rules to your own logged data. The same inputs always give the same decision,
and because the rules are set, the reasoning can be shown to you and checked.
It will not surprise you, and it will not invent something to sound clever.

I am biased here because I built a training app called Volyume, so take that
into account. I went deliberately with the second kind. The weekly decision on
whether to change your training or hold is made by explainable rules, not a
generative model, and it shows the reasons every time. When it has not seen
enough of your data to be sure, it holds rather than guessing.

So my answer is: do not trust or distrust "AI coaching" as a label. Ask whether
it is deterministic and explainable or a black box that generates text. If the
app cannot tell you why it said what it said, be careful with it. If it can,
and the reasons hold up, that is a tool you can actually work with.

## Q5. "Why did my weight go up during a cut?"

*(No Volyume mention. Pure value, per brief, since a product mention would not
add here.)*

A short-term rise on the scale in a cut is normal and almost always is not fat.
Fat gain needs a calorie surplus, and you cannot gain a pound of fat overnight,
so a jump of one to three pounds in a day or two is something else.

The usual causes are all water. A hard or unfamiliar training session causes
temporary inflammation and water retention in the muscles as they repair, and
that shows on the scale for a few days. A higher-carb or saltier day holds more
water, because carbs store with water in the muscles and sodium pulls water in
too. Poor sleep and general life stress raise cortisol, which makes you hold
water as well. For women, the menstrual cycle adds a predictable few pounds of
water at certain points that has nothing to do with fat.

Digestion plays a part too. More food volume or more fibre simply means more
in the pipe at any moment, and that has weight.

The fix is not to panic or slash calories. Judge your cut on the weekly
average, not the daily number, and look at the trend over two to three weeks.
If the weekly average is still drifting down over that window, your cut is
working and the daily spikes are just noise on top of it. If the average has
genuinely flattened for a couple of weeks, then it is worth a small adjustment.
One high morning is never the signal. The trend is.
